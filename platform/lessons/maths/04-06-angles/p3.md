## Why this matters

Polygon-angle questions appear in almost every Cambridge paper. They are usually 2 to 3 marks, split between **method** (using the right formula) and **answer** (the right value). If you know the four cases on this page — interior of regular, interior of irregular, exterior of regular, exterior of irregular — you can score full marks every time.

Two things to fix in your head before the rules. At every vertex of every polygon there are two angles to talk about: the **interior** angle (inside the shape) and the **exterior** angle (between one side extended and the next side). They sit on a straight line, so they always add to $180°$. You will see this widget in different modes throughout the page; play with the slider and the show-mode dropdown to get the feel.

## Interior angle sum: where $(n-2) \times 180°$ comes from

Pick any vertex of an $n$-sided polygon and draw lines from it to every other non-adjacent vertex. The polygon splits into exactly $(n-2)$ triangles. Each triangle is $180°$, so the total is $(n-2) \times 180°$.

This works for **any** $n$-gon, regular or irregular, as long as it is convex.

<iframe src="/widgets/polygon-triangulation.html" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

$$\fcolorbox{#00abfa}{#00abfa1f}{\textcolor{#00abfa}{\text{Sum of interior angles} = (n - 2) \times 180°}}$$

| Sides $n$ | Polygon name | Interior sum |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | triangle | $180°$ |
| 4 | quadrilateral | $360°$ |
| 5 | pentagon | $540°$ |
| 6 | hexagon | $720°$ |
| 7 | heptagon | $900°$ |
| 8 | octagon | $1080°$ |
| 9 | nonagon | $1260°$ |
| 10 | decagon | $1440°$ |

## Interior angles of a REGULAR polygon

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal. So each interior angle is just the sum divided by $n$:

$$\fcolorbox{#fff067}{#fff0671f}{\textcolor{#fff067}{\text{Each interior (regular } n\text{-gon)} = \dfrac{(n - 2) \times 180°}{n}}}$$

Drag the slider to see how each interior angle changes as $n$ grows. Notice how all $n$ green angles are identical — that is what "regular" means.

This formula **only** works when the polygon is regular. If the angles are not all equal, you cannot divide by $n$.

## Interior angles of an IRREGULAR polygon

An irregular polygon has the same interior **sum** $(n-2)\times 180°$ — but each individual angle can be anything. So the divide-by-$n$ shortcut does not apply.

Drag any yellow vertex below to deform the pentagon. Watch how each green interior angle changes as you drag, but the **sum stays at $540°$**.

<iframe src="/widgets/polygon-irregular.html?mode=interior&lock=1" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

The Cambridge approach for irregular polygons is always:
1. Compute the interior sum from $(n-2) \times 180°$.
2. Subtract the angles you are given. What is left is your unknown.

## Exterior angles of a REGULAR polygon

Imagine walking around the boundary of a polygon. At each vertex you turn through one exterior angle. By the time you return to your starting point, you have made one full turn — $360°$. This is true for **any** convex polygon, regular or not.

$$\fcolorbox{#ff822c}{#ff822c1f}{\textcolor{#ff822c}{\text{Sum of exterior angles} = 360° \quad \text{(any convex polygon)}}}$$

For a regular $n$-gon all $n$ exterior angles are equal, so:

$$\fcolorbox{#ff4670}{#ff46701f}{\textcolor{#ff4670}{\text{Each exterior (regular } n\text{-gon)} = \dfrac{360°}{n}}}$$

<iframe src="/widgets/polygon-angle-explorer.html?mode=exterior&lock=1" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

This is usually the **fastest route** in Cambridge questions: if the polygon is regular, find the exterior with $360/n$ first, then the interior with $180 -$ exterior if needed.

## Exterior angles of an IRREGULAR polygon

For an irregular polygon the exterior angles are not equal, but they still **sum to $360°$**. This is the strongest check you have: whatever exterior angles you compute, multiply or add them up and you should get $360°$ exactly.

## The bridge: interior + exterior = 180° at every vertex

At any vertex of any polygon, the interior angle and the exterior angle sit on a straight line (the side extended). So they are supplementary:

$$\fcolorbox{#0fee89}{#0fee891f}{\textcolor{#0fee89}{\text{interior} + \text{exterior} = 180°}}$$

Switch the widget to "both" to see this. The green wedge plus the pink wedge at every vertex always opens out to a straight line.

<iframe src="/widgets/polygon-irregular.html?mode=both&lock=1" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

This bridge is what lets you switch between "find the interior" and "find the exterior" by subtracting from $180°$. In practice the exterior route is almost always faster.

## Worked example 1: interior angle of a regular octagon

Find the size of one interior angle of a regular octagon.

**Step 1: Apply the interior-sum formula.** Regular octagon means $n = 8$. Sum $= (n - 2) \times 180 = 6 \times 180 = 1080°$. **[M1 for $(n-2)\times 180$]**

**Step 2: Divide by $n$.** Each interior angle $= \dfrac{1080}{8}$. **[M1 for $\div n$]**

**Step 3: State the value.** Each interior angle $= 135°$. **[A1 for $135°$]**

**Answer:** $\boxed{135°}$.

Full marks $= 3$.

**Faster route (using exterior angles):** exterior $= \dfrac{360}{8} = 45°$, then interior $= 180 - 45 = 135°$. Both routes get full marks.

## Worked example 2: number of sides from an interior angle

A regular polygon has each interior angle equal to $156°$. Find the number of sides.

**Step 1: Find the exterior angle.** Switch to exteriors (the easier route): each exterior $= 180 - 156 = 24°$. **[M1 for finding the exterior angle]**

**Step 2: Use the exterior formula.** For a regular $n$-gon, exterior $= \dfrac{360}{n}$, so $24 = \dfrac{360}{n}$. **[M1 for using $\dfrac{360}{n}$]**

**Step 3: Solve for $n$.** $n = \dfrac{360}{24} = 15$ sides. **[A1 for $15$]**

**Answer:** $\boxed{15}$.

Full marks $= 3$.

This is a very common Cambridge style. The mark scheme allows either route (interior sum or exterior shortcut). The exterior route is usually faster and less error-prone.

## Worked example 3: irregular pentagon

Four angles of a pentagon are $100°$, $115°$, $130°$, and $90°$. Find the fifth angle.

**Step 1: Interior sum of a pentagon.** $(n-2)\times 180 = (5-2)\times 180 = 540°$. **[M1 for $(n-2)\times 180 = 540$]**

**Step 2: Subtract the four known angles.** Fifth angle $= 540 - 100 - 115 - 130 - 90 = 105°$. **[A1 for $105°$]**

**Answer:** $\boxed{105°}$.

Full marks $= 2$.

The pentagon is **not** regular here (the angles are different sizes), so we cannot use $\dfrac{(n-2) \times 180}{n}$. We can only use the sum.

## Common mistakes

- Using $(n - 2) \times 180$ for ONE angle instead of the SUM. That formula gives the total for the whole polygon: you still have to divide by $n$ if the polygon is regular.
- Using $\dfrac{(n-2) \times 180}{n}$ on an IRREGULAR polygon. That formula assumes all angles are equal. For irregular, use the sum and subtract the known angles.
- Confusing interior and exterior. Interior is inside the shape; exterior is the angle between an extended side and the next side.
- Getting $n$ wrong from the polygon name. Pentagon = 5, hexagon = 6, heptagon = 7, octagon = 8, nonagon = 9, decagon = 10.
- Not using the exterior-sum check. Whenever you compute an exterior angle of a regular polygon, multiply by $n$ — the total should be exactly $360°$. If it is not, something is wrong.
- Forgetting interior + exterior $= 180°$. This is the fastest way to switch between them.

## Quick summary

- Interior sum $= (n - 2) \times 180°$ for any $n$-gon (regular or irregular).
- Each interior of a REGULAR $n$-gon $= \dfrac{(n - 2) \times 180°}{n}$.
- Exterior sum $= 360°$ for any convex polygon (regular or irregular).
- Each exterior of a REGULAR $n$-gon $= \dfrac{360°}{n}$.
- Interior $+$ exterior $= 180°$ at every vertex of every polygon.
- The exterior route is usually the fastest. Use it as a check too.
