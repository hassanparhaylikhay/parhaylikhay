## Why this matters

Pilots, ship navigators, hikers, and Cambridge examiners all use bearings to give a direction unambiguously. "Go that way" is useless. "Go on a bearing of $075°$" is a specific compass direction that anyone in the world can follow.

Bearings combine three things: a **starting point**, a **reference direction** (always North), and a **rotation** (always clockwise). Get those three right and bearings are easy. Skip one of them and you will lose marks consistently.

This part is mostly notation and a single reverse-bearing rule. Both Paper $1$ and Paper $2$ test bearings, often inside a scale-drawing or trigonometry question.

## The bearing convention

A bearing $b$ is an angle, written with three digits and a degree sign: $b°$, where $000° \le b \le 360°$.

Three rules, no exceptions:

1. **Measured from North.** A vertical "North" arrow at the starting point is your reference.
2. **Measured clockwise.** Always go clockwise from North to the target direction.
3. **Always three digits.** $5°$ is written $005°$. $40°$ is written $040°$. $128°$ is written $128°$ (already three digits, no extra zeros). $360°$ and $000°$ are both due North; convention is to write $000°$.

If you write $40°$ when the answer is $040°$, the mark is usually lost. If you write $40$ without the degree sign, the mark is also lost. The exam wants the bearing form, exactly.

## The four cardinal directions

The four "compass points" are useful checkpoints:

- Due **North** $= 000°$
- Due **East** $= 090°$
- Due **South** $= 180°$
- Due **West** $= 270°$

The diagonals (NE, SE, SW, NW) are the bisectors:

- North-east $= 045°$
- South-east $= 135°$
- South-west $= 225°$
- North-west $= 315°$

If a question says "C is due south of A", that pins down a relationship: the bearing of C from A is $180°$, which means C is directly below A on the page (with North up the page).

## Bearing of B from A vs. bearing of A from B

This is the trap. Read carefully: "the bearing of $\square$ from $\bullet$" means **stand at $\bullet$ and look towards $\square$**. The bearing is the angle you rotate from North (at $\bullet$) clockwise to face $\square$.

The two bearings (B from A, and A from B) are not equal: in fact they differ by exactly $180°$. This is the **reverse-bearing rule**:

$$\text{bearing of A from B} = \text{bearing of B from A} \pm 180°$$

with the sign chosen to keep the answer in the range $000°$ to $360°$:

- If the original bearing is **less than $180°$**, add $180°$.
- If the original bearing is **$180°$ or more**, subtract $180°$.

**Examples:**

- B from A is $060°$ $\to$ A from B is $060° + 180° = 240°$.
- B from A is $215°$ $\to$ A from B is $215° - 180° = 035°$.
- B from A is $090°$ $\to$ A from B is $090° + 180° = 270°$ (B is due east of A; A is due west of B).
- B from A is $180°$ $\to$ A from B is $180° - 180° = 000°$ (or equivalently $360°$).

## Why the rule works

Stand at A, face North, rotate clockwise by the bearing $b$ to look at B. Now walk to B, turn around to face A: you are now looking in the **opposite** direction. From B, "facing A" is $180°$ rotated from "facing B from A". The North reference is the same at both ends (parallel North arrows), but the angle to add to the local North direction differs by half a turn, $180°$.

You can also think of it as: the two bearings, sketched on the same diagram with proper North arrows at A and at B, mark out a Z-shape (the two North lines are parallel, and the line AB is a transversal cutting them, so alternate or co-interior angle rules apply).

## Drawing a bearing

To plot a bearing of $075°$ from a point P, follow these steps:

<iframe src="/widgets/bearing-plot-explorer.html?bearing=75&point=P&length=6" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

In words:

1. Mark P.
2. Draw a vertical North arrow at P, pointing up the page.
3. Place the centre of the protractor on P.
4. Line up $0°$ on the protractor with the North arrow.
5. Mark the $75°$ position on the protractor scale, going **clockwise** from North.
6. Use a ruler to draw a line from P through the $75°$ mark.

Two common mistakes here:

- Going **anti-clockwise** by accident. Bearings are always clockwise.
- Lining the protractor with the **horizontal** instead of with North. The reference is always North, never the page edge.

## Bearings combined with scale drawings

A frequent Cambridge question:

Town A is $50$ km from town B on a bearing of $120°$. Town C is $30$ km from B on a bearing of $050°$. Make a scale drawing using $1$ cm $: 10$ km, and find the real-world distance from A to C.

<iframe src="/widgets/scale-bearing-explorer.html?b1=120&d1=50&p1=A&b2=50&d2=30&p2=C&origin=B&scale=10" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

The procedure:

1. Pick a scale and convert: A is $5$ cm from B in the drawing; C is $3$ cm from B.
2. Mark B somewhere central on the page. Draw a North arrow at B (vertical, pointing up).
3. From B, use the protractor to mark the $120°$ direction (clockwise from North), then draw a $5$ cm line in that direction. The endpoint is A.
4. Back at B, mark the $050°$ direction (clockwise from North), then draw a $3$ cm line. The endpoint is C.
5. Use a ruler to measure AC on the drawing.
6. Convert back: real AC $=$ drawn AC $\times 10$ km.
7. (Often) measure the bearing of C from A, or A from C, by drawing a North arrow at the relevant endpoint.

The trick is keeping the North arrows at **every** point of interest. They are all parallel (all "up the page"), but each starting point gets its own visible North line.

## Worked example

The bearing of village B from village A is $128°$. The bearing of village C from village A is $245°$.

(a) Find the bearing of A from B.

(b) Find the bearing of A from C.

(c) C lies due west of B. Find the bearing of B from C.

**Step a: Reverse bearing of $128°$.** $128° < 180°$, so ADD $180°$: $128° + 180° = 308°$. **[B1 for $308°$]**

**Step b: Reverse bearing of $245°$.** $245° \ge 180°$, so SUBTRACT $180°$: $245° - 180° = 065°$. **[B1 for $065°$]**

**Step c: Bearing of B from C.** "C lies due west of B" means B is due east of C — looking from C, B is to the east. Due east is bearing $090°$. **[B1 for $090°$]**

**Answer:** (a) $\boxed{308°}$  ·  (b) $\boxed{065°}$  ·  (c) $\boxed{090°}$.

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- **Two-digit bearings.** Always three digits. $25°$ should be written as $025°$.
- **Anticlockwise from North.** Bearings are clockwise. Always.
- **Reference line wrong.** North means "up the page" in standard orientation. Not the horizontal edge.
- **Forgetting to draw a fresh North arrow** at the new point when finding the reverse bearing. Each starting point needs its own visible North.
- **Reading the wrong protractor scale**: the $250°$ direction shows up at the $110°$ position on the inner protractor scale. Cross-check: should the answer be acute, obtuse or reflex?
- **Mixing up "B from A" with "A from B".** Read the question twice. The from-point is where you stand.
- **Reverse-bearing arithmetic error.** If your answer is negative or above $360°$, you've added when you should have subtracted, or vice versa. Stay in the range $000°$ to $360°$.

## Quick summary

- Bearing $=$ angle measured **clockwise from North**, written as a **three-digit** number with a degree sign.
- Range: $000°$ to $360°$.
- Cardinals: N $= 000°$, E $= 090°$, S $= 180°$, W $= 270°$.
- **Reverse-bearing rule:** if bearing of B from A is $b$, then bearing of A from B is $b + 180°$ (if $b < 180°$) or $b - 180°$ (if $b \ge 180°$).
- "P is due [direction] from Q" pins down the bearing of P from Q exactly to one of $000, 090, 180, 270$.
