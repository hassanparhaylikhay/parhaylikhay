## Why this matters

Most statistics questions begin with a list of numbers and ask you to find an AVERAGE: a single value that represents the whole list. Your exam accepts three different averages, and each tells a slightly different story.

This page covers all three, plus when each one is the right tool. Worth $1$ to $3$ marks per question on the paper, almost always as part of a longer table-based problem.

## Mean: sum divided by count

$$\text{mean} = \dfrac{\text{sum of all values}}{\text{number of values}} = \dfrac{\sum x}{n}$$

Add every value. Divide by how many values there are. Done.

Properties: the mean is sensitive to OUTLIERS. One extreme value can pull the mean far from the typical value. Salary data is a classic example, one billionaire skews the mean for an entire town.

## Median: the middle value

Sort the data from smallest to largest. Then:

- **$n$ odd**: the median is the single middle value, at position $\dfrac{n+1}{2}$.
- **$n$ even**: the median is the AVERAGE of the two middle values.

The position formula $\dfrac{n+1}{2}$ tells you WHICH position to look at. For $n = 9$, position $5$ (the $5$th value). For $n = 10$, position $5.5$, meaning average values $5$ and $6$.

Properties: the median is ROBUST to outliers. Adding one extreme value barely moves it. That is why salary, house price, and income data are typically reported as medians, not means.

## Mode: the most common

The mode is whichever value appears MOST OFTEN. If two values tie for most-frequent, the data is BIMODAL (two modes). If three or more values are tied, the data has multiple modes.

If every value appears once, there is NO mode.

For categorical data ("favourite fruit"), the mode is the only sensible "average", you cannot compute the mean of fruit names.

## Worked example: find mean, median, mode, range

Nine students scored on a maths test: $3, 5, 7, 4, 5, 6, 8, 5, 4$. Find the mean, median, mode, and range.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=stats-averages-small" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Mean.** Sum $= 3 + 5 + 7 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 8 + 5 + 4 = 47$. Mean $= 47 / 9 \approx 5.22$.

**Step 2: Median.** Sort: $3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8$. $n = 9$, so median position $= 5$. The $5$th value is $5$.

**Step 3: Mode.** $5$ appears $3$ times, more than any other value.

**Step 4: Range.** $8 - 3 = 5$. (Range comes in 9.3 p2 properly.)

**Answer:** mean $\approx \boxed{5.22}$, median $= \boxed{5}$, mode $= \boxed{5}$, range $= \boxed{5}$. **[B1 each]**

Full marks $= 4$.

The three averages came out close ($5.22, 5, 5$) because the data has no extreme outliers. Add a $40$ to the list, and the MEAN jumps to about $8.7$ while the median barely shifts. Try it in the widget.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to sort** before finding the median. The middle of an UNSORTED list is meaningless.
- **Using the position as the median.** The median is the VALUE at position $(n+1)/2$, not the position number itself.
- **Mode confusion**. Mode is the value (e.g. $5$), not the frequency (e.g. $3$).
- **Dividing by the wrong number** for the mean. Always divide by $n$ (the count of values), not by the largest value.
- **Calling a unique-values list "mode $= 1$"**. If every value appears once, the mode does not exist.

## Quick summary

- **Mean** $= \dfrac{\sum x}{n}$. Sensitive to outliers.
- **Median** = middle of the SORTED list. Robust to outliers.
- **Mode** = most common value. No mode if every value is unique.
- For categorical data, only the **mode** makes sense.
