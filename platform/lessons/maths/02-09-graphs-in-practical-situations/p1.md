## Why this matters

A Daewoo bus from Lahore to Karachi takes about $18$ hours. If you plotted distance from Lahore on the $y$-axis against time on the $x$-axis, you'd get a single straight-ish line: flat sections where it stops at terminals, steep sections on the motorway. **Distance-time graphs** turn a journey into one picture, and reading the picture tells you the speed at any stage.

Cambridge tests this on Paper 1 directly ("find the speed during this stage") and on Paper 2 inside word problems. The single rule to remember: **gradient = speed**.

## See it on a graph

## What the axes mean

- The **horizontal axis** is **time** : usually in seconds, minutes, or hours.
- The **vertical axis** is **distance** from the start : in metres or kilometres.

A point $(t, d)$ on the graph means "at time $t$, the object is $d$ units from the start."

## What the shape tells you

| Shape on a d-t graph | What it means |
|---|---|
| Straight line going up | Constant speed (forwards) |
| Steeper straight line | Faster constant speed |
| $\textcolor{#fff067}{\text{Horizontal line}}$ | $\textcolor{#fff067}{\text{Stationary (not moving)}}$ |
| Straight line going down | Moving back toward the start |
| Curved line | Speed is changing (accelerating or decelerating) |

The **steepness** of the line tells you the magnitude of the speed. The **direction** (up vs down) tells you whether it's heading away from or back toward the start.

## Gradient = speed

For a straight line on a d-t graph, the **gradient** is

$$\textcolor{#0fee89}{\text{speed}} \;=\; \textcolor{#fff067}{\dfrac{\Delta d}{\Delta t}} \;=\; \dfrac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}}$$

Pick any two points on the line, compute the rise (change in $d$) over the run (change in $t$), and that's the speed in distance-per-time units (km/h, m/s, etc.).

**Example.** A line goes from $(0, 0)$ to $(2, 120)$ on a d-t graph (time in hours, distance in km).

$$\text{speed} = \dfrac{120 - 0}{2 - 0} = 60 \text{ km/h}$$

To find the speed during any segment, **compute its gradient**: take two points on that segment and divide rise by run. Different segments can have different speeds (and the stop has speed zero).

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: a downward-sloping line means returning home, not stopping.** A horizontal line is "not moving". A downward line is "moving BACK toward the starting point" (distance from start is decreasing). These are different things; don't confuse them.

## Worked example

A car drives from town A to town B. The distance-time graph shows:

- $0$ to $1$ h: travels $80$ km
- $1$ to $1.5$ h: stops at a service station
- $1.5$ to $3$ h: travels another $120$ km

Find the speed in each driving stage.

**Stage 1.** Speed $= \dfrac{80 - 0}{1 - 0} = 80$ km/h.

**Stage 2.** Stationary, so speed $= 0$ km/h.

**Stage 3.** Speed $= \dfrac{(80 + 120) - 80}{3 - 1.5} = \dfrac{120}{1.5} = 80$ km/h.

**Answer:** $\boxed{80}$ km/h, $\boxed{0}$, $\boxed{80}$ km/h. **[B1 each for three correct gradient calculations]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the wrong axis. **Distance** is on the vertical axis; **time** is on the horizontal axis. Don't swap.
- Confusing "horizontal line" with "moving at constant speed". A horizontal line is **stationary**. A non-horizontal straight line is constant speed.
- Forgetting units. If $t$ is in hours and $d$ in km, the speed is in km/h.
- Reading gradients off the wrong segment. Pick two points that BOTH lie on the segment you're measuring.

## Quick summary

- Distance-time graph: time on x-axis, distance on y-axis.
- **Gradient = speed.** Steeper line, faster speed.
- Horizontal line = stationary. Downward line = returning toward the start.
- For a curved line, the speed is changing; the **tangent** at a point gives the speed at that instant.
