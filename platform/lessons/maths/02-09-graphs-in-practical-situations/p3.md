## Why this matters

You hand over $5\,000$ PKR at a money exchange and want to know the USD equivalent. The exchange has a **conversion graph** on the wall: PKR on one axis, USD on the other. Trace up from $5\,000$ on the PKR axis, across to the line, then over to the USD axis. Job done.

Cambridge tests conversion graphs and other practical graphs (filling tanks, throwing balls, motion in stages) on Paper 2. The maths is the same gradient/area logic from p1 and p2; the labels just change.

## Conversion graphs

A **conversion graph** turns one unit into another via a straight line. Common pairs:

- Kilograms ↔ pounds
- PKR ↔ USD (or any currency pair)
- Celsius ↔ Fahrenheit
- Kilometres ↔ miles
- Litres ↔ gallons

Most conversion graphs are **straight lines through the origin** (like km to grams: zero kg = zero grams). The single exception you're likely to meet is **Celsius to Fahrenheit**, where the line passes through $(0, 32)$ : water freezes at $0°\text{C} = 32°\text{F}$.

### Reading off a value

To convert from one unit to another:

1. Find the input value on its axis.
2. Trace **up** (or across) to the line.
3. Trace **across** (or down) to read off the equivalent on the other axis.

That's it. Cambridge sometimes asks you to do this from a printed graph; sometimes asks you to compute it from the line's equation directly.

### The conversion rate is the gradient

For a graph with input on the x-axis and output on the y-axis, the **gradient** of the conversion line gives you the conversion rate (output units per input unit).

**Example.** A km-to-mile conversion graph passes through $(0, 0)$ and $(80, 50)$.

Gradient = $\dfrac{50}{80} = 0.625$ miles per km.

So $80$ km $= 50$ miles, and any other distance scales by the same $0.625$ factor.

### Celsius-to-Fahrenheit: the only common exception

The line passes through $(0, 32)$ and $(100, 212)$ : i.e., $0°\text{C} = 32°\text{F}$ and $100°\text{C} = 212°\text{F}$.

Gradient = $\dfrac{212 - 32}{100 - 0} = 1.8$.

Equation: $F = 1.8 \, C + 32$.

This formula is exactly what the line says.

## Filling tanks and similar rate problems

Many Cambridge questions show a quantity that **builds up over time**: water in a tank, money in a savings account, fuel in a tank.

A straight-line graph for "amount vs time" means the **rate** is constant. The **gradient** of that line is the rate.

**Example.** A tank fills at a constant rate. After $30$ seconds, it has $15$ litres. After $60$ seconds, $30$ litres.

The line goes from $(30, 15)$ to $(60, 30)$. Gradient = $\dfrac{30 - 15}{60 - 30} = 0.5$ L/s.

So the tank is filling at $0.5$ litres per second.

## Curved graphs: motion that isn't constant

Not every practical graph is a straight line. A common Paper 2 case: **a ball thrown upward** has a curve (parabola) on a height-vs-time graph.

| Shape | What's happening |
|---|---|
| Curve rising and getting flatter | Slowing down (e.g., ball going up) |
| Curve rising and getting steeper | Speeding up |
| Parabola that peaks and falls | Object thrown up, reaches max, comes down |

For curves, the **gradient at a point** gives the rate AT that instant. To get it from a graph, draw the **tangent** at the point and compute its gradient.

Cambridge usually asks for the average gradient (use two points and divide rise by run) or the tangent gradient (draw a line touching the curve and compute its gradient).

## Worked example

The graph below shows the conversion between PKR and USD. The line passes through $(0, 0)$ and $(280, 1)$.

(a) State the exchange rate.

(b) Use the graph to convert Rs. $1\,400$ to USD.

(c) Use the graph to convert USD $3$ to PKR.

**Step 1 (a).** Gradient = $\dfrac{1}{280}$ USD per PKR. Or equivalently, $1$ USD = $280$ PKR.

**Step 2 (b).** Rs. $1\,400$ in USD: $\dfrac{1400}{280} = 5$ USD.

**Step 3 (c).** USD $3$ in PKR: $3 \times 280 = 840$ PKR.

**Answer:** **(a)** $1$ USD = $280$ PKR. **(b)** USD $5$. **(c)** Rs. $840$. **[B1 for the rate; A1 for (b); A1 for (c)]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the wrong direction off a conversion graph. If PKR is on x-axis and USD on y-axis, "find USD given PKR" goes UP from PKR axis; "find PKR given USD" goes ACROSS from USD axis.
- Forgetting the y-intercept on Celsius-to-Fahrenheit. The line does NOT go through the origin; it goes through $(0, 32)$.
- Computing the gradient as run/rise instead of rise/run. Gradient is always $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$.
- Reading curves as straight lines. If the graph curves, the rate isn't constant.

## Quick summary

- **Conversion graph**: a straight line that turns one unit into another.
- Most pass through the origin. Celsius-to-Fahrenheit doesn't (it passes through $(0, 32)$).
- The **gradient** of a conversion line is the conversion rate.
- For curves (ball throws, etc.), use the gradient of the **tangent** at a point for the instantaneous rate.
