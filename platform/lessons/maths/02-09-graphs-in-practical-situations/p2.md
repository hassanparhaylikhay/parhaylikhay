## Why this matters

A cricket fast bowler's run-up: starts at $0$ m/s, accelerates over a few steps, and reaches release speed at the crease. Plotting speed against time turns the run-up into a graph where you can read both the **acceleration** (steepness) and the **total distance covered** (area under the line). Cambridge tests speed-time graphs on Paper 1 and Paper 2 directly, and the rules are slightly different from distance-time.

The key shift: now both **gradient** AND **area** mean something. Don't confuse them.

## What the axes mean

- The **horizontal axis** is **time** : usually in seconds.
- The **vertical axis** is **speed** : usually in m/s.

A point $(t, v)$ on the graph means "at time $t$, the speed is $v$."

## What the shape tells you

| Shape on a v-t graph | What it means |
|---|---|
| $\textcolor{#fff067}{\text{Horizontal line}}$ | $\textcolor{#fff067}{\text{Constant speed}}$ |
| Straight line going up | Constant acceleration (speed increasing) |
| Straight line going down | Constant deceleration (speed decreasing) |
| Curved line | Acceleration is changing |

A horizontal line on a v-t graph means **constant speed**, NOT stationary. (For "stationary" on a v-t graph, you'd need $v = 0$ : the line would lie on the x-axis.)

## Two key rules

### Rule 1: gradient = acceleration

For a straight line on a v-t graph, the gradient gives the acceleration:

$$\textcolor{#0fee89}{a} \;=\; \textcolor{#fff067}{\dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}}$$

**Example.** A car accelerates from $0$ to $20$ m/s in $4$ seconds.

$$a = \dfrac{20 - 0}{4 - 0} = 5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

### Rule 2: area under the line = distance

The total distance travelled is the **area under the speed-time graph** (between the line and the time axis):

$$\textcolor{#0fee89}{\text{distance}} \;=\; \text{area under } v\text{-}t \text{ graph}$$

For straight-line graphs (linear sections only), the area is a **rectangle**, **triangle**, or **trapezium**. Use the right formula:

| Shape | Area | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | base $\times$ height | constant speed |
| Triangle | $\dfrac{1}{2} \times$ base $\times$ height | starts or ends at $0$, constant acceleration |
| Trapezium | $\dfrac{1}{2} \times$ (sum of parallel sides) $\times$ height | constant acceleration, neither start nor end at $0$ |

**Example.** Car accelerating from $0$ to $20$ m/s in $4$ seconds. The line goes from $(0, 0)$ to $(4, 20)$. The area under is a triangle with base $4$ and height $20$:

$$\text{distance} = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 20 = 40 \text{ m}$$

So the car travels $40$ m while accelerating.

## Combined: a multi-stage journey

A typical Paper 2 problem has three stages:

1. Accelerate from rest to some cruising speed.
2. Cruise at constant speed for a while.
3. Decelerate to a stop.

The graph is a **trapezium-shape**: rising, flat, then falling. The total distance is the **total area** under the whole shape : usually computed as a trapezium directly, or as the sum of the three pieces.

**Trap: don't confuse v-t with d-t.** On a SPEED-time graph, a horizontal line means CONSTANT SPEED (not stationary). On a DISTANCE-time graph, a horizontal line means STATIONARY. The axis label tells you which one you're looking at : read it carefully.

## Worked example

A motorbike starts from rest and accelerates uniformly for $5$ seconds to a speed of $25$ m/s. It then maintains $25$ m/s for $10$ seconds, before decelerating uniformly to rest in $5$ seconds.

(a) Find the acceleration in stage 1.

(b) Find the total distance travelled.

**Step 1 (a): Stage 1 acceleration.**

$$a_1 = \dfrac{25 - 0}{5} = 5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

**Step 2 (b): Total distance = area of the trapezium.**

The trapezium has parallel sides at the top ($10$ s of constant speed) and bottom (total time $20$ s), with height $25$ m/s.

$$\text{area} = \dfrac{1}{2} \times (10 + 20) \times 25 = \dfrac{1}{2} \times 30 \times 25 = 375 \text{ m}$$

**Answer:** **(a)** $\boxed{5 \text{ m/s}^2}$. **(b)** $\boxed{375 \text{ m}}$. **[M1 for stage 1 acceleration; M1 for trapezium area; A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Using gradient for distance, or area for speed. Gradient = acceleration; area = distance.
- Reading "horizontal line" as "stationary" on a v-t graph. It means constant speed.
- Splitting a trapezium incorrectly. For a v-t trapezium, the parallel sides are the two TIME segments where the line is on different speeds; the height is the speed.
- Forgetting units. Distance in m, time in s, speed in m/s, acceleration in m/s².

## Quick summary

- Speed-time graph: time on x-axis, speed on y-axis.
- **Gradient = acceleration** (m/s²).
- **Area under graph = distance** (m).
- Horizontal v-t line: constant speed (NOT stationary).
- For straight-line v-t graphs, area is rectangle, triangle, or trapezium.
