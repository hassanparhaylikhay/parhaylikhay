## Why this matters

Cubes appear less often than squares but Cambridge expects instant recall of a small list: the cubes of $1, 2, 3, 4, 5,$ and $10$. That's only six numbers. Memorise them and the cube questions become free marks.

## What is a cube?

The **cube** of a number $n$ is $n \times n \times n$, written $n^3$.

$$3^3 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$$

The name comes from geometry: a cube of side length $3$ has volume $27$.

For negatives: $(-2)^3 = (-2) \times (-2) \times (-2) = 4 \times (-2) = -8$. Unlike squaring, **cubing keeps the sign** of the original number. Cube of negative = negative.

## What is a cube root?

The **cube root** of $n$ is the number you cube to get $n$. The symbol is $\sqrt[3]{\ }$.

$$\sqrt[3]{27} = 3 \quad \text{because} \quad 3^3 = 27$$

Because cubing keeps the sign, cube roots can be negative: $\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2$.

The cube roots are the same list backwards: $\sqrt[3]{1} = 1$, $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$, $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$, $\sqrt[3]{64} = 4$, $\sqrt[3]{125} = 5$, $\sqrt[3]{1000} = 10$.

## Watch the overlap with squares

Some numbers appear in **both** the squares list and the cubes list:

- $1$ = $1^2$ = $1^3$
- $64$ = $8^2$ = $4^3$
- $1000$ is $10^3$ but not a perfect square in the recall range

Cambridge sometimes asks "find a number that is **both** a square and a cube". The small ones are $1$ and $64$.

- $5^2 = 25$, but $5^3 = 125$. Five times bigger.

## Worked example

Find the value of $5^2 \times \sqrt[3]{8}$.

*(This is a 1.3 example named in the Cambridge syllabus.)*

**Step 1: Recall each piece.** $5^2 = 25$, and $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$ (because $2^3 = 8$).

**Step 2: Multiply.**

$$5^2 \times \sqrt[3]{8} = 25 \times 2 = 50$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{50}$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 1$.

## Common mistakes

- Computing $n^3$ as $n^2$ or $3n$. $4^3 = 64$, not $16$ ($= 4^2$) and not $12$ ($= 3 \times 4$).
- Writing $\sqrt[3]{27}$ as $9$ (confusing with $\sqrt{}$). For cube roots, you want the number that cubes to $27$, not the one that squares to $27$.
- Getting the sign wrong on negative cubes. $(-2)^3 = -8$, not $+8$.

## Quick summary

- $n^3 = n \times n \times n$. Cubing keeps the sign of the original.
- $\sqrt[3]{n}$ is the number whose cube is $n$.
- Memorise $1^3, 2^3, 3^3, 4^3, 5^3, 10^3$ (and their roots): $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 1000$.
- Both square AND cube in the small range: $1$ and $64$.
