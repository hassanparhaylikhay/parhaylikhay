## Why this matters

Cambridge sometimes goes beyond squares and cubes, typically asking for $n^4$, $n^5$, or roots like $\sqrt[4]{m}$. The mechanic is the same as squares and cubes: just multiply more times. Plus, Cambridge loves combined expressions like $5^2 \times \sqrt[3]{8}$ that test whether you handle order of operations correctly.

## Higher powers

The notation $a^n$ means **multiply $a$ by itself $n$ times**.

$$2^4 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 16$$
$$3^4 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 81$$
$$2^5 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32$$

The number $a$ is the **base**, and $n$ is the **index** or **power**.

You don't need to memorise these like you did with squares and cubes. Cambridge gives you the values when needed, or expects calculation. But knowing $2^4 = 16$, $2^5 = 32$, and $3^4 = 81$ saves time.

## Higher roots

The **$n$th root** of $a$, written $\sqrt[n]{a}$, is the number that gives $a$ when raised to the $n$th power.

$$\sqrt[4]{81} = 3 \quad \text{because} \quad 3^4 = 81$$
$$\sqrt[5]{32} = 2 \quad \text{because} \quad 2^5 = 32$$

The little number outside the root sign is called the **index of the root**. When there's no index (just $\sqrt{\ }$), it means $\sqrt[2]{\ }$, the square root.

## Combined calculations: order of operations

When an expression mixes powers, roots, and other operations, follow **order of operations** (BIDMAS / BODMAS):

1. **B**rackets
2. **I**ndices (powers AND roots)
3. **D**ivision and **M**ultiplication
4. **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction

So in $5^2 \times \sqrt[3]{8}$, evaluate the indices ($5^2 = 25$ and $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$) **before** multiplying.

## Worked example 1

Find the value of $5^2 \times \sqrt[3]{8}$.

**Step 1: Evaluate the power.** $5^2 = 25$.

**Step 2: Evaluate the root.** $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$ since $2^3 = 8$.

**Step 3: Multiply.**

$$25 \times 2 = 50$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{50}$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 1$.

## Worked example 2

Find the value of $\sqrt[4]{16} + 3^3$.

**Step 1: Evaluate each piece.**

- $\sqrt[4]{16} = 2$ because $2^4 = 16$.
- $3^3 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$.

**Step 2: Add.**

$$2 + 27 = 29$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{29}$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 1$.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing $a^n$ with $a \times n$. $2^4 = 16$, not $8$ ($= 2 \times 4$).
- Doing the multiplication before the power in expressions like $5^2 \times \sqrt[3]{8}$. Indices come first.
- Reading $\sqrt[3]{}$ as a square root. The little $3$ outside means cube root.
- For higher roots: $\sqrt[4]{16} = 2$ (a fourth root), not $\sqrt{16}/4 = 1$ (no, that's not what it means at all).

## Quick summary

- $a^n$ = $a$ multiplied by itself $n$ times.
- $\sqrt[n]{a}$ = the number that gives $a$ when raised to the $n$th power.
- Order of operations: indices (powers AND roots) come before multiplication and addition.
- Compute each power/root piece first, then combine.
