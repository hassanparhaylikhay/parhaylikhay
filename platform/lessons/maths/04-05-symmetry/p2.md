## Why this matters

After line symmetry, Cambridge tests rotational symmetry. The question is usually one or two marks: "state the order of rotational symmetry of this shape". Easy to score, easy to lose if you confuse the order with the angle, or if you say a parallelogram has order $1$ when the answer is $2$.

## What rotational symmetry is

A shape has **rotational symmetry** if you can rotate it about a fixed centre point through some angle less than $360°$ and have it land back on itself, looking exactly as it did before.

The **order of rotational symmetry** is how many positions the shape lands in (including its starting position) during a full $360°$ turn. The **angle** of the smallest such rotation is

$$\text{angle} = \dfrac{360°}{\text{order}}$$

So a square has order $4$ because it lands back on itself $4$ times in a full turn (every $90°$). An equilateral triangle has order $3$ (every $120°$).

## Order $1$ means "no rotational symmetry"

Every shape, no matter how irregular, lands back on itself after a full $360°$ turn. So technically every shape has order at least $1$. We use **order $1$** to mean the only rotation that works is the trivial $360°$ one. In plain English, **order $1$ = no rotational symmetry**.

A scalene triangle, a general trapezium, a letter "F", and a random blob all have order $1$.

## Numbers to memorise

### Triangles

| Shape | Order |
|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle | $3$ |
| Isosceles triangle | $1$ |
| Scalene triangle | $1$ |

### Quadrilaterals

| Shape | Order |
|---|---|
| Square | $4$ |
| Rectangle | $2$ |
| Rhombus | $2$ |
| Parallelogram | $2$ |
| Kite | $1$ |
| Trapezium (isosceles or general) | $1$ |

Note: an isosceles triangle has $1$ line of symmetry but order **$1$** rotational symmetry. A parallelogram has $0$ lines of symmetry but order **$2$** rotational symmetry. The two types of symmetry are independent: a shape can have one without the other.

### Regular polygons and circle

| Shape | Order |
|---|---|
| Regular pentagon | $5$ |
| Regular hexagon | $6$ |
| Regular octagon | $8$ |
| Regular decagon | $10$ |
| Circle | infinite |

For a regular $n$-sided polygon, **order = $n$**. (Same as the number of lines of symmetry. That is a coincidence specific to regular polygons; in general the two numbers are different.)

A circle has infinite rotational symmetry: any rotation about its centre brings it back to itself.

## How to find the order

If you are not sure of the order, do this:

1. Mark one corner or feature with an imaginary dot.
2. Slowly rotate the shape (in your head) about its centre.
3. Count how many times the dot lands on a position that **looks identical** to the starting position before completing a full $360°$ turn.

Each time you count, the shape is in a position indistinguishable from the original. The total count (including the starting position) is the order.

For shapes drawn on a page, the **centre of rotation** is usually the centre of the shape: the intersection of the diagonals for a parallelogram, the centre of the square, the centre of the regular polygon, the centre of the circle.

## Lines of symmetry vs rotational symmetry

These are different. Some shapes have one but not the other.

| Shape | Lines of symmetry | Order of rotational symmetry |
|---|---|---|
| Equilateral triangle | $3$ | $3$ |
| Isosceles triangle | $1$ | $1$ |
| Square | $4$ | $4$ |
| Rectangle | $2$ | $2$ |
| Rhombus | $2$ | $2$ |
| Parallelogram | $\textcolor{#ff4670}{0}$ | $\textcolor{#0fee89}{2}$ |
| Kite | $1$ | $1$ |
| Regular hexagon | $6$ | $6$ |

The parallelogram is the row to remember. It has rotational symmetry but no mirror line. Many students answer $0$ for both because they confuse the two.

## Worked example

The diagram shows a shape made of an equilateral triangle and a square joined along one side. State (a) the number of lines of symmetry, and (b) the order of rotational symmetry.

**Step 1: Visualise the shape.**

It is a "house"-like figure: a square with a triangular roof on top. The vertical line through the centre of the square also passes through the apex of the triangle.

**Step 2: Lines of symmetry.**

Only the vertical line through the apex and the centre of the bottom side works. Other folds (horizontal, diagonal) do not match the triangle and square halves.

**Answer (a):** $1$ line of symmetry. **[B1]**

**Step 3: Order of rotational symmetry.**

The shape is not symmetric under any rotation less than $360°$. The triangle on top breaks the rotational symmetry of the square. So the order is $1$.

**Answer (b):** Order of rotational symmetry $= 1$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a parallelogram has order $1$. It has order $2$. After a $180°$ turn it lands on itself.
- Confusing **order** (the count) with the **angle** (in degrees). Cambridge asks for the order, which is a positive integer.
- Saying a shape has "order $0$". The minimum is $1$, because every shape comes back after a full turn.
- Mixing up lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry. They are independent. Always read the question.
- Counting the centre of rotation as a position. The centre is a point, not an orientation.

## Quick summary

- Order of rotational symmetry = number of positions in a full turn that look identical to the start.
- Order $1$ means <b style="color:#ff4670">no rotational symmetry</b>.
- Smallest rotation angle $= \dfrac{360°}{\text{order}}$.
- Regular $n$-gon has order $n$. Circle has infinite order.
- Lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry are <b style="color:#0fee89">independent</b>. A parallelogram has $0$ mirror lines but order $2$.
