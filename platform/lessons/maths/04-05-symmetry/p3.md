## Why this matters

Cambridge sometimes asks you to count the planes of symmetry of a 3D solid, or to recognise a plane or axis of symmetry on a given shape. The Cambridge syllabus is clear: you must **recognise** the symmetry properties of prisms, cylinders, pyramids, and cones. You do not need to count exhaustively for unfamiliar shapes, but you should know the standard solids cold.

## Two kinds of symmetry in 3D

In 3D, "symmetry" splits into two ideas:

- A **plane of symmetry** is a flat surface that cuts the solid into two mirror-image halves. Think of it as a 3D mirror line.
- An **axis of rotational symmetry** is a line such that rotating the solid about that line by some angle less than a full turn brings it back onto itself. The **order** of the axis is the number of positions the solid lands in during a full turn.

A solid can have both kinds, only one, or neither.

A **prism** has a uniform cross-section. Its symmetry depends on the shape of the cross-section.

- A prism whose cross-section has $m$ lines of symmetry has $m + 1$ planes of symmetry: $m$ planes that contain the axis of the prism (one for each line of symmetry of the cross-section), plus $1$ plane that cuts the prism in half perpendicular to the axis.
- The **principal axis** runs through the centres of the two end faces; its order equals the order of rotational symmetry of the cross-section.

Example. A triangular prism with an equilateral triangle cross-section: cross-section has $3$ lines of symmetry, so the prism has $3 + 1 = 4$ planes of symmetry. Its principal axis is order $3$.

A pentagonal prism with a regular pentagon cross-section has $5 + 1 = 6$ planes of symmetry, principal axis order $5$.

A **cylinder** is essentially a prism with a circular cross-section. Because the circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry:

- **Infinitely many planes of symmetry** through the central axis (one for every diameter of the circular ends).
- **One additional plane** perpendicular to the axis, halfway up.
- **One axis of rotational symmetry** along the central axis, with **infinite order** (any rotation works).

When asked about a cylinder, the safe Cambridge answer for planes is "infinite" or "infinitely many".

A **pyramid** has a polygonal base and a single apex. The axis of the pyramid is the line from the apex perpendicular to the base.

For a **right regular pyramid** (apex above the centre of a regular base):

- Each line of symmetry of the base, extended into 3D and combined with the apex, gives a plane of symmetry.
- The principal axis (through the apex perpendicular to the base) is a rotational axis of order equal to the rotational order of the base.

| Pyramid type | Planes of symmetry | Principal axis order |
|---|---|---|
| Square-based | $4$ | $4$ |
| Triangular (equilateral base, apex over centre) | $3$ | $3$ |
| Pentagonal (regular base) | $5$ | $5$ |
| Hexagonal (regular base) | $6$ | $6$ |

If the pyramid is **not** symmetric (apex shifted off centre), it has $0$ planes of symmetry.

A **cone** with a circular base and apex directly above the centre is the rotational version of a triangle. Like a cylinder:

- **Infinitely many planes of symmetry**, each containing the axis from apex to centre of base.

There is no perpendicular plane like in the cylinder, because the apex breaks that symmetry.

## Spheres and hemispheres

A **sphere** is the most symmetric solid. Every plane through its centre is a plane of symmetry, so it has infinitely many planes. Every line through the centre is an axis of rotational symmetry of infinite order.

A **hemisphere** has the symmetry of a cone: infinitely many planes through the central axis (top of curved surface to centre of flat face), and one axis of infinite-order rotational symmetry.

## How to spot a plane of symmetry on a diagram

When Cambridge gives you a 3D solid and asks you to draw or describe a plane of symmetry, look for these clues:

- **Through the centre of opposite faces**: works for cuboids and rectangular blocks.
- **Through pairs of opposite edges**: works for cubes (the diagonal-cut planes).
- **Containing the main axis**: works for prisms, cylinders, pyramids, and cones.
- **Perpendicular to the main axis** at the midpoint: works for prisms and cylinders, **not** for pyramids and cones (the apex breaks it).

If you slice the solid along your candidate plane and the two halves are mirror images, the plane is a plane of symmetry.

## Worked example

A cuboid has dimensions $3$ cm by $5.2$ cm by $4.7$ cm.

**(a)** State the number of planes of symmetry.

**(b)** State the order of rotational symmetry of the axis through the centres of the two largest faces.

**Step 1: Recognise the shape.**

A cuboid with three different side lengths. It is the most general cuboid; no two pairs of faces are squares.

**Step 2: Count planes of symmetry.**

A general cuboid has exactly $3$ planes of symmetry, one through the midpoints of each pair of opposite faces.

**Answer (a):** $3$ planes of symmetry. **[B1]**

**Step 3: Order of the chosen axis.**

The axis through the centres of the two largest opposite faces is a rotational axis. A $180°$ rotation about it brings the cuboid back onto itself; a $90°$ rotation does not (because the cross-section perpendicular to this axis is a rectangle, not a square).

**Answer (b):** Order $2$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing planes of symmetry (2D slicing surfaces) with axes of rotation (1D lines). Read the question.
- Saying a cuboid has $6$ or $9$ planes when all three sides are different. The answer is $3$.
- Forgetting the perpendicular plane on a cylinder. The cylinder has the diameter planes plus the equatorial plane.
- Including a perpendicular plane on a cone or pyramid. There is no such plane, because of the apex.
- Saying a sphere has finitely many planes. It has infinitely many.

## Quick summary

- A **plane of symmetry** slices a solid into two mirror halves.
- An **axis of rotational symmetry** is a line; the **order** is how many positions in a full turn match the start.
- General cuboid: $3$ planes. Cube: $9$ planes.
- Cylinder, sphere, cone: infinite planes through the central axis.
- A right pyramid with $n$-sided regular base: $n$ planes, principal axis order $n$.
- Prism with cross-section having $m$ lines of symmetry: $m + 1$ planes.
