## Why this matters

Symmetry questions show up almost every paper. They are short, often $1$ or $2$ marks each, and they test whether you can match a shape to its number of mirror lines without overthinking. Get them right and they are easy marks. Get them wrong (a very common slip is saying a parallelogram has $2$ lines of symmetry along its diagonals) and you lose marks that everyone else picks up.

## What a line of symmetry is

A **line of symmetry** of a 2D shape is a line such that, if you fold the shape along that line, the two halves match exactly. Every point on one side has a matching point at the same distance on the other side.

Another way to say it: a line of symmetry is a **mirror line**. The half on one side is the mirror image of the half on the other.

A shape can have $0$ lines of symmetry, $1$, several, or even infinitely many. There is no rule that says all shapes must be symmetric.

## How to test for a line of symmetry

The fastest mental check: imagine folding along the candidate line.

- If the two halves coincide perfectly (every vertex lands on a vertex, every side lands on a side), it is a line of symmetry.
- If even one corner sticks out or any side does not match, it is not.

For shapes with rotational asymmetry (like a parallelogram), the diagonals look like they should be lines of symmetry, but folding shows they are not. Always do the fold test, do not trust the look.

The rule for any **regular $n$-sided polygon** is: $n$ lines of symmetry. Half pass through opposite vertices, the other half through midpoints of opposite sides. (When $n$ is odd, each line goes from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.)

## Drawing lines of symmetry

When Cambridge asks you to draw the lines of symmetry on a given shape:

- **Use a ruler.** Freehand lines lose marks for accuracy.
- Draw each line so it goes **through the whole shape**, not just inside it. Lines should extend slightly past the boundary if the shape is small.
- Draw **only the lines of symmetry**. Extra lines (for example, drawing the diagonals of a parallelogram) will be marked wrong.

A good habit: count the lines you have drawn and check they match the number you expect for that shape. If you drew $4$ lines on a rectangle, you have made the classic mistake of including the diagonals. Erase the diagonals.

## Why a parallelogram has $0$ lines

This catches students every year. A parallelogram **looks** symmetric, and people assume the diagonals are mirror lines. They are not.

Try the fold test on a typical parallelogram (one with slanted sides). Fold along a diagonal. The top vertex does not land on the bottom vertex, because the angles at those corners are different. So the fold does not produce a perfect overlap. The diagonal is not a line of symmetry.

A parallelogram has $0$ lines of symmetry. It has rotational symmetry instead, which is a separate thing (next lesson).

The exceptions:

- A **rectangle** is a special parallelogram, and it has $2$ lines (through the midpoints of opposite sides).
- A **rhombus** is a special parallelogram, and it has $2$ lines (along the diagonals).
- A **square** is both, and it has $4$ lines.

So if Cambridge says "parallelogram" and gives you a slanted one, the answer is $0$, not $2$.

## Worked example

A regular pentagon and an isosceles trapezium are drawn on a piece of grid paper. State the total number of lines of symmetry of the two shapes combined.

**Step 1: Lines of symmetry of the regular pentagon.** A regular $n$-gon has $n$ lines of symmetry, so a pentagon has $5$. **[B1 for $5$]**

**Step 2: Lines of symmetry of the isosceles trapezium.** Only the line through the midpoints of the two parallel sides, so $1$. **[B1 for $1$]**

**Step 3: Add the two counts.** $5 + 1 = 6$ lines of symmetry. **[B1 for $6$]**

**Answer:** $\boxed{6}$.

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Saying a parallelogram has $2$ lines of symmetry along its diagonals. It has $0$.
- Saying a rectangle has $4$ lines, including its diagonals. It has $2$.
- Forgetting that a kite has $1$ line, not $2$. (Only the diagonal joining the vertex angles between the equal sides is a line of symmetry.)
- Drawing freehand. Use a ruler whenever you draw symmetry lines.
- Not extending lines through the shape. Tiny lines lose accuracy marks.

## Quick summary

- A line of symmetry is a <b style="color:#fff067">mirror line</b>: fold along it and the halves match.
- Common shapes to memorise: equilateral $3$, isosceles $1$, square $4$, rectangle $2$, rhombus $2$, parallelogram $\textcolor{#ff4670}{0}$, kite $1$, regular $n$-gon $n$, circle infinite.
- Use a ruler to draw lines and extend them through the whole shape.
- A shape may have $0$ lines of symmetry. Symmetry is not guaranteed.
