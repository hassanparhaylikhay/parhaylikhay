## Why this matters

A scatter diagram shows TWO numeric values per item (like height and weight, or hours studied and test score). When you plot them as points on a grid, the SHAPE of the cloud tells you whether the two variables are related.

The exam asks:

- **Describe the correlation** (TYPE: positive / negative / none) and **STRENGTH** (strong / weak), worth $1$ to $2$ marks.
- **Interpret in context** ("hours studied vs test score", describe what the correlation tells us about studying), worth $1$ mark.
- **Draw a line of best fit** and use it, covered in p2.

## Correlation STRENGTH

Strength describes how TIGHTLY the points cluster along a trend line:

- **Strong**: points lie close to a straight line. Clear pattern.
- **Weak**: points have a trend, but with lots of scatter. The pattern is hinted but not crisp.

You can also describe correlation as **none** when there''s no trend at all.

The classification is by eye at O-Level. There is no formula to memorise. The mark scheme accepts any of "strong positive", "weak positive", "strong negative", "weak negative", or "no correlation".

## Interpret in context

The exam usually asks you to interpret the correlation in PLAIN WORDS, using the variable names. Standard phrasings:

- Positive: "As $X$ INCREASES, $Y$ tends to INCREASE."
- Negative: "As $X$ INCREASES, $Y$ tends to DECREASE."
- None: "There is no apparent relationship between $X$ and $Y$."

The word "tends to" is key, correlation describes the typical pattern, not a guarantee.

## Correlation is not causation

A subtle but important point: a strong correlation does NOT prove that one variable CAUSES the other. The classic example: ice-cream sales and drownings are positively correlated, but ice-cream sales don''t cause drownings, both are linked to a third factor, summer heat.

At O-Level you mostly describe correlations; you are not asked to prove causation. But the exam may ask you to recognise that "X causes Y" is too strong a claim from correlation alone.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the strength** when only saying "positive".
- **Using "always"** instead of "tends to".
- **Claiming causation** from correlation.
- **Mis-reading the axis direction**. Positive correlation slopes UP-RIGHT, not just "to the right".

## Quick summary

- **Type**: positive (up-right), negative (down-right), none (no trend).
- **Strength**: strong (tight) vs weak (loose).
- **In context**: "as $X$ increases, $Y$ tends to ___".
- Correlation is a pattern, not a cause.
