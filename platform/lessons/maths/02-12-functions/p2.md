## Why this matters

If $f$ is a function that takes $x$ and produces $y$, the **inverse function** $f^{-1}$ is the function that goes the other way: it takes $y$ and gives back $x$. It "undoes" $f$.

In real life: if $f$ is "add 7 then multiply by 3", the inverse is "divide by 3 then subtract 7". Reverse the operations and reverse the order. Cambridge tests this directly with formulas: given $f$, find $f^{-1}$, and use the inverse correctly in problems.

## The notation

If $f(x)$ is a function, its inverse is written

$$f^{-1}(x)$$

(read "f inverse of x"). The $-1$ is NOT an exponent. It doesn't mean $\dfrac{1}{f}(x)$. It's purely a label saying "this is the inverse function".

The defining property:

$$f(f^{-1}(x)) = x \quad \text{and} \quad f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$$

In words: applying $f$ then $f^{-1}$ (or the other way around) gets you back to $x$.

## The mechanical recipe

To find $f^{-1}$ from $f(x)$, follow these four steps. Throughout the recipe, the **input** $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}$ stays blue and the **output** $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$ stays green. Watch them trade places in step 2:

**Step 1.** Write $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = f(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x})$.

**Step 2.** **Swap** $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}$ and $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$ throughout. Every blue becomes green; every green becomes blue.

**Step 3.** Solve for $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$.

**Step 4.** Replace $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$ with $f^{-1}(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x})$.

That's the entire process. It works for every linear and rational function Cambridge will throw at you.

### Example 1: linear

$f(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}) = 4\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 7$.

- **Step 1:** $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = 4\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 7$.
- **Step 2, swap:** $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} = 4\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} - 7$. *(Every $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$ on the left became $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}$; every $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}$ on the right became $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$.)*
- **Step 3, solve for $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$:** $4\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = \textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 7$, so $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = \dfrac{\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 7}{4}$.
- **Step 4, relabel:** $f^{-1}(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}) = \dfrac{\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 7}{4}$.

**Check.** $f(f^{-1}(x)) = 4 \times \dfrac{x+7}{4} - 7 = (x + 7) - 7 = x$. ✓

### Example 2: rational

$f(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}) = \dfrac{3\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 2}{5}$.

- **Step 1:** $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = \dfrac{3\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 2}{5}$.
- **Step 2, swap:** $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} = \dfrac{3\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} + 2}{5}$.
- **Step 3, solve for $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$:** multiply both sides by 5: $5\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} = 3\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} + 2$. Then $3\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = 5\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 2$, so $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = \dfrac{5\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 2}{3}$.
- **Step 4, relabel:** $f^{-1}(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}) = \dfrac{5\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 2}{3}$.

## Worked example

The function $f$ is defined by $f(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}) = \dfrac{2\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 3}{4}$.

(a) Find $f^{-1}(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x})$.

(b) Find $f^{-1}(5)$.

**Step 1 (a): apply the recipe.** Blue is the input variable, green is the output. Watch them trade in the swap.

- Write: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = \dfrac{2\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} - 3}{4}$.
- Swap $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}$ and $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y}$: $\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} = \dfrac{2\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} - 3}{4}$.
- Multiply both sides by 4: $4\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} = 2\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} - 3$.
- Add 3: $2\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = 4\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 3$.
- Divide: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{y} = \dfrac{4\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 3}{2}$.

So $f^{-1}(\textcolor{#00abfa}{x}) = \dfrac{4\textcolor{#00abfa}{x} + 3}{2}$.

**Step 2 (b).** $f^{-1}(5) = \dfrac{4(5) + 3}{2} = \dfrac{23}{2} = 11.5$.

**Answer (a):** $f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{4x + 3}{2}$. **[M1 swap; M1 solve; A1]**

**Answer (b):** $f^{-1}(5) = 11.5$. **[A1]**

Full marks $= 4$.

## The inverse on a graph

The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the reflection of $y = f(x)$ in the line $y = x$.

This makes sense: swapping $x$ and $y$ in step 2 of the recipe is exactly the geometric operation of reflecting in $y = x$. Every point $(a, b)$ on the curve $y = f(x)$ becomes the point $(b, a)$ on the curve $y = f^{-1}(x)$.

If a question asks you to sketch $f$ and $f^{-1}$ on the same axes, draw $f$ first, then sketch the reflection in $y = x$.

## Common mistakes

- Treating $f^{-1}(x)$ as $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. They're different. $f^{-1}$ is the INVERSE function, not the reciprocal.
- Forgetting to swap $x$ and $y$ in step 2. If you skip the swap, you'll just re-derive $f$.
- Solving $x = 4y - 7$ but writing the result as $f^{-1}(y)$ instead of $f^{-1}(x)$. The variable name doesn't matter mathematically, but Cambridge expects $f^{-1}$ written as a function of $x$.
- Reflecting in the x-axis or y-axis instead of $y = x$. Inverse is always $y = x$ reflection.

## Quick summary

- $f^{-1}$ undoes $f$. $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$.
- Recipe: write $y = f(x)$ → swap → solve for $y$ → relabel as $f^{-1}(x)$.
- Graph: reflect $f$ in $y = x$.
