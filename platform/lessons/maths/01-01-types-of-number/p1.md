## Why this matters

Two small skills that show up as 1-mark questions every year: writing a number from its words (or vice versa), and finding a reciprocal. They are easy marks if you know the conventions Cambridge uses, and common slip-ups if you don't.

## Reading and writing large numbers

Cambridge groups digits of large numbers in **threes**, separated by a single space, never a comma:

Count groups from the right:

| Group (from right) | Name | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Ones | $10^0$ |
| 2nd | Thousands | $10^3$ |
| 3rd | Millions | $10^6$ |
| 4th | Billions | $10^9$ |
| 5th | Trillions | $10^{12}$ |

So $6\,000\,000\,000$ has digits ending in **three groups of three zeros** after the 6. That's 9 zeros. Nine is the billions exponent, so it's **six billion**.

### Writing a number from its words

Walk the words left to right. Each "thousand", "million", "billion" tells you which group the digits belong to.

**Ten thousand and seven.**

- *Ten thousand* fills the **thousands** group: `10` in the thousands gives `10 ___`.
- *Seven* fills the **ones** group: `7`. It's a single digit, so pad the other two places with zeros, giving `007`.
- Combine: **10 007**.

The word *and* in Cambridge's convention is just a connector between groups. It does **not** mean "add extra zeros" or "move the decimal point". A common mistake is writing 10 007 as 10 070 or 100 007 because the student tried to read "and" as a position marker.

## Decimals in words

For decimals, read the **whole-number part** using the rules above, then say **"point"**, then read the digits **one by one**.

- $3.25$ is *three point two five* *(not "three point twenty-five")*
- $0.057$ is *zero point zero five seven*
- $12.4$ is *twelve point four*

Why digit-by-digit? Because "twenty-five" in the second group would mean $3\tfrac{25}{100}$, which is a fraction reading, not a digit-by-digit reading. Cambridge expects the digit-by-digit form.

## Reciprocals

The **reciprocal** of a number $n$ is the number you multiply $n$ by to get $1$.

$$\text{reciprocal of } n = \frac{1}{n}, \qquad \text{because } n \times \frac{1}{n} = 1$$

Three cases you must know cold:

- **Whole number $n$:** reciprocal is $\tfrac{1}{n}$. So reciprocal of $5$ is $\tfrac{1}{5}$.
- **Fraction $\tfrac{p}{q}$:** reciprocal is $\tfrac{q}{p}$, i.e. **flip it**. So reciprocal of $\tfrac{2}{5}$ is $\tfrac{5}{2}$. Check: $\tfrac{2}{5} \times \tfrac{5}{2} = \tfrac{10}{10} = 1$. ✓
- **Negative number:** the sign is **preserved**. Reciprocal of $-3$ is $-\tfrac{1}{3}$, not $\tfrac{1}{3}$ and not $3$.

Special cases:

- Reciprocal of $1$ is $1$. Reciprocal of $-1$ is $-1$. (The only numbers that are their own reciprocal.)
- **$0$ has NO reciprocal.** $\tfrac{1}{0}$ is undefined, since division by zero is not allowed.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: reciprocal vs negative.** The reciprocal of a number is **not** its negative. The reciprocal of $3$ is $\tfrac{1}{3}$, not $-3$. *Negative* means opposite sign; *reciprocal* means "one over". Different operations entirely.

</div>

## Worked example 1

Write the reciprocal of $2\tfrac{1}{3}$ as a fraction in its simplest form.

**Working.** First, rewrite the mixed number as an improper fraction:

$$2\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2 \times 3 + 1}{3} = \tfrac{7}{3}$$

Flip to get the reciprocal:

$$\boxed{\text{reciprocal of } 2\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{3}{7}}$$

**[M1 for converting to improper fraction; A1 for the flipped answer]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example 2

Write $1\,200\,003$ in words.

**Working.** Group from the right in threes: $1\mid 200\mid 003$.

- First group (from right, ones): $003$ is *three*.
- Second group (thousands): $200$ is *two hundred thousand*.
- Third group (millions): $1$ is *one million*.

Reading left to right: **one million two hundred thousand and three**. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 1$.

## Common mistakes and exam traps

- Writing 10 007 as 10 070 or 100 007, because *"ten thousand and seven"* feels like it should have more zeros.
- Writing $3.25$ as *"three point twenty-five"*. The decimal part is always read digit by digit.
- Reading the reciprocal of $-3$ as $\tfrac{1}{3}$ instead of $-\tfrac{1}{3}$. The sign never moves.
- Trying to find the reciprocal of $0$. There isn't one; write "undefined" or "does not exist".
- Forgetting that $\tfrac{1}{n}$ is also *a* number. So the reciprocal of $\tfrac{1}{4}$ is $4$, not $\tfrac{4}{1}$ left as a fraction. Both are technically correct, but an integer answer is expected.

## Quick summary

- Group digits in threes from the right; ones, thousands, millions, billions.
- **And** in number-words is just a connector, not a position marker.
- Decimals: whole part in words, then "point", then digits one by one.
- Reciprocal of $n$ is $\tfrac{1}{n}$; for $\tfrac{p}{q}$, flip to $\tfrac{q}{p}$; sign preserved; $0$ has none.
