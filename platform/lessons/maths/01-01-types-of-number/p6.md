## What this part is about

**HCF** and **LCM** are the two questions that build directly on the prime factorisations you learned in Part 5. Get the factorisations right, and HCF/LCM become a 30-second exercise. The exam question almost always dresses them up as a word problem, "flashing lights", "boxes packed every N minutes", "the smallest number where …", but the maths underneath is exactly the same.

> **Prerequisite:** this part assumes you can build a prime-factor tree. If you find the factor trees in this part hard to follow, work through Part 5 first.

## The definitions

**<span style="color:#0fee89">HCF</span>** = **Highest Common Factor**. The **biggest** number that divides both (or all) of the given numbers exactly. *("Highest" and "Greatest" mean the same here. HCF and GCD are the same thing.)*

**<span style="color:#fff067">LCM</span>** = **Lowest Common Multiple**. The **smallest** number that is a multiple of both (or all) of the given numbers. *("Lowest" and "Least" mean the same. LCM and LCD are the same thing.)*

In plain language:

- HCF is the biggest number that fits into both, what they both share.
- LCM is the smallest number you would have to count up to before **both** numbers divide into it evenly.

## The prime-factorisation method

Once you have each number written as a product of primes, HCF and LCM follow a simple rule:

- **<span style="color:#0fee89">HCF:</span>** for each prime that appears in **both** numbers, take the **lowest** power. Ignore primes that only appear in one.
- **<span style="color:#fff067">LCM:</span>** for each prime that appears in **either** number, take the **highest** power.

That is all there is to it. Write them side by side as a table and you can read the answers straight off.

## Memory trick: HC**F**ew vs LC**M**ore

HC**F** → take **F**ewer (the **lowest** powers; primes only if in **both**).

LC**M** → take **M**ore (the **highest** powers; primes if in **either**).

If you're ever stuck mid-question, write the table out like above. The visual layout stops you picking the wrong powers.

## Word-problem version

Most exam HCF/LCM questions are phrased as real-world scenarios. Translating them is the skill:

| Phrasing | Maths underneath |
|---|---|
| "When do two events **next happen at the same time**?" | **LCM** of their periods |
| "What is the **largest square tile** that fits both floor dimensions?" | **HCF** of the dimensions |
| "The **smallest number of boxes** needed so each type is packed whole" | **LCM** of the item counts |
| "The **largest group** that divides two piles evenly" | **HCF** of the pile sizes |

The keyword **"next together"** or **"smallest"** → LCM. The keyword **"largest"** or **"biggest piece"** → HCF.

## Worked example 3: word problem

Two lights flash: a green light every $12$ minutes, a red light every $45$ minutes. They flash together at $11{:}00$. At what time do they next flash together?

*(s25_13 Q11.)*

**Step 1: Spot the keyword.** "Next flash together" tells you it's an LCM problem.

**Step 2: Prime-factorise both numbers.**

$$12 = 2^2 \times 3, \qquad 45 = 3^2 \times 5$$

**[M1 for a valid method (factor tree or listing multiples both accepted)]**

**Step 3: Take the highest power** of each prime to get the LCM.

$$\text{LCM} = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 4 \times 9 \times 5 = 180 \text{ minutes}$$

**Step 4: Convert back to clock time.** $180$ minutes $= 3$ hours, so the next time they flash together is $11{:}00 + 3\text{h}$.

**Answer:** $\boxed{14{:}00}$. **[B2 for LCM $= 180$ seen, leading to $14{:}00$]**

Full marks $= 3$.

> **Examiner tip:** Cambridge rewards the **method**, showing the factor trees or listing multiples. Writing down "$180$ minutes = $3$ hours" with no method, even if the answer is right, can lose the method mark.
