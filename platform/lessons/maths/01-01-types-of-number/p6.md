## What this part is about

In Part 2 we said every recurring decimal is rational — meaning it can be written as a fraction of integers. This part shows **how** to do the conversion. It's always a 2- or 3-mark Cambridge question and the trick is the same every time: use algebra to **cancel the recurring tail**.

## The core idea

If you multiply a recurring decimal by $10$ (or $100$, or $1000$), you shift the digits left without changing the recurring tail. Subtracting the original from the shifted version causes the tails to cancel — leaving you with a clean integer. Divide through to get the fraction.

## Worked example 1 — one digit recurs

Write $0.\overline{3}$ as a fraction in its simplest form.

**Working.** Let $x = 0.333\ldots$.

**One digit recurs**, so multiply by $10$ to shift one full period:

$$10x = 3.333\ldots$$

Now subtract the original equation. The $.333\ldots$ tails are the same on both lines, so they **cancel**:

**[M1 for the algebraic setup]**

Divide by $9$: $x = \dfrac{3}{9} = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{3}}$. **[A1]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example 2 — two digits recur

Write $0.\overline{14}$ (both the $1$ and the $4$ recur) as a fraction.

**Working.** Let $x = 0.141414\ldots$.

**Two digits recur**, so multiply by $100$:

$$100x = 14.141414\ldots$$

Subtract:

<div class="diagram aligned-subtract">
  <div class="row"><span class="lhs">100x =</span><span class="rhs">14.141414…</span></div>
  <div class="row"><span class="lhs">x =</span><span class="rhs">&nbsp;0.141414…</span></div>
  <div class="row result"><span class="lhs">99x =</span><span class="rhs">14</span></div>
</div>

**[M1]**

$$x = \boxed{\dfrac{14}{99}}$$

**[A1]**

Full marks $= 2$.

**Pattern to notice:** if $n$ digits recur, you multiply by $10^n$, and the resulting subtraction gives a denominator of $\underbrace{99\ldots9}_{n\ \text{nines}}$. So $0.\overline{7} = \tfrac{7}{9}$, $0.\overline{23} = \tfrac{23}{99}$, $0.\overline{142} = \tfrac{142}{999}$ — and so on.

## Worked example 3 — the trickier case: mixed recurring

Write $0.1\overline{4}$ (the $1$ does **not** recur, only the $4$ does) as a fraction in its simplest form.

*(This is w25_11 Q18 — the mark scheme gives the setup explicitly.)*

**Working.** Let $x = 0.1444\ldots$.

There's a **non-recurring $1$** before the recurring $4$. We need two multiplications to align the recurring tails so they can cancel.

**Step A:** multiply by $10$ to move past the non-recurring digit.

$$10x = 1.444\ldots$$

**Step B:** multiply by $100$ to shift one full recurring period.

$$100x = 14.444\ldots$$

Now $10x$ and $100x$ **have the same recurring tail** ($.444\ldots$). Subtract them:

  <div class="diagram-caption">MIXED RECURRING: ALIGN THE TAILS FIRST, THEN SUBTRACT</div>
  <div class="row"><span class="lhs">100x =</span><span class="rhs">14.444…</span></div>
  <div class="row"><span class="lhs">10x =</span><span class="rhs">&nbsp;1.444…</span></div>
  <div class="row result"><span class="lhs">90x =</span><span class="rhs">13</span></div>
</div>

**[M1 for the algebraic setup — this is the key insight Cambridge rewards]**

$$x = \boxed{\dfrac{13}{90}}$$

**[A1]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Why the denominator is $90$ here, not $99$

Look carefully at the denominator in each case:

| Decimal | Subtraction | Denominator |
|---|---|---|
| $0.\overline{3}$ | $10x - x$ | $9$ |
| $0.\overline{14}$ | $100x - x$ | $99$ |
| $0.1\overline{4}$ | $100x - 10x$ | $90$ |

The denominator is always (the bigger multiplier) minus (the smaller multiplier). When *both* sides are the original $x$, you get $9, 99, 999, \ldots$. When one side is $10x$, you subtract $10$ off the multiplier — giving $90, 990, \ldots$.

> **Examiner tip:** Read the dot notation very carefully. $0.\overline{14}$ (both digits recur, answer $\tfrac{14}{99}$) and $0.1\overline{4}$ (only the $4$ recurs, answer $\tfrac{13}{90}$) are **different numbers** with **different fractions**. Miss that difference and the whole question is wrong.

## What Cambridge looks for

The mark scheme for these questions says things like *"M1 for $14.444\ldots - 1.444\ldots$ oe, or for $90x = 13$ oe"*. The $\boxed{\text{A1}}$ is the final simplified fraction.

**The algebra setup must be visible.** A correct fraction with no working can lose the M1 mark — Cambridge wants to see that you understood the method, not that you remembered the answer.
