## How Cambridge marks this topic

The Number topic rewards **visible method** above almost everything else. Across every past paper from 2023 to 2025, the marking pattern is tight and predictable if you know what to look for.

**For HCF/LCM questions**, the prime factorisations must be shown. Cambridge awards B1 or M1 for the factorisation being visible, **even if the final HCF or LCM is wrong**. A student who writes "$360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5$, $N = 2^3 \times 3^x \times 5^y$" and then picks the wrong powers still earns the M1 for factorising. Skipping straight to the answer loses that mark. This pattern appears on w25_22 Q14, s23_21 Q5, w23_12 Q9, every year.

**For recurring decimals**, the mark scheme says things like *"M1 for $14.444\ldots - 1.444\ldots$ oe, or for $90x = 13$ oe"*. The A1 is the final fraction. A correct fraction *without* the algebraic setup can lose the M1. Cambridge wants to see you actually ran the subtraction.

**For type-identification questions** ("which of these is irrational?"), each part is usually **one B1 mark, cao** *(correct answer only)*. No method marks, because there is no method. Either the student picks the right number or they don't. Care and clarity beat working.

**For estimation** ("round each to 1 s.f. and estimate"), Cambridge awards **B1 for at least two values correctly rounded**, even if the final calculation is wrong. So a student who rounds two of three numbers correctly but slips on arithmetic still gets half marks. Seen on w25_11 Q9 and s23_12 Q11.

**For standard form**, the answer must be in **strict** standard form, $a \times 10^n$ with $1 \le a < 10$. Writing $12.9 \times 10^{2n-1}$ when the answer should be $1.29 \times 10^{2n}$ loses the B2 mark. This exact trap is on s23_22 Q5(b).

## The 5 most common mistakes

1. **Calling any square root irrational without checking.** $\sqrt{169} = 13$, $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$, $\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{9}} = \tfrac{1}{3}$, all rational. Work out the square root first every time.

2. **Confusing factor and multiple.** A factor divides *into* the number; a multiple is made *from* it. Factors of 26 = {1, 2, 13, 26}; multiples of 26 = 26, 52, 78, … .

3. **Saying $1$ is prime.** $1$ has only one factor, not two. $1$ is neither prime nor composite.

4. **Flipping HCF and LCM powers.** HC**F** → **F**ewer (lowest); LC**M** → **M**ore (highest).

5. **Leaving standard form un-normalised.** $0.034$ is *not* standard form. It must be $3.4 \times 10^{-2}$, exactly one non-zero digit before the decimal point.

## Quick summary: the whole subunit in five bullets

- **Families nest**: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$. Irrationals sit inside $\mathbb{R}$ but outside $\mathbb{Q}$.
- **Rational = can be written as a fraction.** Terminating decimals and recurring decimals are rational; $\pi$, $e$, and square roots of non-squares are irrational.
- **Every number has one and only one prime factorisation.** Use a factor tree, write in index form, primes from smallest to largest.
- **HCF takes the lowest powers of common primes. LCM takes the highest powers of all primes.** HC**F**ew, LC**M**ore.
- **Recurring decimal → fraction** by multiplying to shift the recurring tail, then subtracting to cancel it. Show the algebra. Cambridge wants to see $10x$, $100x$, $90x = 13$.
