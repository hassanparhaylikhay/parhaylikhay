## What this part is about

Decimals come in three shapes: they **stop**, they **repeat**, or they **do neither**. That one idea, what the decimal does, is what separates rationals from irrationals. Cambridge tests it almost every year.

## The three types

**<span style="color:#0fee89">Terminating decimal</span>**, the decimal stops after a fixed number of digits. Examples: $0.5$, $0.75$, $0.25$, $0.125$. If it stops, it's rational, you can always rewrite it as a fraction: $0.5 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $0.75 = \tfrac{3}{4}$.

**<span style="color:#fff067">Recurring decimal</span>**, the decimal goes on forever, but a block of digits **repeats in a pattern**. Examples: $0.333\ldots$, $0.166666\ldots$, $0.142857142857\ldots$. These are also rational, every recurring decimal can be written as a fraction. (We'll prove this in Part 7.)

**<span style="color:#ff4670">Irrational number</span>**, the decimal goes on forever with **no repeating pattern**. Examples: $\pi = 3.14159\ldots$, $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421\ldots$. These **cannot** be written as a fraction of integers.

## The dot and bar notation

Writing every recurring digit out ($0.333333\ldots$) takes too long. Cambridge uses a shorthand: put a **dot** or a **bar** over the digit(s) that repeat.

$$0.\overline{3}\ \text{or}\ 0.\dot{3}\ \ \text{means}\ \ 0.333333\ldots$$

$$0.\overline{14}\ \text{or}\ 0.\dot{1}\dot{4}\ \ \text{means}\ \ 0.14141414\ldots$$

$$0.1\overline{4}\ \text{means}\ \ 0.1444444\ldots\ \text{(only the 4 repeats)}$$

That last one is the important one. $0.\overline{14}$ and $0.1\overline{4}$ look similar but are different numbers. Read carefully, the dots tell you exactly **which** digits recur.

## How to tell irrational from rational

Ask yourself one question: *can the decimal be written as a fraction of integers?* If yes, it's rational. If no, irrational. For numbers you meet at O-Level, two quick rules do the job:

1. If it's a fraction, a terminating decimal, or a recurring decimal → **rational**.
2. If it's $\pi$, $e$, or a square root whose answer isn't a whole number (or a fraction of whole numbers) → **irrational**.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: the automatic square-root.** Students see $\sqrt{\ }$ and write "irrational" on reflex. But:

- $\sqrt{9} = 3$, rational
- $\sqrt{169} = 13$, rational
- $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, rational
- $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} = 2.828\ldots$, **irrational** (8 is not a perfect square)
- $\sqrt{2} = 1.414\ldots$, **irrational**

The rule: **a square root is irrational only when the number inside is not a perfect square.** Always work out the root first.

## Worked example

From the list $\{52,\ \sqrt{169},\ 27,\ \sqrt{8},\ \tfrac{12}{23},\ 49\}$, identify:

(a) a square number,
(b) a cube number,
(c) a factor of 26,
(d) the irrational number.

*(w25_21 Q1.)*

**(a) Squares.** The squares are $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, \ldots$. The only one in the list is $49$, since $49 = 7^2$.

**Answer:** $\boxed{49}$. **[B1]**

**(b) Cubes.** The cubes are $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, \ldots$. The only one in the list is $27$, since $27 = 3^3$.

**Answer:** $\boxed{27}$. **[B1]**

**(c) Factor of 26.** Factors of $26$ are $\{1, 2, 13, 26\}$. Since $\sqrt{169} = 13$, that's a factor.

**Answer:** $\boxed{\sqrt{169}}$. **[B1]**

**(d) Irrational.** Work out the roots first. $\sqrt{169} = 13$ is rational. $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.828\ldots$ can't be written as a fraction.

**Answer:** $\boxed{\sqrt{8}}$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 4$.

> **Examiner tip:** There's no working to show on these type-identification questions, each part is cao *(correct answer only)*. Write the answer clearly. A wrong answer earns zero even if the question below it is right.
