## What this part is about

**Every** whole number bigger than 1 can be broken down into one and only one list of primes multiplied together. That list is called its **prime factorisation**, and it is the single most useful tool in the Number topic. Once you can factorise, HCF, LCM, finding square roots of big numbers, and recurring-decimal questions all become step-by-step exercises.

## The idea

A composite number is made by multiplying smaller numbers. Keep breaking those smaller numbers down, and you'll eventually hit numbers that can't be broken any further, the **primes**.

For example, $12$ can be written as:

$$12 = 2 \times 6 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$$

The $2$s and the $3$ can't be split any further. So the prime factorisation of $12$ is $2 \times 2 \times 3$.

Two important facts:

1. **Every number has exactly one prime factorisation.** No matter how you start breaking it down, you end up at the same list of primes (just possibly in a different order).
2. **Index notation keeps it tidy.** $2 \times 2 \times 3$ becomes $2^2 \times 3$. If $2$ appears five times, write $2^5$. Always write the primes in ascending order: $2^3 \times 5^2 \times 7$, not $7 \times 2^3 \times 5^2$.

## The factor tree method

A **factor tree** is the standard way to find a prime factorisation. Write the number at the top. Split it into any two factors. Split each factor again. Keep going until every branch ends at a prime.

## Tips for building a tree quickly

1. **Start with the smallest prime that divides.** If the number is even, divide by 2. If not, try 3, then 5, then 7.
2. **Keep dividing by 2** until you can't any more, then move to 3, then 5, then 7, ….
3. **Stop when every leaf is prime.** Use the prime list from Part 4 to check.
4. **Write the final answer in index form,** primes in ascending order.

Any starting split works, you end up at the same primes, but starting with 2 usually keeps the numbers small.
