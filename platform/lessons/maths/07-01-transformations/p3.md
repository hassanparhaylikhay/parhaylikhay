## Why this matters

Hold a pen up to a mirror at your dressing table. The reflection looks identical, but the writing on it reads backwards. A geometric reflection is the same idea: every point on the shape flips to the other side of a "mirror line", at the same distance. The shape keeps its size, but its left-right "handedness" flips.

Cambridge reflection questions are usually worth $2$ marks: $1$ for the word "reflection" and $1$ for the mirror line as an equation. If you can read a coordinate and recognise which line acts as the mirror, you can score full marks here.

## The mirror line is a perpendicular bisector

Here is the entire definition of a reflection in one picture: **every point's image is the same distance from the mirror, but on the other side**. The line from any point to its image is perpendicular to the mirror, and the mirror cuts that line exactly in half.

In short: the mirror is the **perpendicular bisector** of the line joining every point to its image. That single property is what distinguishes reflection from every other transformation.

The first four are by far the most common. The last two appear when the mirror is parallel to an axis but offset from the origin (a "shelf" or a "wall").

## A small puzzle: try it on $(2, 5)$

Reflect the point $(2, 5)$ in each of the six mirrors above. Work each one out on paper first; the answers are in the bullet list below.

- In $y = 0$: $(2, -5)$ (flip the sign of $y$).
- In $x = 0$: $(-2, 5)$ (flip the sign of $x$).
- In $y = x$: $(5, 2)$ (swap).
- In $y = -x$: $(-5, -2)$ (swap and flip both).
- In $y = 3$: $(2, 1)$ (use $2k - y = 6 - 5 = 1$).
- In $x = 4$: $(6, 5)$ (use $2h - x = 8 - 2 = 6$).

If you got any of those wrong, re-read the swap rule and try again. The patterns are short and worth memorising.

## What does NOT change

Reflection preserves every length and every angle. So after reflecting:

- Every side of the shape keeps its length.
- Every angle inside the shape keeps its size.
- Area stays the same.

The image is **congruent** to the object: identical in shape and size, but in a flipped position. The only thing that "flips" is **orientation**: a triangle labelled $A, B, C$ going clockwise on the object will have $A', B', C'$ going anticlockwise on the image. That orientation reversal is the visual signature of a reflection.

## Worked example: identify a reflection

A triangle has vertices $A(2, 1), B(5, 1), C(2, 3)$. After a single transformation, the image has vertices $A'(1, 2), B'(1, 5), C'(3, 2)$. Describe fully the transformation.

<iframe src="/widgets/transformations-step-explorer.html?preset=p3-reflection-identify" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:620px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Eliminate enlargement.** Each side of the object has length $3$ (sides $AB$ and $AC$). Each side of the image still has length $3$. So the size is unchanged, this is not an enlargement.

**Step 2: Eliminate translation.** The shift from $A$ to $A'$ is $(1 - 2, 2 - 1) = (-1, 1)$. The shift from $B$ to $B'$ is $(1 - 5, 5 - 1) = (-4, 4)$. The shifts are different. So this is not a translation.

**Step 3: Check the midpoint test.** For a reflection, the midpoint of every (object, image) pair must lie on the mirror line. Compute the three midpoints:

$$\text{midpoint of } AA' = \left(\tfrac{2+1}{2}, \tfrac{1+2}{2}\right) = (1.5, 1.5)$$

$$\text{midpoint of } BB' = \left(\tfrac{5+1}{2}, \tfrac{1+5}{2}\right) = (3, 3)$$

$$\text{midpoint of } CC' = \left(\tfrac{2+3}{2}, \tfrac{3+2}{2}\right) = (2.5, 2.5)$$

All three midpoints have $x = y$, so they lie on the line $y = x$.

**Step 4: Confirm perpendicularity.** The segment from $A(2, 1)$ to $A'(1, 2)$ has gradient $\dfrac{2-1}{1-2} = -1$. The line $y = x$ has gradient $1$. Gradients $-1$ and $1$ multiply to $-1$, so the segments are perpendicular to the mirror. Reflection confirmed.

**Answer:** $\boxed{\text{Reflection in the line } y = x}$. **[B1 for "reflection"; B1 for the equation of the mirror line]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the mirror in words** ("reflected diagonally", "across the slanted line") instead of as an equation. Always write $y = x$, $y = -x$, $x = 0$, etc.
- **Confusing $y = x$ with $y = -x$**. The first reflects across the line through quadrants 1 and 3; the second reflects across the line through quadrants 2 and 4. Always check the sign.
- **Mixing up $y = k$ and $x = k$**. $y = k$ is a horizontal mirror (a "shelf"); $x = k$ is a vertical mirror (a "wall"). The variable that is constant tells you which.
- **Forgetting that orientation flips**. If the question gives you the image as a triangle with vertices labelled in a specific order, the orientation should match the reflection. A clockwise object becomes an anticlockwise image (and vice versa).
- **Computing distance to the mirror wrong** for $y = k$ or $x = h$. Use $2k - y$ or $2h - x$, not just $k - y$ or $h - x$.

## Quick summary

- Reflection needs ONE thing: the mirror line as an equation.
- The mirror is the perpendicular bisector of every (object, image) segment.
- Lengths and angles are preserved (congruent image); orientation reverses.
- The six common mirrors are $y = 0$, $x = 0$, $y = x$, $y = -x$, $y = k$, $x = h$. Learn the swap rule for each.
- "Describe fully" means TYPE (reflection) plus RULE (the mirror equation).
