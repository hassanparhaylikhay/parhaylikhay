## Why this matters

You apply one transformation, then another. The shape moves twice. The question Cambridge loves: **what single transformation has the same effect as both put together?** Sometimes the answer is a clean rotation, sometimes a translation, sometimes a third reflection. Knowing which kind to expect saves you time and earns the marks.

These questions are worth $2$ to $3$ marks each, almost always at the end of a transformation question (after a few parts where you applied each transformation individually). The trick is to track the shape carefully through the two steps, then read off the relationship between the start and the end.

## Order matters

If you apply transformation $A$ first and then transformation $B$, you get one image. If you do them the other way round, $B$ first and then $A$, you usually get a completely different image. Always check which one is applied first.

The one exception: two translations. Whichever order you add the column vectors, you get the same result. That is because adding numbers gives the same total whichever order you add them in.

Everything else, the order matters. A rotation followed by a translation will land in a different place than the same translation followed by the same rotation. When Cambridge gives a multi-step transformation question, read carefully which transformation is applied first.

## Two translations

The simplest case. Adding two column vectors gives the single equivalent translation:

$$\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ b_1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} a_2 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 + a_2 \\ b_1 + b_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

Order does not matter, as we said. Two translations are always equivalent to one translation.

## Two reflections: the big idea

This pattern shows up on almost every paper. Two reflections always combine to give a transformation that is NOT a reflection. It is either a translation or a rotation, depending on whether the two mirrors are parallel or not.

Try picking $y = 0$ then $y = 1$ (parallel mirrors): the result is a translation by $(0, 2)$, twice the perpendicular distance. Then try $y = 0$ then $y = x$ (intersecting at the origin, $45°$ apart): the result is a rotation $90°$ anticlockwise about $(0, 0)$. Then try $y = x$ then $y = 0$: the rotation is in the opposite direction (now clockwise). The order of the two mirrors changes the direction of the rotation, but not its magnitude.

## Worked example: two reflections → one rotation

Triangle $PQR$ has vertices $P(2, 1), Q(4, 1), R(2, 2)$. It is reflected in $y = 0$ to give triangle $P'Q'R'$. Then $P'Q'R'$ is reflected in $y = x$ to give triangle $P''Q''R''$. Describe fully the single transformation that maps $PQR$ onto $P''Q''R''$.

<iframe src="/widgets/transformations-step-explorer.html?preset=p6-two-reflections" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: First reflection in $y = 0$.** Flip the sign of $y$ for each vertex:

$$P(2, 1) \to P'(2, -1), \quad Q(4, 1) \to Q'(4, -1), \quad R(2, 2) \to R'(2, -2)$$

**Step 2: Second reflection in $y = x$.** Swap the two coordinates of each $P'$:

$$P'(2, -1) \to P''(-1, 2), \quad Q'(4, -1) \to Q''(-1, 4), \quad R'(2, -2) \to R''(-2, 2)$$

**Step 3: Identify the single equivalent.** The two mirrors $y = 0$ and $y = x$ cross at the origin. The angle from $y = 0$ (the positive $x$-axis) to $y = x$ is $45°$ anticlockwise. So the combined transformation is a rotation through $2 \times 45° = 90°$ anticlockwise about the crossing point $(0, 0)$.

**Step 4: Quick check.** A $90°$ anticlockwise rotation about the origin sends $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$. Test on $P(2, 1)$: $(2, 1) \to (-1, 2) = P''$. Match.

**Answer:** $\boxed{\text{Rotation, } 90° \text{ anticlockwise, centre } (0, 0)}$. **[B1 for "rotation"; B1 for $90°$ anticlockwise; B1 for centre $(0, 0)$]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying the transformations in the wrong order**. "Reflected in $y = 0$, then in $y = x$" means $y = 0$ first. If you do $y = x$ first you get a different image. Always read which one comes first.
- **Confusing the angle of rotation with the angle between the mirrors**. The rotation is TWICE the angle between the mirrors, not the same.
- **Getting the direction of the rotation wrong**. The rotation goes from the first mirror toward the second. Swap the order and the direction reverses.
- **Forgetting to check parallel vs intersecting**. Parallel mirrors $\Rightarrow$ translation. Intersecting mirrors $\Rightarrow$ rotation. Get this wrong and the whole answer is the wrong type.
- **Stopping at the intermediate shape**. The question asks for the SINGLE transformation that maps the original onto the final. Don't describe both reflections one after another; describe the equivalent.

## Quick summary

- Order matters for almost all transformation pairs (translations are the exception).
- Two translations $=$ one translation (add the column vectors).
- Two reflections in **parallel** mirrors $=$ a translation by twice the perpendicular gap, from the first mirror to the second.
- Two reflections in **intersecting** mirrors $=$ a rotation about the intersection, through twice the angle between the mirrors, in the direction from the first to the second.
- Always answer with the SINGLE equivalent transformation, not the two steps.
