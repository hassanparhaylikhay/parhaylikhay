## Why this matters

Imagine pinning a paper triangle to a board with a thumbtack and spinning it around. The thumbtack is the **centre** of rotation. The amount you spin is the **angle**. The way you spin is the **direction** (clockwise or anticlockwise). Those three numbers fully describe what just happened to your triangle.

Rotation questions on your exam are usually worth $3$ marks: $1$ for the word "rotation", $1$ for the angle and direction, $1$ for the centre coordinate. The trap is forgetting one of the three. "$90°$ about the origin" earns $2$ out of $3$. You need clockwise or anticlockwise too.

If you forget which way "anticlockwise" goes, picture a clock. Clockwise is the natural direction of the hands. Anticlockwise is the opposite. Cambridge mark schemes use both terms; "counterclockwise" means the same as anticlockwise.

Try the preset $90°$ ACW first; that is the most common Cambridge rotation. Then try $180°$ and notice that direction does not matter for $180°$: clockwise $180°$ and anticlockwise $180°$ give the same image. (For every other angle, direction does matter.)

## Three rotations to memorise

Most Cambridge rotation problems use the origin as the centre and a multiple of $90°$. Learn these three coordinate rules:

  <div style="font-family:'Geist Mono',monospace;font-size:11px;color:#7a7875;letter-spacing:0.4px;margin-bottom:10px;text-transform:uppercase;text-align:center">ROTATIONS ABOUT THE ORIGIN · COORDINATE RULES</div>

    <div>· <b style="color:#00abfa">\(90°\) anticlockwise</b>: \((x, y) \to (-y, x)\). The $y$ flips sign and trades places with $x$.</div>
    <div>· <b style="color:#fff067">\(180°\)</b> (either direction): \((x, y) \to (-x, -y)\). Both signs flip.</div>
    <div>· <b style="color:#ff822c">\(90°\) clockwise</b>: \((x, y) \to (y, -x)\). The $x$ flips sign and trades places with $y$.</div>

Cambridge sometimes writes the same rotation in two different ways. "$90°$ clockwise" is the same as "$270°$ anticlockwise". Both earn the mark, but stick to one convention per answer.

## How to find the centre when it's NOT the origin

When the centre is not the origin, you need a way to find it. The trick uses one geometric fact: **the centre is the same distance from each object vertex and its corresponding image vertex**. That puts the centre on the **perpendicular bisector** of every (object, image) line. So if you draw two of those bisectors, they cross at the centre.

There is also a shortcut that works for $180°$ rotations only, because $180°$ is the special case where the centre IS the midpoint of every (object, image) pair. Let's walk through both.

### The 180° shortcut: midpoints coincide

<iframe src="/widgets/transformations-step-explorer.html?preset=p4-find-centre-180" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:640px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

For $180°$ rotation, every point and its image are exactly opposite the centre at equal distances. So the centre lands exactly halfway between them. Compute one midpoint, then check it against another pair to be sure. If they agree, that's your centre.

### The 90° (or any non-180°) method: two perpendicular bisectors

<iframe src="/widgets/transformations-step-explorer.html?preset=p4-find-centre-90" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

For any other angle, the midpoint of $AA'$ is NOT the centre, but the centre still sits on the perpendicular bisector of $AA'$. So draw two perpendicular bisectors (from any two pairs), and they cross at the centre. This is the **trace-paper method** in disguise: trace the object on paper, pin it through a candidate centre, rotate, and check if it lands on the image.

In practice for Cambridge questions, the centre is almost always at a nice integer coordinate (often the origin), so trial-and-error with a quick perpendicular-distance check works fast. The perpendicular bisector method is the back-up when trial and error fails.

## Worked example: identify a rotation

A triangle has vertices $A(1, 1), B(3, 1), C(1, 2)$. After a single transformation, the image has vertices $A'(-1, 1), B'(-1, 3), C'(-2, 1)$. Describe fully the transformation.

<iframe src="/widgets/transformations-step-explorer.html?preset=p4-rotation-identify" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:620px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Rule out other transformations.** The object and image are the same size, so this is not an enlargement. The shifts of the three pairs are different ($A \to A' = (-2, 0)$, $B \to B' = (-4, 2)$, $C \to C' = (-3, -1)$), so this is not a translation. The orientation has not flipped, so this is not a reflection. That leaves rotation.

**Step 2: Try the origin as the centre.** Compute the distance from $(0, 0)$ to each object vertex and to each image vertex:

$$|OA| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}, \quad |OA'| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$$

$$|OB| = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10}, \quad |OB'| = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10}$$

$$|OC| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{5}, \quad |OC'| = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5}$$

All three pairs match. The origin is the centre of rotation.

**Step 3: Find the angle.** Use the rule $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$. Test it on $A$: $(1, 1) \to (-1, 1)$. That matches $A'$. The rule is $90°$ anticlockwise about the origin.

**Answer:** $\boxed{\text{Rotation, } 90° \text{ anticlockwise, centre } (0, 0)}$. **[B1 for "rotation"; B1 for $90°$ anticlockwise; B1 for centre $(0, 0)$]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the direction**. "$90°$ about the origin" loses a mark. Always write clockwise or anticlockwise (except for $180°$, where it doesn't matter).
- **Forgetting the centre**. "Rotation $90°$ anticlockwise" alone is incomplete. You need a centre coordinate.
- **Mixing up the two $90°$ rules**. $90°$ ACW sends $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$. $90°$ CW sends $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$. Pick one and test on a known vertex.
- **Calling a reflection a rotation when both fit**. A $180°$ rotation about the origin sends $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$. A reflection in both axes does the same thing. To tell them apart: check the orientation. Rotation keeps clockwise-labelled vertices clockwise; reflection flips them.
- **Assuming the centre is always the origin**. Often it is, but not always. Cambridge sometimes uses $(1, 1)$ or $(2, 0)$ as the centre. If the origin doesn't work, try other nearby integer points.

## Quick summary

- Rotation needs THREE facts: centre (coordinate), angle (degrees), direction (CW or ACW).
- The image is congruent to the object; orientation is preserved.
- Three rotations to memorise (about the origin):
  - $90°$ ACW: $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$
  - $180°$: $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$
  - $90°$ CW: $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$
- $90°$ CW $=$ $270°$ ACW (same image).
- To find a non-origin centre: distance from centre to each object vertex must equal the distance from centre to its image.
