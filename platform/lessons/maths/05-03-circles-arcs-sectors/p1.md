## Why this matters

A circle question usually opens an exam: 1 or 2 marks for area or circumference, easy to grab if you nail two things.

1. **Use the radius**, not the diameter. If the question gives you the diameter, halve it first.
2. **Read the units carefully**. Circumference is a length (cm, m). Area is a length squared (cm², m²). Mixing them up costs the A1.

Cambridge gives you both formulas on the formula sheet:

$$C = 2\pi r \qquad A = \pi r^2$$

But the M mark is for **showing** the method. Write the formula on your page before substituting, even though it's printed on the sheet.

In this part:

- $C = 2\pi r$ (or $C = \pi d$).
- $A = \pi r^2$.
- "In terms of π" means leave π in the answer.

## Worked example: in terms of π

A circle has radius $9$ cm. Find its circumference and area, in terms of π.

**Circumference.**

$$C = 2\pi r = 2\pi \times 9 = \boxed{18\pi}\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $2\pi r$; A1 for $18\pi$]**

**Area.**

$$A = \pi r^2 = \pi \times 9^2 = \boxed{81\pi}\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for $\pi r^2$; A1 for $81\pi$]**

Full marks $= 4$.

A common slip on the area: writing "$\pi \times 9 = 9\pi$" without squaring. The exponent matters.

## Worked example: a decimal answer

A circle has diameter $14$ cm. Find its area, correct to 1 decimal place.

**Step 1: get the radius.** $r = d \div 2 = 14 \div 2 = 7$ cm.

**Step 2: apply the formula.** $A = \pi r^2 = \pi \times 7^2 = 49\pi$ cm².

**Step 3: convert to a decimal.** $49\pi \approx 49 \times 3.14159 = 153.93\ldots \approx \boxed{153.9}\;\text{cm}^2$.

**[M1 for $\pi r^2$ with the right radius; A1 for $153.9$ with cm² units]**

Full marks $= 2$.

A decimal answer should match the precision the question asks for. "1 decimal place" means one digit after the dot. "3 significant figures" means three meaningful digits regardless of the dot's position. Read carefully.

## Reverse problems: find the radius

If the question gives you the area or circumference and asks for the radius, rearrange.

From $A = \pi r^2$, solving for $r$:

$$r = \sqrt{\dfrac{A}{\pi}}$$

If $A = 36\pi$, then $r = \sqrt{36} = 6$ cm. The π cancels because the answer is already in terms of π.

From $C = 2\pi r$, solving for $r$:

$$r = \dfrac{C}{2\pi}$$

If $C = 24\pi$, then $r = 12$ cm.

## Common mistakes

- **Squaring the diameter instead of halving first.** Always convert to radius before squaring.
- **Forgetting the squared unit on area.** $A = 49\pi$ cm² is correct; $A = 49\pi$ cm is half a mark.
- **Writing a decimal when the question says "in terms of π".** "$153.94$" earns no marks for an in-terms-of-π question. Leave the π in.
- **Doing it the other way too.** Some questions specify "to 3 sf", in which case leaving the answer as $49\pi$ doesn't match.

## Quick summary

| Quantity | Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Circumference | $C = 2\pi r$ or $\pi d$ | cm, m, etc. |
| Area | $A = \pi r^2$ | cm², m², etc. |
| Radius from area | $r = \sqrt{A / \pi}$ | cm, m, etc. |
| Radius from circumference | $r = C / (2\pi)$ | cm, m, etc. |

The four-step habit: identify $r$ (halve $d$ if needed), pick the right formula, substitute, write the unit. That's the whole content of this lesson.
