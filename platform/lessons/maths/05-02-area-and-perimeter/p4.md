## Why this matters

A trapezium is a quadrilateral with **exactly one pair of parallel sides**. Cambridge calls these two parallel sides $a$ and $b$. The other two sides are slants and can be any length.

The area formula looks awkward but it has a clean meaning. If you have a trapezium with parallel sides of length $4$ and $10$, imagine averaging them: $\frac{4 + 10}{2} = 7$. The trapezium has the same area as a **rectangle** with base $7$ and the same perpendicular height. That is what $\tfrac{1}{2}(a+b) \times h$ says: average the parallel sides, multiply by the perpendicular height.

In this part:

- $A = \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h$ where $a$ and $b$ are the parallel sides.
- $P = $ sum of all four sides (no shortcut).

## The formula in one line

$$\boxed{\;A = \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h\;}$$

For perimeter, just add all four sides. There is no clever shortcut: every side has its own length and you sum them.

## Why this works (intuition)

Take a trapezium and a copy of it. Flip the copy upside down and slot it next to the original. You get a **parallelogram** with base $a + b$ and the same perpendicular height $h$.

Parallelogram area $= (a + b) \times h$. The trapezium is half of that, so:

$$A_{\text{trapezium}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h$$

That is the proof. The formula is just "half of the parallelogram you get by doubling the trapezium".

## Worked example

A trapezium has parallel sides $a = 8$ cm and $b = 12$ cm, with a perpendicular height of $5$ cm.

Find its area.

**Step 1: identify the parallel sides.** Read the diagram carefully. The two horizontal sides are parallel: $a = 8$ cm (top) and $b = 12$ cm (bottom).

**Step 2: drop the perpendicular height.** $h = 5$ cm. This is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel sides, NOT the slant.

**Step 3: apply the formula.**

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h = \tfrac{1}{2}(8 + 12) \times 5$$

$$= \tfrac{1}{2}(20) \times 5 = 10 \times 5 = \boxed{50}\;\text{cm}^2$$

## Worked example with perimeter

A trapezium $ABCD$ has parallel sides $AB = 6$ cm and $DC = 14$ cm. The slant sides are $AD = 5$ cm and $BC = 7$ cm. Find the perimeter.

$$P = AB + BC + CD + DA = 6 + 7 + 14 + 5 = \boxed{32}\;\text{cm}$$

**[A1 for $32$ with cm units]**

That is all there is for perimeter: write down all four sides and add. The trick is making sure you read every side from the diagram.

## What if you only know three sides?

Cambridge sometimes gives you a trapezium where one of the slant sides has to be found before you can compute the perimeter. Strategies:

- If the trapezium has a **right angle** at one vertex, the unknown slant is part of a right triangle, and Pythagoras gives it.
- If the diagram gives you the perpendicular height and the **horizontal offset** between the parallel sides, the slant side is $\sqrt{\text{offset}^2 + h^2}$.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the wrong sides as $a$ and $b$.** They MUST be the two parallel sides. Many students grab any two sides; that gives a wrong answer.
- **Using a slant side as the height.** Same trap as the parallelogram. The height $h$ is the perpendicular distance between the parallel sides.
- **Forgetting the $\tfrac{1}{2}$.** Without it you get the area of the doubled-up parallelogram, which is twice too big.
- **Skipping the formula on the page.** Cambridge marks the M1 for "$\tfrac{1}{2}(a+b)h$ seen". Write it before substituting.

## Quick summary

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Area | $\tfrac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h$ |
| Perimeter | sum of all four sides |

The phrase to memorise: **average the parallel sides, multiply by the perpendicular height**. That is the trapezium area in plain English.
