## Why this matters

Sharing a quantity in a given ratio is one of the most-tested skills in O-Level. Cambridge asks it in many disguises: sharing money, splitting ingredients, mixing concrete, dividing inheritance. The method is always the same: find the value of one part, then multiply.

## The "one part" method

If a quantity is shared in the ratio $a : b$, the total number of parts is $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a + b}$. So:

$$\text{value of one part} \;=\; \dfrac{\text{total quantity}}{\textcolor{#0fee89}{a + b}}$$

Then multiply by the ratio number to get each share.

**Always check:** \$24 + \$36 = \$60. The shares should add back to the original total.

## Three or more parts

The same method works for any number of parts. Add them all to get the total parts, divide once to get one part, then multiply for each share.

**Example.** $120$ sweets shared in the ratio $3 : 4 : 5$.

- Total parts: $3 + 4 + 5 = 12$.
- One part: $\tfrac{120}{12} = 10$ sweets.
- Shares: $30, 40, 50$.

Check: $30 + 40 + 50 = 120$. ✓

## Working backwards: given one share

Sometimes Cambridge gives you ONE share and asks for the total. The same logic works in reverse.

**Example.** Two friends share money in the ratio $5 : 7$. The smaller friend gets \$25. How much in total?

The smaller share is **$5$ parts**, valued at \$25. So **$1$ part** is $\tfrac{25}{5} = 5$, i.e. \$5. Total $= 12$ parts $= 12 \times 5 = 60$, i.e. \$60.

The idea: figure out which **number of parts** matches the given share, find one part, then scale up.

## Worked example

Concrete is mixed using cement, sand, and gravel in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5$. To make $36$ kg of concrete, how much sand is needed?

**Step 1: Total parts.** $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$.

**Step 2: One part.** $\tfrac{36}{9} = 4$ kg.

**Step 3: Sand share.** $3 \times 4 = 12$ kg.

**Answer:** $\boxed{12}$ kg of sand. **[M1 for the part value; A1 for the answer]**

Full marks $= 2$.

**Check.** $4 + 12 + 20 = 36$ kg total. ✓

**Trap: don't divide the total by one of the ratio numbers directly.** A common mistake: "share \$60 in $2 : 3$, so smaller $= \tfrac{60}{2} = 30$, i.e. \$30". WRONG. Always add all the parts first to get the divisor, NOT one of the ratio numbers.

## Worked example: working backwards

Three siblings share money in the ratio $2 : 3 : 5$. The eldest receives \$45. How much was shared in total?

**Step 1: Match the share to its part count.** Eldest = largest share = $5$ parts.

**Step 2: One part.** $\tfrac{45}{5} = 9$, i.e. \$9.

**Step 3: Total parts.** $2 + 3 + 5 = 10$.

**Step 4: Total quantity.** $10 \times 9 = 90$, i.e. \$90.

**Answer:** \$$\boxed{90}$. **[M1 for the part value; M1 for using the total parts; A1 for the answer]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the total by one of the ratio numbers, not by the SUM. For \$60 in $2:3$, divide by $5$ (total parts), not by $2$ or $3$.
- Mixing up which share is which. The ratio $a : b$ means the FIRST share is $a$ parts, the SECOND is $b$ parts.
- Forgetting to check that shares add to the total. A quick sum confirms your answer.
- For working-backwards problems, dividing by the wrong part count. Match the given share to its position in the ratio first.

## Quick summary

- Total parts $=$ sum of all the ratio numbers.
- One part $=$ <b style="color:#fff067">total quantity</b> $\div$ <b style="color:#0fee89">total parts</b>.
- Each share $=$ ratio number $\times$ value of one part.
- Always check the shares add back to the original total.
- Working backwards: identify which share is given, divide to find one part, scale up.
