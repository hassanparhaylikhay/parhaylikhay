## Why this matters

Cubic and reciprocal sketches show up on Paper 2 most series. The marking is similar to quadratics: shape mark, root marks, intercept mark.

A cubic given in factored form like $y = (x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3)$ is a gift question: roots are obvious, y-intercept comes from multiplying constants, shape comes from the sign of the leading coefficient. Cambridge gives the cubic in factored form precisely because they want you to read all three roots off it.

A reciprocal $y = \dfrac{a}{x} + b$ is just the basic hyperbola from 2.10 p2 shifted up (or down) by $b$. The asymptotes shift with it.

## Sketching a cubic

For a cubic $y = ax^3 + ...$, four features:

### 1. Shape from leading coefficient

- $a > 0$: bottom-left to top-right (rises overall)
- $a < 0$: top-left to bottom-right (falls overall)

The middle of an a > 0 cubic typically wiggles: a local maximum on the left, local minimum on the right (in between roots). The total shape is the classic **S-curve**.

### 2. Roots from factored form

If you're given $y = (x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3)$, the roots are $r_1, r_2, r_3$ — points where the curve crosses the x-axis.

If a factor is **repeated** (like $(x - 2)^2$), the curve **touches** the x-axis at that root and bounces back, instead of crossing through. This matters for the sketch.

### 3. Y-intercept

Set $x = 0$. For $y = (x - r_1)(x - r_2)(x - r_3)$, this is $(-r_1)(-r_2)(-r_3) = -r_1 r_2 r_3$.

### 4. Behaviour at the roots

Between consecutive roots, the curve must do its single up-down or down-up wiggle to stay continuous. Mark the rough wiggle direction on your sketch.

## Sketching a reciprocal

For $y = \dfrac{a}{x} + b$ (the basic reciprocal shifted up by $b$):

- **Vertical asymptote** still at $x = 0$ (you cannot divide by zero).
- **Horizontal asymptote** at $y = b$ (as $x \to \pm \infty$, $\dfrac{a}{x} \to 0$, so $y \to b$).
- **Shape**: same two-branch hyperbola as $y = \dfrac{a}{x}$, but shifted up by $b$.
- **No y-intercept** (you cannot evaluate at $x = 0$).
- **One x-intercept**: set $y = 0$, get $\dfrac{a}{x} = -b$, so $x = -\dfrac{a}{b}$ (provided $b \neq 0$).

If the question gives $y = \dfrac{a}{x}$ (no shift), the asymptotes are both axes and the curve never crosses either.

## Worked example: cubic

Sketch $y = (x + 2)(x - 1)(x - 3)$, showing all intercepts.

**Step 1: shape.** Expanding the leading term gives $x \times x \times x = x^3$, with coefficient $+1$. Positive cubic: bottom-left to top-right S.

**Step 2: x-intercepts.** Roots at $x = -2$, $x = 1$, $x = 3$.

**Step 3: y-intercept.** $x = 0 \Rightarrow y = (2)(-1)(-3) = 6$.

**Step 4: behaviour.** Between $x = -2$ and $x = 1$ the curve has a local maximum; between $x = 1$ and $x = 3$ it has a local minimum. The y-intercept $(0, 6)$ sits in the upper-left wiggle, confirming the local-max location.

**Answer:** S-shape; x-intercepts at $-2, 1, 3$; y-intercept at $6$. **[B1 shape; B1 each x-intercept (B3 max); B1 y-intercept]**

Full marks $= 5$.

## Worked example: reciprocal

Sketch $y = \dfrac{2}{x} - 1$, marking asymptotes and intercepts.

**Step 1: asymptotes.** Vertical at $x = 0$; horizontal at $y = -1$ (the constant shift).

**Step 2: x-intercept.** Set $y = 0$: $\dfrac{2}{x} = 1$, so $x = 2$. Point $(2, 0)$.

**Step 3: shape.** Two branches. For $a = 2 > 0$, the unshifted reciprocal sits in Q1 and Q3. After shifting down by 1, both branches drop by 1. The right branch (originally Q1) now sits mostly above $y = -1$; the left branch (originally Q3) sits mostly below $y = -1$.

**Answer:** Hyperbola, asymptotes $x = 0$ and $y = -1$, x-intercept at $(2, 0)$. **[B1 shape; B1 each asymptote; B1 x-intercept]**

Full marks $= 4$.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing a positive cubic going from top-left to bottom-right. That's a NEGATIVE cubic. Check the sign of the leading coefficient.
- Forgetting that a repeated root **touches** rather than crosses. $y = (x - 2)^2(x + 1)$ touches at $x = 2$ and crosses at $x = -1$.
- Drawing the asymptote line as part of the curve. The asymptote is a guide; the curve never touches it.
- Confusing $y = \dfrac{a}{x} + b$ with $y = \dfrac{a}{x + b}$. The first shifts up by $b$ (asymptote $y = b$). The second shifts left by $b$ (asymptote $x = -b$).

## Quick summary

- Cubics: shape from sign of leading coefficient; roots from factored form (watch for repeated roots that touch); y-intercept from constant term.
- Reciprocals $y = \dfrac{a}{x} + b$: asymptotes at $x = 0$ and $y = b$; one x-intercept at $-\dfrac{a}{b}$.
- Each labelled feature on your sketch is a B mark.
