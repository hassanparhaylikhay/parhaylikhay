## A page for the curious

Most of unit 2.11 is about getting fast and accurate at sketching the five families Cambridge tests. This page is different. It is a playground.

The widget below lets you add multiple curves on the same grid, slide their coefficients around to see what changes, type your own equations, and see where curves cross each other (those green dots).

There are no marks on this page. Use it to build the kind of intuition that makes a sketch question feel obvious instead of mechanical.

## Things to try

### 1. The roots of a quadratic, visually

Add a `quadratic` and a `linear` curve. Set the line to $y = 0$. The intersections of your parabola with the x-axis are exactly its **roots**. Now slide the parabola's coefficients:

- What happens to the green intersection dots when the discriminant becomes negative?
- Can you make the parabola touch the x-axis at exactly one point?

This is the same content as P1, but the green dots make discriminant signs feel concrete.

### 2. A cubic and its three roots

Add a `cubic`. Slide $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ until you have three real roots (three crossings of the x-axis). Notice how:

- A small bump in $b$ rotates the wiggle.
- $d$ shifts the whole curve up or down, sometimes by enough to merge two roots into one.
- If you set $a$ negative, the S-shape flips. (P2 told you this; now you can feel it.)

### 3. Sine vs cosine: the same curve, shifted

Add `sin` and `cos` with default coefficients. Look at the intersections. Both pass through $y$-values where the two curves agree. Then slide one's $a$ down to $0$. Now the other curve sits on top of the constant.

### 4. Tangent's asymptotes

Add `tan`. Notice the vertical strips where the curve shoots off. Those are the asymptotes at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2b}, \dfrac{3\pi}{2b}, \ldots$. Slide $b$ up and the asymptotes get closer together. Slide $b$ down and they spread out.

### 5. Type your own

Tap `custom` in the add-bar. The card has a text box. Try:

- `1/x` shows the standard hyperbola.
- `1/(x-2)` is the same hyperbola, shifted right by 2.
- `x^3 - 3x` gives a cubic with three roots.
- `sin(x) + x/3` is a sine wave riding a slow rise.
- `e^(-x^2)` is the bell curve. (`e` is Euler's number, `pi` is also available.)

The text box accepts `^` for powers, `*` for multiplication (and `2x` works too, since implicit multiplication is auto-inserted), and the standard functions `sin cos tan asin acos atan log ln exp sqrt abs`.

### 6. Find an intersection on purpose

Type `2x + 1` as a custom curve, then add a `quadratic` and slide its coefficients until the green dot lands on a clean integer point. (Hint: if your line is $y = 2x + 1$ and the parabola is $y = x^2$, the intersections solve $x^2 - 2x - 1 = 0$, which has no integer roots. Try a different line.)

## What to look for

Hover over a curve and a vertical guide line follows your finger. The tooltip shows every active curve's $y$-value at that $x$. Hover near a green dot and the tooltip flips to show the intersection coordinates exactly.

Slide a parameter and watch the equation in the card update live. The mental model you want by the end: when the question says "$y = ax^2 + bx + c$", you can already see the parabola in your head.

## Why this matters for exams

You don't get a graphing calculator on the 4024 paper. But you do get sketch questions where the marker is checking whether you've pictured the curve correctly. Time spent here trains the picture-in-your-head:

- A negative leading coefficient flips the shape. Felt that.
- Adding to the constant shifts the curve vertically. Felt that.
- The number of intersections of $y_1$ and $y_2$ equals the number of solutions of $y_1 = y_2$. Felt that.

The widget is a sandbox; the exam paper is the real game. But the better your sandbox time, the faster the real game gets.
