## Why this matters

Exponential sketches are the "unusual" curve in 2.11. They are not polynomials, they have an asymptote, and they grow (or decay) in a way that makes them easy to confuse with each other. You need to know the standard shape and to identify the y-intercept and the asymptote on sight.

## The general form

$$y = a \times r^x + b$$

with $a$ a non-zero rational, $b$ rational, and $r$ a positive rational ($r \neq 1$).

Three features drive the sketch:

### 1. Y-intercept at $(0, a + b)$

Because $r^0 = 1$ for any positive $r$. Setting $x = 0$:

$$y = a \times 1 + b = a + b$$

So the curve always crosses the y-axis at $a + b$.

### 2. Horizontal asymptote at $y = b$

As $|x| \to \infty$, $r^x \to 0$ on one side and $\to \infty$ on the other. The "vanishing" side approaches $y = b$. This is the horizontal asymptote.

- If $r > 1$ (growth): $r^x \to 0$ as $x \to -\infty$, so the curve flattens to $y = b$ on the LEFT.
- If $0 < r < 1$ (decay): $r^x \to 0$ as $x \to +\infty$, so the curve flattens to $y = b$ on the RIGHT.

### 3. Direction (growth vs decay)

| Case | $r$ | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | $r > 1$ | Curve rises steeply to the right, flattens to $y = b$ on the left |
| Decay | $0 < r < 1$ | Curve rises steeply to the left, flattens to $y = b$ on the right |

For positive $a$, the curve sits **above** the asymptote. For negative $a$, it sits **below**.

## Worked example: growth

Sketch $y = 3 \times 2^x - 1$, showing the y-intercept and asymptote.

**Step 1: y-intercept.** $x = 0 \Rightarrow y = 3 \times 1 - 1 = 2$. Point $(0, 2)$.

**Step 2: asymptote.** $b = -1$, so horizontal asymptote at $y = -1$.

**Step 3: shape.** $r = 2 > 1$ → growth. $a = 3 > 0$ → curve above asymptote. Curve rises steeply to the right; flattens toward $y = -1$ as $x \to -\infty$.

**Answer:** Exponential growth shape, y-intercept $(0, 2)$, asymptote $y = -1$. **[B1 shape; B1 y-intercept; B1 asymptote]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Worked example: decay

Sketch $y = 4 \times \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^x$.

**Step 1: y-intercept.** $x = 0 \Rightarrow y = 4 \times 1 = 4$. Point $(0, 4)$.

**Step 2: asymptote.** $b = 0$, so the asymptote is the x-axis $y = 0$.

**Step 3: shape.** $r = 0.5 < 1$ → decay. $a = 4 > 0$ → above asymptote. Curve falls from upper-left, passes through $(0, 4)$, flattens toward $y = 0$ on the right.

**Answer:** Exponential decay, y-intercept $(0, 4)$, asymptote $y = 0$. **[B1 shape; B1 y-intercept; B1 asymptote]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the asymptote line as part of the curve. The asymptote is a dashed guide line; the curve approaches but never touches it.
- Confusing growth and decay. Look at $r$, not $a$. $r > 1$ is growth; $r < 1$ is decay.
- Putting the y-intercept at $(0, 0)$. Exponentials never pass through the origin (unless $a = 0$, which is degenerate). The y-intercept is at $(0, a + b)$.
- Drawing the curve crossing the asymptote. For positive $a$, the curve always stays on one side of the asymptote.

## Quick summary

- Form: $y = a \times r^x + b$.
- Y-intercept: $(0, a + b)$.
- Horizontal asymptote: $y = b$.
- $r > 1$ → growth (rises right). $0 < r < 1$ → decay (falls right).
- For $a > 0$, curve sits above the asymptote everywhere.
