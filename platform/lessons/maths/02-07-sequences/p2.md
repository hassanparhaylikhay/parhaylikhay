## Why this matters

You start saving Rs. $200$ this month and add Rs. $50$ every following month. Month 1 you have $200$, month 2 you have $250$, ... what about month $24$? You could keep adding, or you can write the **nth term formula** once and substitute $n = 24$ to get the answer in one step. That formula is the key to Paper 1's "find the $n$-th term" questions, worth $2$ marks every time.

A **linear** (or **arithmetic**) sequence has a constant difference between consecutive terms. The nth term takes a clean form Cambridge always asks for.

## The nth term formula

For a linear sequence with **common difference** $\textcolor{#fff067}{d}$ and **first term** $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a}$, the $n$-th term is

$$T_n \;=\; \textcolor{#fff067}{d} n + (\textcolor{#0fee89}{a} - \textcolor{#fff067}{d})$$

The Cambridge-preferred form: write it as $jn + k$ where $j$ is the common difference and $k$ is whatever makes $n = 1$ give the first term.

## The recipe

To find the nth term of a linear sequence:

1. **Compute the common difference $d$** (the constant change between consecutive terms).
2. **Write $T_n = dn + k$** with $k$ unknown.
3. **Substitute $n = 1$** and the first term to find $k$.
4. **Check** with the second term.

**Example.** Sequence $3, 8, 13, 18, \dots$.

**Step 1.** Common difference $d = 5$.

**Step 2.** $T_n = 5n + k$.

**Step 3.** $n = 1$: $T_1 = 5(1) + k = 3$, so $k = -2$.

**Step 4.** $T_n = 5n - 2$. Check at $n = 2$: $5(2) - 2 = 8$ ✓.

## Why $jn + k$ matters

Cambridge marks the nth term as a single expression in $n$, not the recursive "add $5$ each time" rule. The formula $5n - 2$ means:

- The 1st term is $5(1) - 2 = 3$ ✓
- The 50th term is $5(50) - 2 = 248$
- The 100th term is $5(100) - 2 = 498$

You compute any term in one substitution. That's the whole point.

## Find the position of a term

If you know a value and want to know what position it sits at, **set the formula equal to that value and solve**.

**Example.** Sequence has nth term $2n + 5$. What position is the term $25$?

$$2n + 5 = 25$$

$$2n = 20$$

$$n = 10$$

So the term $25$ is in position $10$. It's the 10th term.

## Decreasing sequences (negative $d$)

Common difference can be **negative**. The recipe is identical.

**Example.** $100, 95, 90, 85, \dots$.

$d = -5$ (each term is $5$ less than the last).

$T_n = -5n + k$. At $n = 1$: $-5 + k = 100$, so $k = 105$.

$T_n = -5n + 105$, also written $105 - 5n$.

Check at $n = 2$: $105 - 10 = 95$ ✓.

The same formula handles increasing (positive $d$) and decreasing (negative $d$) sequences.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: $T_n$ in $jn + k$ form is what Cambridge wants.** Writing "$T_n = a + (n - 1)d$" gives the right value but loses you the **A1** mark for "fully simplified form". Multiply out and collect: $a + (n-1)d = dn + (a - d)$. Then state $T_n = dn + (a-d)$.

</div>

## Worked example

The first four terms of a sequence are $11, 15, 19, 23$.

**Step 1: Common difference.** $d = 4$.

**Step 2: $T_n = 4n + k$.** At $n = 1$: $4 + k = 11$, so $k = 7$.

**Step 3: Verify.** $4(2) + 7 = 15$ ✓, $4(3) + 7 = 19$ ✓.

**Answer:** $\boxed{T_n = 4n + 7}$. **[B1 for $4n + k$; A1 for the constant; or M1 + A1 if working is shown]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Writing $T_n = a + nd$ instead of $a + (n-1)d$. The first term is at $n = 1$, so the formula must give $T_1 = a$ exactly.
- Forgetting to fully simplify. Cambridge expects $jn + k$ form for full marks.
- Sign error on a decreasing sequence. $100, 95, 90$ has $d = -5$, not $5$. The signs propagate.
- Mistaking "the term is X" for "X is the position". Find a position by setting the formula equal to the value and solving for $n$.

## Quick summary

- **Linear** (arithmetic) sequences have a constant common difference $d$.
- nth term takes the form $T_n = jn + k$, where $j = d$ and $k$ is found from $T_1$.
- Compute any term: substitute the position. Find a position: solve the formula = value.
- Negative $d$: the sequence decreases; the formula still has the form $jn + k$.
