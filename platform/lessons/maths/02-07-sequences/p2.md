## Why this matters

You start saving Rs. $200$ this month and add Rs. $50$ every following month. Month 1 you have $200$, month 2 you have $250$, ... what about month $24$? You could keep adding, or you can write the **nth term formula** once and substitute $n = 24$ to get the answer in one step. That formula is the key to Paper 1's "find the $n$-th term" questions, worth $2$ marks every time.

A **linear** (or **arithmetic**) sequence has a constant difference between consecutive terms. The nth term takes a clean form Cambridge always asks for.

## The nth term formula

$$T_n \;=\; \textcolor{#0fee89}{a} \;+\; (n - 1)\, \textcolor{#fff067}{d}$$

$\textcolor{#0fee89}{a}$ is the **first term**, $\textcolor{#fff067}{d}$ is the **common difference**. Substitute the position $n$ to get any term.

## The recipe

To find the nth term of a linear sequence:

1. **Identify $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a}$ (the first term)** and **$\textcolor{#fff067}{d}$ (the common difference)** between consecutive terms.
2. **Substitute into $T_n = a + (n-1)d$.**
3. **Simplify** by expanding the bracket and collecting like terms into $jn + k$ form.

**Example.** Sequence $3, 8, 13, 18, \dots$.

**Step 1.** $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a = 3}$, $\textcolor{#fff067}{d = 5}$.

**Step 2.** $T_n = 3 + (n - 1)(5)$.

**Step 3.** Expand and simplify:

$$T_n = 3 + 5n - 5 = 5n - 2$$

**Check at $n = 2$:** $5(2) - 2 = 8$ ✓.

## Why the simplified form matters

The substituted form $3 + (n-1)(5)$ gives the right values, but Cambridge marks the **simplified** form for the accuracy mark. The expression $5n - 2$ means:

- The 1st term is $5(1) - 2 = 3$ ✓
- The 50th term is $5(50) - 2 = 248$
- The 100th term is $5(100) - 2 = 498$

You compute any term in one substitution. That's the whole point.

## Find the position of a term

If you know a value and want to know what position it sits at, **set the formula equal to that value and solve**.

**Example.** Sequence has nth term $2n + 5$. What position is the term $25$?

$$2n + 5 = 25$$

$$2n = 20$$

$$n = 10$$

So the term $25$ is in position $10$. It's the 10th term.

## Decreasing sequences (negative $d$)

Common difference can be **negative**. The formula is unchanged.

**Example.** $100, 95, 90, 85, \dots$.

$\textcolor{#0fee89}{a = 100}$, $\textcolor{#fff067}{d = -5}$.

$$T_n = 100 + (n - 1)(-5)$$

Expand and simplify:

$$T_n = 100 - 5n + 5 = 105 - 5n$$

Check at $n = 2$: $105 - 10 = 95$ ✓.

Same formula, signs propagate naturally.

## Worked example

The first four terms of a sequence are $11, 15, 19, 23$.

**Step 1: First term and common difference.** $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a = 11}$, $\textcolor{#fff067}{d = 4}$.

**Step 2: Substitute into $T_n = a + (n-1)d$.**

$$T_n = 11 + (n - 1)(4)$$

**Step 3: Expand and simplify.**

$$T_n = 11 + 4n - 4 = 4n + 7$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{T_n = 4n + 7}$. **[M1 for $a + (n-1)d$ with values; A1 for the simplified form]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Writing $T_n = a + nd$ instead of $a + (n-1)d$. The first term sits at $n = 1$, so we use $(n - 1)$ jumps of $d$ to get $T_1 = a$ exactly.
- Forgetting to simplify. The substituted form gives correct values, but Cambridge wants the cleaned-up $jn + k$ for the accuracy mark.
- Sign error on a decreasing sequence. $100, 95, 90$ has $d = -5$, not $5$. Carry the minus sign through the bracket carefully.
- Mistaking "the term is X" for "X is the position". Find a position by setting the formula equal to the value and solving for $n$.

## Quick summary

- **Linear** (arithmetic) sequences have a constant common difference $d$.
- **nth term formula**: $T_n = a + (n - 1)d$.
- Substitute the values of $a$ and $d$, then **expand and simplify** to $jn + k$ form.
- Compute any term: substitute the position. Find a position: solve the formula = value.
- Negative $d$: the sequence decreases; same formula applies.
