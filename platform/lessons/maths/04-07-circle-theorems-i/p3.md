## Why this matters

These are the two theorems that catch students out. The cyclic-quadrilateral rule is easy once you spot the four vertices on the circle. The alternate segment theorem is the trickiest of the lot - it links a tangent to an angle on the far side of the chord. Both reward careful diagram-reading and a clean reason statement.

In this part:

- Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180°$.
- Alternate segment theorem: angle between tangent and chord = angle in the alternate segment.

## Theorem: opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180°$

A **cyclic quadrilateral** has all four vertices on a circle. The two pairs of **opposite** angles each sum to $180°$.

**Reason to write:** "opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180°$".

Note: it is the **opposite** pairs that sum to $180°$, not the adjacent pairs. Adjacent angles can be anything.

A neat consequence: if you know one angle, the angle directly across from it is forced. The other diagonal pair is independent.

## Theorem: alternate segment theorem

A tangent touches the circle at $T$, and a chord $TQ$ is drawn from the contact point. The angle between the tangent and the chord equals the angle in the **alternate segment** - that is, the angle inscribed in the segment on the **other** side of the chord.

In the diagram, the angle between the tangent and chord $TQ$ at point $T$ equals the angle $TPQ$ inscribed in the segment on the other side.

**Reason to write:** "alternate segment theorem".

The trick is to spot the right pair. The angle is between the **tangent** and the **chord**. The matching angle is inscribed in the **opposite** segment, with its vertex on the far arc, and its arms going to the same two endpoints of the chord.

## Worked example

In the circle, $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral with angle $BAD = 95°$. The tangent at $D$ meets line $DC$ at angle $40°$ on the same side as $A$.

Find:

(a) angle $BCD$.

(b) angle $DBC$ (where $B$ is on the major arc cut by chord $DC$).

**Step 1 (a): cyclic quadrilateral.**

Angles $BAD$ and $BCD$ are opposite in the cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$.

$$\text{angle } BCD = 180° - 95° = 85°$$

**Step 2 (b): alternate segment theorem.**

The tangent at $D$ makes a $40°$ angle with chord $DC$. By the alternate segment theorem, this equals the inscribed angle in the segment on the other side of chord $DC$ - that is, the angle $DBC$ at vertex $B$ on the major arc.

$$\text{angle } DBC = 40°$$

**Answer:**

(a) angle $BCD = 85°$ (opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180°$). **[M1 subtraction; A1 value; B1 reason]**

(b) angle $DBC = 40°$ (alternate segment theorem). **[A1 value; B1 reason]**

Full marks $= 5$.

## Common mistakes

- **Pairing the wrong angles in a cyclic quadrilateral.** It is opposite, not adjacent. In quadrilateral $ABCD$ the pairs are $(A, C)$ and $(B, D)$.
- **Using the rule on a non-cyclic quadrilateral.** All four vertices must lie on the circle. If even one vertex is off the circle, the rule does not apply.
- **Picking the wrong "alternate" angle.** The matching inscribed angle is on the **opposite** side of the chord from where the tangent-chord angle is measured. Sketch both segments lightly before deciding.
- **Stating "alternate angles" instead of "alternate segment theorem".** "Alternate angles" is the parallel-lines rule. They are unrelated. Use the full name "alternate segment theorem".

## Quick summary

- Cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles sum to $180°$.
- Alternate segment theorem: tangent-chord angle = inscribed angle in alternate segment.
- The reason wording matters: write "opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral sum to $180°$" or "alternate segment theorem" exactly.
- Cambridge often combines these with the same-segment and centre rules in a single multi-step question.
