## Why this matters

These two facts come from the **symmetry** of a circle. They are simpler than the angle theorems in 4.7 - no angle chasing - but they unlock a few standard exam moves:

- "Find the length of a chord given the radius and the distance from the centre" (Pythagoras inside the circle).
- "Find the centre of a circle given three points on it" (intersection of perpendicular bisectors).
- "Show that two chords are equal" (use distance from the centre).

In this part:

- Equal chords are equidistant from the centre.
- The perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre.

## Theorem: equal chords are equidistant from the centre

If two chords have the same length, their perpendicular distances from the centre are equal. The converse is also true: chords at equal distance from the centre have the same length.

**Reason to write:** "equal chords are equidistant from the centre" (or its converse "chords equidistant from the centre are equal").

How to use it in calculations: drop a perpendicular from the centre to the chord. That perpendicular **bisects** the chord, so you get a right triangle with:

- hypotenuse = radius $r$,
- one leg = distance from centre $d$,
- other leg = half the chord length.

Then Pythagoras gives:

$$r^2 = d^2 + \left(\dfrac{\text{chord}}{2}\right)^2$$

## Theorem: perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre

Take any chord. Find its midpoint. Draw the perpendicular line through that midpoint. That line passes through the centre of the circle.

Equivalently: the perpendicular dropped from the centre to a chord meets the chord at its midpoint.

## Recipe: find the centre given three points

If you are given three points $A$, $B$, $C$ that lie on a circle and asked for the centre:

1. Treat $AB$ as one chord. Find its midpoint and draw its perpendicular bisector.
2. Treat $BC$ as a second chord. Find its midpoint and draw its perpendicular bisector.
3. The two perpendicular bisectors meet at one point. That point is the centre.

You only need two chords because two lines fix a point. A third chord (using $AC$) is a useful check - all three perpendicular bisectors should meet at the same point.

## Worked example

A chord of a circle has length $24$ cm. Its perpendicular distance from the centre is $5$ cm.

(a) Find the radius of the circle.

(b) A second chord in the same circle is $7$ cm from the centre. Find its length.

**Step 1 (a): set up the right triangle.**

The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so half the chord is $12$ cm. The right triangle has legs $5$ and $12$, hypotenuse $r$.

**Step 2 (a): apply Pythagoras.**

$$r^2 = 5^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$$

$$r = 13 \text{ cm}$$

**Step 3 (b): use the same circle, new chord.**

For the new chord at distance $7$ cm from centre, with $r = 13$:

$$13^2 = 7^2 + \left(\dfrac{\text{chord}}{2}\right)^2$$

$$169 - 49 = \left(\dfrac{\text{chord}}{2}\right)^2$$

$$\left(\dfrac{\text{chord}}{2}\right)^2 = 120$$

$$\dfrac{\text{chord}}{2} = \sqrt{120} = 2\sqrt{30}$$

$$\text{chord} = 4\sqrt{30} \approx 21.9 \text{ cm}$$

**Answer:**

(a) $r = 13$ cm. **[M1 set up Pythagoras with half-chord; A1 value]**

(b) chord $= 4\sqrt{30}$ cm $\approx 21.9$ cm. **[M1 substitute; A1 value]**

Full marks $= 4$.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the full chord length as a leg.** The perpendicular from the centre **bisects** the chord, so you must use **half** the chord length as the leg of the right triangle.
- **Confusing "distance from centre" with "radius".** The distance from the centre to a chord is shorter than the radius (unless the chord is a single point - which never happens). The radius is the hypotenuse.
- **Drawing the perpendicular bisector but forgetting to extend it to the centre.** The bisector of a chord is a full line. If your sketch only shows a short segment, extend it.
- **Trying to use only one chord to find the centre.** A single chord's perpendicular bisector passes through the centre but does not pin down where on that line the centre is. You need two chords.

## Quick summary

- Equal chords $\Leftrightarrow$ equidistant from the centre.
- Perpendicular bisector of any chord goes through the centre.
- Half-chord, distance from centre, and radius form a right triangle: $r^2 = d^2 + (\text{half chord})^2$.
- To find the centre from three points: intersect two perpendicular bisectors.
