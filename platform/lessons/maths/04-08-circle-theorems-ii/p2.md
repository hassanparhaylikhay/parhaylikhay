## Why this matters

A common Cambridge setup: from an external point, two tangents are drawn to a circle, forming an "ice-cream cone" shape with the circle. Once you spot it, several facts unlock at once:

- The two tangent lengths are equal.
- Each tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
- The triangle formed by the two contact points and the external point is isosceles.
- The line from the external point to the centre bisects the angle between the tangents.

This is the only theorem in 4.8 that involves angles, and it shows up in both Paper 1 ("find the missing length") and Paper 2 ("show that... and hence find...").

## Theorem: tangents from an external point are equal

If $P$ is a point outside a circle and two tangents from $P$ touch the circle at $A$ and $B$, then $PA = PB$.

**Reason to write:** "tangents from an external point are equal".

The picture above also shows the two radii $OA$ and $OB$. Each of those is perpendicular to its tangent at the point of contact. So in fact you have **two right-angled triangles** $OAP$ and $OBP$, both with:

- the radius as one leg ($OA = OB = r$),
- the tangent as the other leg ($PA = PB$),
- the line $OP$ as the shared hypotenuse.

These two triangles are **congruent** (RHS), which is another way to see why $PA = PB$.

## The kite OAPB

The four vertices $O$, $A$, $P$, $B$ form a **kite**: $OA = OB$ (both radii) and $PA = PB$ (equal tangents). The diagonal $OP$ is its axis of symmetry, so it bisects both angle $AOB$ and angle $APB$.

When you see two tangents from an external point, immediately mark these on your diagram:

1. Right angles at $A$ and $B$ (tangent perpendicular to radius).
2. $PA = PB$ (equal tangents).
3. Triangle $PAB$ is **isosceles**, with $PA = PB$ as the equal sides.
4. The line $OP$ from external point to centre **bisects** angle $APB$ (and bisects angle $AOB$).

## Worked example

In the diagram, $PA$ and $PB$ are tangents from external point $P$ to a circle with centre $O$. Angle $APB = 56°$ and the radius is $r = 7$ cm.

(a) Find the length $PA$.

(b) Find angle $OPA$.

(c) Find angle $PAB$ (the base angle of the isosceles triangle $PAB$).

**Step a: Length $PA$.** $\angle OAP = 90°$ (tangent ⟂ radius). $OP$ bisects $\angle APB$, so $\angle OPA = \dfrac{56°}{2} = 28°$. In right triangle $OAP$ with opposite $OA = 7$ and adjacent $PA$:

$$\tan 28° = \dfrac{7}{PA} \;\Rightarrow\; PA = \dfrac{7}{\tan 28°} \approx \boxed{13.2}\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for the right triangle with $\tan$; A1 for $13.2$]**

**Step b: Angle $OPA$.** $OP$ bisects $\angle APB$ (tangents from an external point), so $\angle OPA = \dfrac{56°}{2} = \boxed{28°}$. **[B1 for $28°$]**

**Step c: Angle $PAB$.** $\triangle PAB$ is isosceles with $PA = PB$ (tangents from an external point are equal), so the base angles at $A$ and $B$ are equal:

$$\angle PAB = \dfrac{180° - 56°}{2} = \boxed{62°}$$

**[M1 for the base-angle method; A1 for $62°$]**

Full marks $= 5$.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the two tangent lengths as different.** They are always equal. Mark $PA = PB$ on the diagram immediately.
- **Forgetting the right angle at the contact point.** Without that, you cannot use $\tan$ or Pythagoras in triangle $OAP$.
- **Using angle $APB$ directly inside triangle $OAP$.** The angle in $OAP$ at $P$ is **half** of angle $APB$, because $OP$ bisects the apex.
- **Saying triangle $PAB$ is equilateral.** It is isosceles. It is only equilateral when angle $APB = 60°$, which is a special case.
- **Stating only "tangents equal" without the "from external point" qualifier.** The full reason is "tangents from an external point are equal".

## Quick summary

- $PA = PB$ when both are tangents from the same external point $P$.
- Two right-angled triangles $OAP$ and $OBP$ are congruent (RHS).
- Triangle $PAB$ is isosceles.
- $OP$ bisects angle $APB$ and angle $AOB$.
- Reason wording: "tangents from an external point are equal".
