## Why this matters

If your bike covers $\tfrac{2}{3}$ km in one minute and you ride for $\tfrac{x}{3}$ minutes, the distance is $\tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{x}{3} = \tfrac{2x}{9}$ km. Multiplying fractions with letters in them is exactly the same arithmetic as multiplying numeric fractions; the letters just come along for the ride. Dividing has one extra step, **keep, flip, multiply**, and then it's the same again.

Cambridge tests both directly on Paper 2. The key insight: **multiplication and division of fractions are far easier than addition and subtraction**, because you don't need a common denominator.

## Multiplying: top times top, bottom times bottom

For any two fractions:

$$\dfrac{a}{b} \times \dfrac{c}{d} \;=\; \dfrac{a \times c}{b \times d}$$

The **numerators multiply together** to form the new top. The **denominators multiply together** to form the new bottom. Then simplify by cancelling any common factors.

**Example.** $\dfrac{2x}{3} \times \dfrac{9}{4x}$.

$$\dfrac{2x \times 9}{3 \times 4x} \;=\; \dfrac{18x}{12x}$$

Cancel the shared $x$ on top and bottom: $\dfrac{18}{12}$. Then simplify the numbers: $\dfrac{3}{2}$.

So $\dfrac{2x}{3} \times \dfrac{9}{4x} = \dfrac{3}{2}$.

## Cancelling before multiplying saves time

You don't have to wait until the end to cancel. If a factor on a top matches a factor on a bottom (in either fraction), you can cancel diagonally **before** multiplying. This keeps numbers small.

**Example.** $\dfrac{2x}{3} \times \dfrac{9}{4x}$ again, but cancelling first.

$$\dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{2}\textcolor{#0fee89}{x}}{\textcolor{#00abfa}{3}} \times \dfrac{\textcolor{#00abfa}{9}}{\textcolor{#fff067}{4}\textcolor{#0fee89}{x}}$$

- $\textcolor{#0fee89}{x}$ on top of the first cancels with $\textcolor{#0fee89}{x}$ on the bottom of the second.
- $\textcolor{#00abfa}{3}$ on the bottom of the first divides into $\textcolor{#00abfa}{9}$ on the top of the second, leaving $3$.
- $\textcolor{#fff067}{2}$ on the top of the first cancels with the $\textcolor{#fff067}{4}$ on the bottom of the second, leaving $2$ on the bottom.

Result: $\dfrac{1 \times 3}{1 \times 2} = \dfrac{3}{2}$.

Same answer, less arithmetic.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: only cancel a FACTOR, not a TERM.** You can cancel $x$ from $\dfrac{2x}{4x}$ because $x$ is a factor of both the top and the bottom. You CANNOT cancel $x$ from $\dfrac{x + 2}{x + 4}$, because $x$ is a TERM there, not a factor. Cancelling requires the whole top or the whole bottom to be a product.

</div>

## Dividing: keep, flip, multiply

To divide one fraction by another, **flip the second fraction (reciprocal) and multiply**. Three quick words sum it up: **keep, flip, multiply**.

$$\dfrac{a}{b} \div \dfrac{c}{d} \;=\; \dfrac{a}{b} \times \dfrac{d}{c} \;=\; \dfrac{ad}{bc}$$

- **Keep** the first fraction as is.
- **Flip** the second (swap its top and bottom).
- **Multiply** as usual.

**Example.** $\dfrac{3a}{4} \div \dfrac{9a}{10}$.

**Step 1: Keep, flip, multiply.**

$$\dfrac{3a}{4} \times \dfrac{10}{9a}$$

**Step 2: Multiply tops and bottoms.**

$$\dfrac{30a}{36a}$$

**Step 3: Cancel.**

$$\dfrac{30}{36} \;=\; \dfrac{5}{6}$$

So $\dfrac{3a}{4} \div \dfrac{9a}{10} = \dfrac{5}{6}$.

## Algebraic factors that cancel

When tops and bottoms contain bracketed expressions, the **whole bracket** can cancel if it appears identically in both.

**Example.** $\dfrac{x}{x + 1} \times \dfrac{x + 1}{2}$.

$$\dfrac{x \times (x + 1)}{(x + 1) \times 2}$$

The $\textcolor{#fff067}{(x + 1)}$ on top matches the $\textcolor{#fff067}{(x + 1)}$ on the bottom. Cancel:

$$\dfrac{x}{2}$$

This kind of cancellation is the punchline of many Cambridge questions. As long as the bracket on top is identical to the bracket on bottom, cancel.

**Example.** $\dfrac{x + 2}{3} \times \dfrac{6}{x + 2}$.

$$\dfrac{(x + 2) \times 6}{3 \times (x + 2)}$$

$\textcolor{#fff067}{(x + 2)}$ cancels. Then $\dfrac{6}{3} = 2$.

So the answer is $\boxed{2}$.

## Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{x^2 - 4}{6} \times \dfrac{3}{x + 2}$.

**Step 1: Factor the top of the first fraction.** $x^2 - 4$ is a difference of squares: $(x - 2)(x + 2)$.

$$\dfrac{(x - 2)(x + 2)}{6} \times \dfrac{3}{x + 2}$$

**Step 2: Multiply.**

$$\dfrac{(x - 2)(x + 2) \times 3}{6 \times (x + 2)}$$

**Step 3: Cancel.** $(x + 2)$ on top matches $(x + 2)$ on bottom. $\dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

$$\dfrac{x - 2}{2}$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{\dfrac{x - 2}{2}}$. **[M1 for factoring; M1 for the multiplication; A1 for the simplified result]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Cancelling a TERM instead of a FACTOR. $\dfrac{x + 3}{x}$ is NOT $1 + 3 = 4$; nothing cancels because $x$ is not a factor of $(x + 3)$.
- Flipping the wrong fraction in division. **Keep** the first, **flip** the second, then multiply. Don't flip both.
- Forgetting to factorise tops or bottoms before cancelling. Often the cancellation only becomes visible after a difference-of-squares or perfect-square factorisation.
- Cross-cancelling without checking. $\dfrac{2x}{3} \times \dfrac{9}{4x}$: the $x$'s cancel, but $\dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{9}{4}$ still simplifies via $3$ into $9$ and $2$ into $4$.

## Quick summary

- **Multiply**: top times top, bottom times bottom. Cancel any common factor.
- **Divide**: keep, flip, multiply.
- Cancel only **factors**, not terms inside a sum.
- Factorise tops and bottoms first when bracketed expressions hint at hidden common factors.
- Check the final answer for any remaining common factor.
