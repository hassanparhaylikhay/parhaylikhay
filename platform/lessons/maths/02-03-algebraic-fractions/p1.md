## Why this matters

You and three friends each chip in different fractions of a Daewoo ticket: you pay $\tfrac{1}{3}$, your friend pays $\tfrac{1}{4}$, and the rest is split between two more. To find the total before the rest, you need a common denominator and then add the tops together. Algebraic fractions work exactly the same way, only the tops and bottoms have letters tagging along.

Cambridge tests adding and subtracting algebraic fractions on Paper 2 directly, and indirectly inside equation-solving questions (2.5 p2). The technique is identical to numeric fractions: **find a common denominator, scale each fraction up, add or subtract the tops, simplify**. This part hammers the steps until they feel automatic.

## Step 1: find the common denominator

When the two fractions have **different bottoms**, you can't add them directly. The shortcut: scale each fraction so its bottom matches the other's, then add the tops.

The common denominator can be just one of the two existing bottoms (when one divides the other), or the product of the two bottoms (when they share no common factor).

| Fractions | Common denominator | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{x}{2}$ | $\textcolor{#fff067}{6}$ | LCM of $3$ and $2$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{1}{2x}$ | $\textcolor{#fff067}{2x}$ | $2x$ already contains $x$ |
| $\dfrac{3}{x+1} + \dfrac{2}{x-1}$ | $\textcolor{#fff067}{(x+1)(x-1)}$ | no shared factor |
| $\dfrac{a}{4} - \dfrac{b}{6}$ | $\textcolor{#fff067}{12}$ | LCM of $4$ and $6$ |

Pick the **lowest common multiple** (LCM) of the two bottoms. Multiplying the bottoms together always works, but the LCM keeps the numbers small.

## Step 2: scale each fraction

Multiply the **top and bottom** of each fraction by whatever turns its bottom into the common denominator. The fraction's value doesn't change because top and bottom both scale equally.

**Example.** $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{x}{2}$.

Common denominator is $\textcolor{#fff067}{6}$.

- $\dfrac{x}{3}$: multiply top and bottom by $\textcolor{#0fee89}{2}$ to get $\dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{2}x}{6}$.
- $\dfrac{x}{2}$: multiply top and bottom by $\textcolor{#0fee89}{3}$ to get $\dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{3}x}{6}$.

Now both fractions sit over the same $6$.

## Step 3: combine the tops

With matching bottoms, add (or subtract) the tops over the shared bottom.

$$\dfrac{2x}{6} + \dfrac{3x}{6} \;=\; \dfrac{2x + 3x}{6} \;=\; \dfrac{5x}{6}$$

That's the answer. Always check whether the result simplifies further (in this case, $5x$ and $6$ share no factor, so it's done).

## Putting it together: a clean walkthrough

Simplify $\dfrac{2x}{5} - \dfrac{x}{3}$.

**Step 1: Common denominator.** LCM of $5$ and $3$ is $\textcolor{#fff067}{15}$.

**Step 2: Scale each fraction.**

$$\dfrac{2x}{5} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{6}x}{15} \qquad \dfrac{x}{3} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{5}x}{15}$$

**Step 3: Subtract the tops.**

$$\dfrac{6x}{15} - \dfrac{5x}{15} \;=\; \dfrac{6x - 5x}{15} \;=\; \dfrac{x}{15}$$

Done.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: don't add the bottoms.** $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{x}{2} \ne \dfrac{2x}{5}$. Bottoms NEVER add. Re-read this rule whenever your gut wants to write $\dfrac{a}{b} + \dfrac{c}{d} = \dfrac{a + c}{b + d}$. That is wrong every time.

</div>

## When the top is itself a sum (use brackets)

If a fraction's top has more than one term, **wrap it in brackets** when you scale it up. This avoids sign mistakes.

**Example.** $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{x - 4}{2}$.

**Step 1: Common denominator.** $\textcolor{#fff067}{6}$.

**Step 2: Scale.**

$$\dfrac{x}{3} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{2}x}{6} \qquad \dfrac{x - 4}{2} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{3}(x - 4)}{6} \;=\; \dfrac{3x - 12}{6}$$

**Step 3: Add the tops.**

$$\dfrac{2x + (3x - 12)}{6} \;=\; \dfrac{5x - 12}{6}$$

The brackets around $(x - 4)$ are important when subtracting, because the minus would distribute through both terms.

## Linear denominators: use the product

When the bottoms are **linear** expressions like $x + 1$ and $x - 1$, the common denominator is usually their **product** $(x + 1)(x - 1)$.

**Example.** $\dfrac{3}{x + 1} + \dfrac{2}{x - 1}$.

**Step 1: Common denominator.** $\textcolor{#fff067}{(x + 1)(x - 1)}$.

**Step 2: Scale.**

$$\dfrac{3}{x + 1} \;=\; \dfrac{3(x - 1)}{(x+1)(x-1)} \qquad \dfrac{2}{x - 1} \;=\; \dfrac{2(x + 1)}{(x+1)(x-1)}$$

**Step 3: Combine the tops.**

$$\dfrac{3(x - 1) + 2(x + 1)}{(x+1)(x-1)} \;=\; \dfrac{3x - 3 + 2x + 2}{(x+1)(x-1)} \;=\; \dfrac{5x - 1}{(x+1)(x-1)}$$

The bottom is usually left in factored form unless the question asks you to expand.

## Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{2}{x + 2} + \dfrac{3}{2x - 1}$.

**Step 1: Common denominator.** $\textcolor{#fff067}{(x + 2)(2x - 1)}$.

**Step 2: Scale.**

$$\dfrac{2}{x + 2} \;=\; \dfrac{2(2x - 1)}{(x+2)(2x-1)} \qquad \dfrac{3}{2x - 1} \;=\; \dfrac{3(x + 2)}{(x+2)(2x-1)}$$

**Step 3: Add the tops.**

$$\dfrac{2(2x - 1) + 3(x + 2)}{(x+2)(2x-1)} \;=\; \dfrac{4x - 2 + 3x + 6}{(x+2)(2x-1)} \;=\; \dfrac{7x + 4}{(x+2)(2x-1)}$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{\dfrac{7x + 4}{(x+2)(2x-1)}}$. **[M1 for the common denominator; M1 for scaling; A1 for the simplified top]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Adding the bottoms. $\dfrac{a}{b} + \dfrac{c}{d} \ne \dfrac{a + c}{b + d}$. Bottoms NEVER add.
- Forgetting brackets when scaling a multi-term top. $\dfrac{x - 4}{2}$ scaled to $/6$ is $\dfrac{3(x - 4)}{6} = \dfrac{3x - 12}{6}$, NOT $\dfrac{3x - 4}{6}$.
- Sign error on subtraction with brackets. $\dfrac{a}{p} - \dfrac{b - c}{q}$: scale $b - c$, then carry the minus through the WHOLE bracket: $\dfrac{aq - p(b - c)}{pq} = \dfrac{aq - pb + pc}{pq}$.
- Skipping the simplification check at the end. Always look for a common factor in the final top and bottom.

## Quick summary

- Find the **common denominator** (LCM of the bottoms; product if no shared factor).
- **Scale** each fraction by multiplying top and bottom by the missing piece.
- **Add or subtract the tops** over the shared bottom.
- Use **brackets** around any multi-term top before scaling.
- Always check the final answer for a common factor to cancel.
