## Why this matters

Increase and decrease problems are everywhere on Cambridge papers: discounts, profit, depreciation, salary rises, growth in population. The fastest method is the **multiplier**, a single number that does the whole change in one step. Once it clicks, you stop using slow "find the change, then add or subtract" working.

## The multiplier method

For an increase of $\textcolor{#fff067}{P}\%$, multiply the original by $1 + \tfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{P}}{100}$.

For a decrease of $\textcolor{#fff067}{P}\%$, multiply by $1 - \tfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{P}}{100}$.

| Change | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| $+20\%$ | $1.20$ |
| $+5\%$ | $1.05$ |
| $-15\%$ | $0.85$ |
| $-30\%$ | $0.70$ |
| $-5\%$ | $0.95$ |
| $+100\%$ | $2.00$ (doubled) |

The intuition: $100\%$ of the original is the original itself, so the multiplier $1.00$ means "no change". Adding $20\%$ on top makes it $1.20$. Taking $15\%$ off makes it $0.85$.

The multiplier is one step. **Original $\times$ multiplier $=$ new value.**

## Increase examples

**Example.** A price of \$80 is increased by $20\%$.

$$\$80 \times 1.20 \;=\; \$96$$

**Example.** A salary of \$2\,000 rises by $5\%$.

$$\$2\,000 \times 1.05 \;=\; \$2\,100$$

## Decrease examples

**Example.** A price of \$50 is reduced by $30\%$.

$$\$50 \times 0.70 \;=\; \$35$$

**Example.** A jacket priced at \$240 has $15\%$ off.

$$\$240 \times 0.85 \;=\; \$204$$

## Finding the percentage change

If you know the **before** and **after** values and want the percentage change:

$$\textcolor{#0fee89}{\text{percentage change}} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{\text{change}}}{\textcolor{#00abfa}{\text{original}}} \times 100\%$$

Where **change $=$ new $-$ original** (positive for increase, negative for decrease).

**Example.** A price rises from \$40 to \$50.

- Change: \$50 - \$40 = \$10.
- Percentage: $\tfrac{10}{40} \times 100\% = 25\%$ increase.

**Example.** A weight drops from $80$ kg to $72$ kg.

- Change: $80 - 72 = 8$ kg (a decrease).
- Percentage: $\tfrac{8}{80} \times 100\% = 10\%$ decrease.

The denominator is **always the ORIGINAL**, not the new value. This catches students every year.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: the denominator is the ORIGINAL value, not the new value.** A price rising from \$40 to \$50 is a $25\%$ increase ($\tfrac{10}{40}$), NOT a $20\%$ increase ($\tfrac{10}{50}$). Always divide by the starting amount.

## Worked example

A laptop is listed at \$1\,200. The shop is offering $12\%$ off. What is the sale price?

**Step 1: Find the multiplier.** $-12\%$ gives multiplier $1 - 0.12 = 0.88$.

**Step 2: Multiply.**

$$\$1\,200 \times 0.88 \;=\; \$1\,056$$

**Answer:** \$$\boxed{1\,056}$. **[M1 for the multiplier; A1 for the sale price]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: percentage change

A house was bought for \$120\,000 and sold for \$150\,000. What is the percentage profit?

**Step 1: Find the change.** \$150\,000 - \$120\,000 = \$30\,000.

**Step 2: Apply the formula.**

$$\dfrac{30\,000}{120\,000} \times 100\% \;=\; 25\%$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{25\%}$ profit. **[M1 for the change/original ratio; A1 for the percentage]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Using the wrong denominator for percentage change. Always divide by the ORIGINAL value, not the new one.
- Two-step "find the change then add" instead of using a multiplier. Slower and more error-prone. Multiplier is one step.
- Forgetting to subtract for a decrease multiplier. $-15\%$ gives $0.85$ ($1 - 0.15$), NOT $-0.15$.
- Adding instead of multiplying. "\$80 increase by $20\%$" is NOT \$80 + 20, but \$80 × 1.20 = \$96.

## Quick summary

- <b style="color:#0fee89">Increase</b> by $P\%$: multiply by $1 + \tfrac{P}{100}$.
- <b style="color:#ff4670">Decrease</b> by $P\%$: multiply by $1 - \tfrac{P}{100}$.
- Percentage change: $\tfrac{\text{change}}{\text{original}} \times 100\%$. Original goes on the bottom.
- The multiplier method is one step; use it instead of "change then add".
