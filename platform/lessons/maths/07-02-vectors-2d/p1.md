## Why this matters

Throw a cricket ball $20\,\text{metres}$. That tells me HOW FAR, but not WHERE the ball went. To describe the throw completely, you also need the direction: $20\,\text{metres}$ towards the boundary, or $20\,\text{metres}$ straight up. A quantity with both a size AND a direction is a **vector**.

In maths, you already met one vector in 7.1: the column vector that described a translation. Now we treat the column vector as a thing in its own right, not just a translation rule. Vectors get their own notation, their own arithmetic (adding, subtracting, scaling), and their own questions in Cambridge papers, $2$ to $5$ marks each.

The simplest way to describe a vector on a grid is to write down its two components: how far it goes RIGHT and how far it goes UP. That is the **column vector** $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}$ you already used for translations.

## Three names for the same thing

Cambridge uses three different ways to write the same vector. They all mean exactly the same arrow.

<div class="diagram" style="border:1px solid #141e2a;border-radius:10px;padding:14px 16px;margin:14px 0;background:#0b1118">
  <div style="font-family:'Geist Mono',monospace;font-size:11px;color:#7a7875;letter-spacing:0.4px;margin-bottom:10px;text-transform:uppercase;text-align:center">THE THREE NOTATIONS</div>
  <div style="display:flex;flex-direction:column;gap:10px;font-size:14px;line-height:1.6">
    <div>· <b style="color:#00abfa">\(\vec{AB}\)</b> means "the vector from point \(A\) to point \(B\)". The arrow on top shows direction. Read it as "AB" but never forget the arrow.</div>
    <div>· <b style="color:#fff067">\(\mathbf{a}\)</b> means "the vector called \(a\)". Bold letter. When you handwrite it, underline instead: \(\underline{a}\).</div>
    <div>· <b style="color:#ff822c">\(\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\)</b> is the column form, the two numbers that say how far right and up.</div>
  </div>
</div>

Notice the last one is just two numbers in a tall bracket. So a vector named $\mathbf{a}$ with $\vec{AB} = \mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$ goes $3$ units right and $4$ units up.

Try moving $A$ to the right by $1$ and $B$ to the right by $1$. The arrow looks identical, just shifted. The column vector $\vec{AB}$ does not change. **A vector does not care WHERE you draw it**; only its length and direction matter.

## Finding $\vec{AB}$ from coordinates

Given two points, the vector between them is "image minus object":

$$\vec{AB} = B - A = \begin{pmatrix} x_B - x_A \\ y_B - y_A \end{pmatrix}$$

Top is the change in $x$ from $A$ to $B$. Bottom is the change in $y$. If you remember "image minus object" from translations in 7.1, this is the same trick. The image is just whichever point you are going TO.

## The reverse vector: $\vec{BA} = -\vec{AB}$

If $\vec{AB}$ takes you from $A$ to $B$, then $\vec{BA}$ takes you the other way, from $B$ back to $A$. The arrow is the same length but points in the opposite direction. The column vector flips both signs:

$$\vec{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec{BA} = \begin{pmatrix} -a \\ -b \end{pmatrix} = -\vec{AB}$$

For a quick check, $\vec{AB} + \vec{BA}$ should equal $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, the **zero vector**: go from $A$ to $B$ and then back to $A$, you have moved nowhere.

## Worked example: find $\vec{AB}$

Point $A$ is at $(2, 1)$. Point $B$ is at $(5, 5)$. Write $\vec{AB}$ as a column vector.

<iframe src="/widgets/transformations-step-explorer.html?preset=7p2-p1-find-vector" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:620px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Set up $B - A$.** $\vec{AB}$ always equals the end point minus the start point.

**Step 2: Subtract component by component.**

$$\vec{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 - 2 \\ 5 - 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{\vec{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}}$. **[M1 for setting up $B - A$; A1 for the correct column]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing $A - B$ instead of $B - A$.** The vector $\vec{AB}$ starts at $A$ and ENDS at $B$. To get there, subtract the start FROM the end. End minus start.
- **Writing the vector in row form**: $(3, 4)$. Cambridge marks the row form wrong for vectors; columns only.
- **Forgetting the arrow on $\vec{AB}$.** Without the arrow it looks like the product of two letters. The arrow tells the reader "this is a vector, from $A$ to $B$".
- **Not underlining the bold letter when handwriting**. $\mathbf{a}$ is bold in print, but you cannot bold pen ink. Underline: $\underline{a}$.
- **Saying "the same vector starts at the same point"**. Wrong. Two parallel arrows of the same length anywhere on the grid are the same vector.

## Quick summary

- A vector has size AND direction. A column vector $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}$ captures both.
- Three notations for the same vector: $\vec{AB}$, $\mathbf{a}$ (or $\underline{a}$ handwritten), and the column form.
- $\vec{AB} = B - A$: end minus start.
- $\vec{BA} = -\vec{AB}$: the reverse vector flips both signs.
- A vector is fixed by its components alone; the starting point does not matter.
