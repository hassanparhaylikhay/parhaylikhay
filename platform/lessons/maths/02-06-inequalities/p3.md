## Why this matters

A bank requires you to keep at least Rs. $10\,000$ minimum balance AND deposit at most $50\%$ of your salary monthly. Each requirement is a **constraint**; together they define a 2D **region** of all valid (balance, deposit) combinations. Inequalities in two variables work the same way: each inequality cuts the plane into a "valid" half and an "invalid" half, and several together define the **feasible region**.

Cambridge tests this on Paper 2: sketch the region defined by a list of inequalities, and identify the integer points inside it. The technique is purely visual; once you see it on a graph, the algebra is just bookkeeping.

## A single linear inequality in $x$ and $y$

A single inequality like $y \leq 2x + 1$ describes a **half-plane**:

1. **Draw the boundary line** $y = 2x + 1$.
2. **Decide which side** of the line satisfies the inequality.
3. **Shade the region** (or follow Cambridge's convention to shade the *unwanted* region).

For $y \leq 2x + 1$, the line itself is included (because of $\leq$), and we want all points where $y$ is **at most** $2x + 1$ : that's the region **on or below** the line.

## Solid vs broken boundary

The boundary type tells you whether the line itself is part of the region:

| Inequality | Boundary | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| $y \leq 2x + 1$ | $\textcolor{#0fee89}{\text{solid}}$ | line included |
| $y < 2x + 1$ | $\textcolor{#ff4670}{\text{broken (dashed)}}$ | line NOT included |
| $y \geq 2x + 1$ | $\textcolor{#0fee89}{\text{solid}}$ | line included |
| $y > 2x + 1$ | $\textcolor{#ff4670}{\text{broken}}$ | line NOT included |

Solid for "or equal to" ($\leq$, $\geq$). Broken for strict ($<$, $>$). This visual cue is **always tested** on Paper 2.

## Above or below: use a test point

To decide which side of the line is the solution region, **pick a test point not on the line** and check the inequality.

The origin $(0, 0)$ is the easiest test point unless the line passes through it.

**Example.** Region for $y \leq 2x + 1$.

Test $(0, 0)$: is $0 \leq 2(0) + 1$? Is $0 \leq 1$? Yes. So $(0, 0)$ is in the region. The region includes the origin and the half-plane below the line.

**Example.** Region for $y > 3x - 1$.

Test $(0, 0)$: is $0 > 3(0) - 1$? Is $0 > -1$? Yes. So $(0, 0)$ is in the region. The region is above the line (and not on it, since strict).

This single test always works and saves you from getting confused about "$y >$" vs "$y \geq$" vs the slope of the line.

## Vertical and horizontal boundaries

Some inequalities don't have $y$ at all, like $x \geq 2$. The boundary is a **vertical line** at $x = 2$, and the region is everything to the right of (and on) it.

Similarly, $y < 4$ has a horizontal boundary at $y = 4$, and the region is everything below it (broken line because of $<$).

| Inequality | Boundary | Region |
|---|---|---|
| $x \geq 2$ | vertical line $x = 2$ (solid) | right of the line |
| $x < -1$ | vertical line $x = -1$ (broken) | left of the line |
| $y \leq 0$ | horizontal line $y = 0$ (solid) | on and below |
| $y > 3$ | horizontal line $y = 3$ (broken) | above only |

## Multiple inequalities: the feasible region

When two or more inequalities apply at once, the **feasible region** is the intersection : the set of points satisfying ALL of them at once.

**Example.** Sketch the region defined by $x \geq 1$, $y \geq 0$, and $x + y \leq 5$.

- $x \geq 1$: solid vertical line at $x = 1$, region to the right.
- $y \geq 0$: solid horizontal line at $y = 0$ (the $x$-axis), region above.
- $x + y \leq 5$: solid diagonal line $y = -x + 5$, region below.

The three regions overlap in a **triangle** with vertices at $(1, 0)$, $(5, 0)$, and $(1, 4)$.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: Cambridge's "shade the unwanted" convention.** Cambridge marks the region you DO NOT want, leaving the wanted region clear. So you shade everything OUTSIDE the feasible region. Always read the question carefully and follow the convention it tells you.

</div>

## Testing whether a point is in a region

Cambridge often asks: "Does the point $(p, q)$ lie inside the region?" To answer:

1. Substitute $(p, q)$ into each inequality.
2. If ALL are true, the point is inside (or on a boundary).
3. If any one is false, the point is outside.

**Example.** Is $(2, 2)$ inside the region $x \geq 1$, $y \geq 0$, $x + y \leq 5$?

- $x = 2 \geq 1$ ✓
- $y = 2 \geq 0$ ✓
- $x + y = 4 \leq 5$ ✓

All three pass, so $(2, 2)$ is inside the region.

## Worked example

The region $R$ is defined by $y \geq x$, $y \leq 4$, and $x \geq 0$. List the integer points $(x, y)$ inside $R$.

**Step 1: Sketch.** Three boundaries:

- $y = x$ (solid, region above).
- $y = 4$ (solid, region below).
- $x = 0$ (solid, region right).

The intersection is a triangle with vertices at $(0, 0)$, $(4, 4)$, and $(0, 4)$.

**Step 2: List the integer points.** For each integer $x$ from $0$ to $4$, the valid $y$ range is from $x$ (lower) to $4$ (upper) inclusive.

- $x = 0$: $y \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ : $5$ points
- $x = 1$: $y \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ : $4$ points
- $x = 2$: $y \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ : $3$ points
- $x = 3$: $y \in \{3, 4\}$ : $2$ points
- $x = 4$: $y = 4$ : $1$ point

**Step 3: Total.** $5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15$ integer points.

**Answer:** $\boxed{15}$ integer points. **[M1 for the region; A1 for the count]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing a solid line for a strict inequality (or broken for inclusive). Always match the line type to the symbol.
- Shading the wrong half. Always TEST a point : the origin if it's not on the line.
- Forgetting one of several constraints. Every inequality must be satisfied; if one fails, the point is outside.
- Mishandling vertical/horizontal boundaries. $x \geq 2$ is a vertical line, NOT $y \geq 2$.

## Quick summary

- A linear inequality in $x$ and $y$ defines a **half-plane**.
- Boundary line: **solid** for $\leq$ / $\geq$, **broken** for $<$ / $>$.
- Pick a **test point** (often the origin) to decide which side is the region.
- Multiple inequalities → **feasible region** is their intersection.
- Cambridge often shades the UNWANTED region; read the question.
