## Why this matters

Fraction arithmetic shows up in almost every Number question on the paper, and Cambridge specifically expects you to handle improper fractions and mixed numbers without a calculator. The four operations have different rules for fractions, and mixing them up loses easy marks.

The single biggest mistake: trying to add or subtract without a common denominator, or thinking multiplication needs one (it doesn't).

## Adding and subtracting

Both need a **common denominator** so the slices are the same size before you combine them.

**Example.** $\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{2}$.

The denominators are $4$ and $2$. The lowest common multiple is $4$, so rewrite both fractions with denominator $4$:

$$\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{2} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{2}{4} \;=\; \tfrac{3}{4}$$

The $\tfrac{1}{2}$ becomes $\tfrac{2}{4}$ because we multiplied top and bottom by $2$. The fraction's value doesn't change; only the way we write it does.

For subtraction, same setup: $\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{4}{6} - \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{3}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

## Multiplying

Multiplying fractions is the easiest operation: just multiply tops together and bottoms together.

$$\dfrac{a}{b} \times \dfrac{c}{d} \;=\; \dfrac{a \times c}{b \times d}$$

**Example.** $\tfrac{3}{5} \times \tfrac{2}{7} = \tfrac{6}{35}$.

You can also **cancel** common factors before multiplying to keep the numbers small. For $\tfrac{3}{8} \times \tfrac{4}{9}$: notice $8$ and $4$ share a factor of $4$, and $3$ and $9$ share a factor of $3$. Cancel:

$$\dfrac{\textcolor{#00abfa}{3}}{\textcolor{#fff067}{8}} \times \dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{4}}{\textcolor{#00abfa}{9}} \;=\; \dfrac{1}{2} \times \dfrac{1}{3} \;=\; \dfrac{1}{6}$$

Cancelling first or simplifying after gives the same answer; cancelling first keeps the arithmetic friendlier.

**Example.** $\tfrac{2}{3} \div \tfrac{4}{5}$:

$$\tfrac{2}{3} \div \tfrac{4}{5} \;=\; \tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{5}{4} \;=\; \tfrac{10}{12} \;=\; \tfrac{5}{6}$$

## Mixed numbers

Whenever the question has a mixed number like $2\tfrac{1}{3}$, **convert to an improper fraction first** (the rule from 1.4 p1):

$$2\tfrac{1}{3} \;=\; \tfrac{2 \times 3 + 1}{3} \;=\; \tfrac{7}{3}$$

Then apply the four-operation rules normally. Convert back to a mixed number at the end if Cambridge asks for it.

## Worked example

Compute $1\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{2}{5}$ and give your answer as a mixed number in simplest form.

**Step 1: Convert the mixed number** to improper.

$$1\tfrac{1}{2} \;=\; \tfrac{1 \times 2 + 1}{2} \;=\; \tfrac{3}{2}$$

**Step 2: Common denominator** for $\tfrac{3}{2} + \tfrac{2}{5}$ is $10$.

$$\tfrac{3}{2} + \tfrac{2}{5} \;=\; \tfrac{15}{10} + \tfrac{4}{10} \;=\; \tfrac{19}{10}$$

**Step 3: Convert back to a mixed number.** $19 \div 10 = 1$ remainder $9$.

**Answer:** $\boxed{1\tfrac{9}{10}}$. **[M1 for the conversion + common denominator; A1 for the mixed-number final form]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Adding tops and adding bottoms separately: $\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{2}{6}$. Wrong: addition needs a common denominator first.
- Trying to find a common denominator for multiplication. You don't need one. Just multiply straight across.
- Forgetting to flip when dividing. $\tfrac{2}{3} \div \tfrac{4}{5}$ is NOT $\tfrac{2 \div 4}{3 \div 5}$. It's $\tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{5}{4}$.
- Doing arithmetic on mixed numbers without converting first. $1\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{2}{5}$ is NOT $1\tfrac{1+2}{2+5}$. Convert to improper, then add.
- Leaving the answer un-simplified. Mark schemes credit lowest terms only.

## Quick summary

- <b style="color:#00abfa">+ / −</b>: common denominator first, then combine tops, then simplify.
- <b style="color:#0fee89">×</b>: top × top, bottom × bottom. Cancel before multiplying if you can.
- <b style="color:#fff067">÷</b>: keep, flip, multiply.
- Mixed numbers: convert to improper before any operation; convert back at the end.
