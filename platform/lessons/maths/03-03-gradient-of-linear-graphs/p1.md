## Why this matters

The **gradient** of a line is one number that captures everything about its tilt. Cambridge tests the gradient formula on Paper 1 (calculate from two points), Paper 2 (use it to find equations), and inside many word problems where "rate" really means "gradient".

Once you can compute it from two points, you can do most of unit 3.

## What gradient means

Pick any two points on a straight line. As you move from one to the other, you change your $x$ by some amount (the **run**) and your $y$ by some amount (the **rise**).

$$\text{gradient } m \;=\; \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} \;=\; \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$$

If the rise is $6$ for every run of $3$, the gradient is $\dfrac{6}{3} = 2$. The line goes up $2$ units for every $1$ unit you move to the right.

The gradient does not depend on which two points you pick - for a straight line, it's the same number anywhere along it.

## The two-point formula

Given any two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ on the line:

$$m \;=\; \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

The order matters in BOTH the numerator and the denominator - they must match. Either compute $\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$ OR $\dfrac{y_1 - y_2}{x_1 - x_2}$. Both give the same answer. Mixing them up (e.g. $\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_1 - x_2}$) flips the sign and is a common mistake.

## Worked example: positive gradient

Find the gradient of the line through $A(1, 2)$ and $B(5, 14)$.

**Step 1: identify $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$.** Let $(x_1, y_1) = (1, 2)$ and $(x_2, y_2) = (5, 14)$.

**Step 2: substitute into the formula.**

$$m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \dfrac{14 - 2}{5 - 1}$$

**Step 3: evaluate.**

$$m = \dfrac{12}{4} = 3$$

**Answer:** Gradient is $3$. **[M1 substitute; A1 simplify]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: negative gradient

Find the gradient of the line through $P(-3, 5)$ and $Q(2, -5)$.

**Step 1: identify points.** $(x_1, y_1) = (-3, 5)$, $(x_2, y_2) = (2, -5)$.

**Step 2: substitute.**

$$m = \dfrac{-5 - 5}{2 - (-3)} = \dfrac{-10}{5}$$

**Step 3: evaluate.**

$$m = -2$$

**Answer:** Gradient is $-2$. The line slopes downhill. **[M1 substitute; A1 simplify]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Why "y on top, x on bottom"

A common slip is putting the x-difference on top. Mnemonic: "**Y**ou go up first, then across" - Y comes first, on top. (Same way you read a coordinate: x first, then y. The gradient formula is the only place that flips it: y in the numerator.)

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up the order in numerator vs denominator.** If you put $y_2$ first on top, put $x_2$ first on the bottom too. Don't mix.
- **Forgetting brackets around negatives.** $5 - (-3) = 5 + 3 = 8$. Without brackets you might write $5 - -3$ and lose the second negative.
- **Writing run/rise instead of rise/run.** Cambridge always wants $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$. Saying "the gradient is the run divided by the rise" is wrong.
- **Computing $\dfrac{y_2 + y_1}{x_2 + x_1}$.** That's the midpoint formula's structure, not gradient. Subtract, don't add.

## Quick summary

- Gradient = rise ÷ run = $\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$.
- Two-point formula: $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$.
- Match the order: y order on top must match x order on bottom.
- Bracket negative coordinates when subtracting.
