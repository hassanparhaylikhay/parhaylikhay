## Why this matters

Two lines are **parallel** when they tilt at the same angle - they never meet, no matter how far you extend them. The condition is simple: **same gradient**.

Cambridge tests this in two main ways: "is line 1 parallel to line 2?" (compare gradients) and "find the equation of the line parallel to L through point P" (use L's gradient + the recipe from 3.5).

## The rule

For two non-vertical lines $y = m_1 x + c_1$ and $y = m_2 x + c_2$:

$$\textbf{parallel} \iff m_1 = m_2 \;\;\text{and}\;\; c_1 \neq c_2$$

If both gradient AND intercept are equal, they're the **same line**, not "parallel" in the usual sense.

For vertical lines ($x = k_1$ and $x = k_2$), they're parallel as long as $k_1 \neq k_2$.

## Quick recognition

To check if two lines are parallel, write each in $y = mx + c$ form and compare the $m$'s.

**Example.** Are $2x + y = 5$ and $4x + 2y = 9$ parallel?

Rearrange both:

- $2x + y = 5 \;\Rightarrow\; y = -2x + 5$, so $m_1 = -2$.
- $4x + 2y = 9 \;\Rightarrow\; 2y = -4x + 9 \;\Rightarrow\; y = -2x + \dfrac{9}{2}$, so $m_2 = -2$.

Same gradient, different y-intercepts ($5 \neq \dfrac{9}{2}$). So **parallel**. ✓

## Worked example: find the parallel line through a point

Find the equation of the line parallel to $y = 3x - 7$ that passes through $(2, 1)$.

**Step 1: gradient of the parallel line.**

Parallel ⇒ same gradient as $y = 3x - 7$, so $m = 3$.

**Step 2: substitute the point into $y = 3x + c$.**

$$1 = 3(2) + c$$

**Step 3: solve.**

$$1 = 6 + c \;\Rightarrow\; c = -5$$

**Step 4: write the final equation.**

**Answer:** $y = 3x - 5$. **[B1 gradient; M1 substitute; A1 final]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Why "same gradient" works

Gradient is the angle the line makes with the horizontal. Two lines tilted at the SAME angle, but starting at DIFFERENT heights, never cross - they march in lockstep, always the same vertical distance apart. That's exactly what "parallel" means in geometry.

If the y-intercepts were also equal, the two lines would be IDENTICAL (every point in common). For "parallel" Cambridge expects them to be distinct.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming "different y-intercept" is the condition.** It's same $m$ that makes parallel; different $c$ just makes them distinct.
- **Reusing the original $c$ from L.** If the question gives $y = 3x - 7$ and asks for a parallel line through $(2, 1)$, the new $c$ is **not** $-7$ - you must recompute it from the new point.
- **Comparing $c$ when you should compare $m$.** Two lines with the same y-intercept can have ANY gradients; that's not the parallel condition.
- **Forgetting that two vertical lines are also parallel.** $x = 3$ and $x = -1$ are parallel.

## Quick summary

- Parallel iff $m_1 = m_2$ (and $c_1 \neq c_2$).
- To find a parallel line through a point: keep the gradient, find the new $c$ from the point.
- Two vertical lines are parallel as long as they're distinct.
