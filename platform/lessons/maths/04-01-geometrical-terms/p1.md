## Why this matters

Cambridge often gives you a question that says "give a reason" or "name the shape" or "describe this angle". One word, one mark. If you write "this line cuts the other line" instead of "this line is **perpendicular** to the other line", the mark scheme cannot give you that B1. The vocabulary IS the mark.

This part is the dictionary. It has no calculation; the goal is that every word here triggers an instant picture in your head, and every picture has a word ready to describe it. Once that link is solid, the rest of unit 4 (constructions, similarity, symmetry, angles, circle theorems) reads like ordinary English.

## Points, lines, planes

A **point** is a single position; it has no size. We mark it with a small cross or a labelled dot, e.g. $A$.

A **line** is a straight path with no thickness. In O-Level we usually mean a **line segment**: a piece of a line with two endpoints, like $AB$. The notation $AB$ on its own can mean either the segment or its length, depending on context.

A **plane** is a flat surface that extends in two directions. The page in your notebook is a plane.

## Parallel and perpendicular

Two lines are **parallel** if they run in the same direction and never meet, no matter how far you extend them. Think of the two long edges of a cricket pitch. We mark parallel lines with little arrows: $>$ on one pair, $>>$ on a different pair.

Two lines are **perpendicular** if they meet at exactly $90°$ (a right angle). Perpendicular gets a small square at the corner, never an arc. The corner of a brick is a perpendicular meeting.

A **perpendicular bisector** of a segment $AB$ is the line that

1. is perpendicular to $AB$, and
2. passes through the midpoint of $AB$.

Every point on the perpendicular bisector is the same distance from $A$ as from $B$. (You will not be asked to construct this with compasses at O-Level, but you should still recognise the term.)

## Bearings

A **bearing** is a direction given as a three-figure angle measured clockwise from North. Always three digits, from $000°$ to $360°$:

- Due North $= 000°$
- Due East $= 090°$
- Due South $= 180°$
- Due West $= 270°$

A bearing of $025°$ means $25°$ clockwise from North. Even small bearings get a leading zero : you write $025°$, not $25°$. Mark schemes deduct for missing digits.

## Angle types

When two line segments meet at a point, they form an angle. The size of an angle is measured in degrees. We name angles by the type:

| Name | Size | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| Acute | $0° < \theta < 90°$ | sharp corner |
| Right | $\theta = 90°$ | square corner |
| Obtuse | $90° < \theta < 180°$ | wider than square |
| Straight | $\theta = 180°$ | flat line |
| Reflex | $180° < \theta < 360°$ | "outside" angle, more than a straight |

A **reflex** angle is the *larger* angle when you go around the long way. At any vertex the acute/obtuse angle and its reflex partner add to $360°$.

To name an angle we use three letters: $\angle ABC$ means the angle at vertex $B$, formed by going from ray $BA$ to ray $BC$. The middle letter is always the vertex. Cambridge requires the three-letter form whenever a diagram has multiple angles at the same point.

## Interior and exterior angles

For any polygon (a closed shape with straight sides):

- An **interior angle** is the angle inside the shape, at one of its vertices.
- An **exterior angle** is the angle between one side and the **extension** of the next side. Interior $+$ exterior $= 180°$ at every vertex.

For a regular polygon with $n$ sides, all exterior angles are equal and they add to $360°$, so each exterior angle $= \dfrac{360°}{n}$.

## Similar and congruent

Two shapes are **congruent** if you can place one exactly on top of the other (allowing flips and rotations). Same shape AND same size. All matching sides equal, all matching angles equal.

Two shapes are **similar** if one is an enlargement of the other. Same shape, possibly different size. All matching angles are equal, and all matching sides are in the same ratio. That ratio is the **scale factor**:

$$\text{scale factor} = \dfrac{\text{new length}}{\text{old length}}$$

If scale factor $= 1$, the shapes are also congruent. If scale factor $= 2$, every length doubles, every area is $\times 4$, every volume is $\times 8$. (More on this in 4.4.)

## Triangles by sides and angles

By **sides**:

- **Equilateral**: all three sides equal. All three angles also equal ($60°$).
- **Isosceles**: exactly two sides equal. The two angles opposite the equal sides are also equal.
- **Scalene**: all three sides different. All three angles different too.

By **angles**:

- **Right-angled**: one angle is $90°$.
- **Acute-angled**: all three angles are below $90°$.
- **Obtuse-angled**: one angle is above $90°$ (the other two must then be acute).

A right-angled isosceles triangle has angles $90°, 45°, 45°$. The corner of half a square.

## Quadrilaterals

A **quadrilateral** has four sides. The interior angles always add to $360°$. The named special cases:

- **Square**: 4 equal sides, 4 right angles.
- **Rectangle**: opposite sides equal, 4 right angles.
- **Rhombus**: 4 equal sides; opposite angles equal (but not necessarily right angles). A "tilted square".
- **Parallelogram**: opposite sides parallel and equal; opposite angles equal.
- **Kite**: two pairs of adjacent (touching) equal sides. One diagonal is the perpendicular bisector of the other.
- **Trapezium**: exactly one pair of parallel sides.

A square is also a rectangle, a rhombus and a parallelogram. A rhombus is also a parallelogram. A rectangle is also a parallelogram. The hierarchy matters when a question says "give the *most specific* name".

## Polygons

A **polygon** is a closed shape with straight sides. Common ones:

- $3$ sides: triangle
- $4$ sides: quadrilateral
- $5$ sides: pentagon
- $6$ sides: hexagon
- $8$ sides: octagon
- $10$ sides: decagon

A **regular** polygon has all sides equal AND all angles equal. A regular hexagon (the shape of a bee's honeycomb cell) has six $120°$ interior angles. An **irregular** polygon does not satisfy both conditions; its sides or angles vary.

## Solids (3D shapes)

The named 3D shapes you must recognise:

- **Cube**: 6 square faces, 12 equal edges, 8 vertices.
- **Cuboid**: 6 rectangular faces. Like a brick or a juice carton.
- **Prism**: 2 identical parallel ends (the cross-section), joined by rectangles. Triangular prism, hexagonal prism, etc. A cylinder is a prism with a circular cross-section.
- **Cylinder**: 2 circular ends, 1 curved side.
- **Pyramid**: a polygon base with triangular sides meeting at a single apex (top point). A square-based pyramid has 4 triangular faces.
- **Cone**: circular base, curved side, single apex.
- **Sphere**: a perfect ball. Every surface point is the same distance from the centre.
- **Hemisphere**: half of a sphere (cut along a great circle).
- **Frustum**: a cone or pyramid with the top part chopped off parallel to the base. Like a paper cup or a bucket.

The pieces of a solid:

- **Face**: a flat (or curved) surface bounding the solid.
- **Edge**: where two faces meet.
- **Vertex**: a corner where edges meet (plural: vertices).
- **Surface**: the entire outside of the solid (including curved parts).

## Circles

The parts of a circle that Cambridge expects you to name on sight:

- **Centre**: the middle point.
- **Radius**: a line from the centre to the circumference. Plural: radii.
- **Diameter**: a chord that passes through the centre. $\text{diameter} = 2 \times \text{radius}$.
- **Circumference**: the curved boundary of the circle (the "perimeter").
- **Semicircle**: half a circle, cut along a diameter.
- **Chord**: a straight line joining two points on the circumference (does not have to pass through the centre).
- **Tangent**: a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point. The tangent is always perpendicular to the radius at that point.
- **Arc**: a piece of the circumference between two points. The shorter one is the **minor arc**; the longer one is the **major arc**.
- **Sector**: a "pizza slice" of the circle, bounded by two radii and an arc. Minor sector and major sector follow the same idea as arcs.
- **Segment**: the region between a chord and one of the arcs. Minor segment is the smaller piece; major segment is the larger piece.

A sector is bounded by two straight edges (radii) and one curved edge (arc). A segment is bounded by one straight edge (chord) and one curved edge (arc). Easy to mix up : the rule is *sect*or has straight si*des*; *segm*ent has *one* straight side.

## Worked example

In the diagram below, $O$ is the centre of a circle. $A$ and $B$ are points on the circumference. $AB$ is a straight line passing through $O$. $T$ is a point outside the circle, and the line $TA$ touches the circle only at $A$.

(a) Name the line $AB$.

(b) Name the line $TA$.

(c) State the size of $\angle OAT$, and give a geometrical reason.

**Step 1.** $AB$ joins two points on the circumference and passes through the centre.

**Step 2.** A chord through the centre is called a **diameter**.

**Step 3.** $TA$ touches the circle at exactly one point.

**Step 4.** A line that touches a circle at exactly one point is called a **tangent**.

**Step 5.** $OA$ is a radius (centre to circumference). $TA$ is a tangent at $A$.

**Step 6.** The tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact, so $\angle OAT = 90°$.

**Answer (a):** $AB$ is a **diameter**. **[B1]**

**Answer (b):** $TA$ is a **tangent**. **[B1]**

**Answer (c):** $\angle OAT = 90°$, because the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact. **[B1 for $90°$; B1 for the reason]**

Full marks $= 4$.

## Common mistakes

- Calling every straight line through a circle a "diameter". A diameter must pass through the centre. Otherwise it's a chord.
- Mixing up sector and segment. Two straight sides $\to$ sector. One straight side (the chord) $\to$ segment.
- Saying a triangle is "isosceles" when only the angles match. The definition is about equal **sides** (which forces equal angles), but Cambridge writes "isosceles triangle" expecting you to spot the side property.
- Writing a bearing as $25°$ instead of $025°$. Always three digits.
- Calling a square a "rectangle" when the question wants the most specific name. A square is *also* a rectangle, but if every side is equal, write "square".
- Confusing reflex with obtuse. Reflex is bigger than $180°$, not just bigger than $90°$.

## Quick summary

- Parallel = same direction, never meet. Perpendicular = meet at $90°$.
- Bearings: clockwise from North, three digits, $000°$ to $360°$.
- Angles: acute $< 90°$, right $= 90°$, obtuse between $90°$ and $180°$, straight $= 180°$, reflex $> 180°$.
- Triangle by sides: equilateral / isosceles / scalene. By angles: right / acute / obtuse.
- Quadrilateral hierarchy: square $\subset$ rectangle / rhombus $\subset$ parallelogram. Trapezium has one pair of parallel sides; kite has none.
- Similar = same angles, sides in ratio. Congruent = identical.
- Circle parts: centre, radius, diameter, circumference, semicircle, chord, tangent, major / minor arc, sector (two radii + arc), segment (chord + arc).
- Solid parts: face (flat or curved), edge (where faces meet), vertex (corner).
