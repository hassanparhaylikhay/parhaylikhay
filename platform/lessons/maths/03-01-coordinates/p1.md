## Why this matters

When you describe where to meet a friend in Lahore, you say something like "the corner of Mall Road and Davis Road". Two streets, one named for each direction, and the spot they cross is the meeting point.

A **Cartesian coordinate system** does the same for any point on a flat sheet - except instead of street names it uses two perpendicular number lines (the **axes**), and instead of an address it gives you a pair of numbers like $(3, 5)$.

Cambridge tests this on Paper 1 in the gentlest form ("plot this point", "name this point") and on Paper 2 inside almost every coordinate-geometry question. Get the basics rock-solid here and the rest of unit 3 sits on top of it.

## The two axes

Two perpendicular number lines:

- The **x-axis**: horizontal. Values increase to the right.
- The **y-axis**: vertical. Values increase upward.

They cross at the **origin**, which has coordinates $(0, 0)$. The origin is the "zero" of the whole grid - every coordinate is measured from there.

## What a coordinate means

A coordinate is a pair $(x, y)$:

- The **first number** $x$ is how far right (positive) or left (negative) of the y-axis the point sits.
- The **second number** $y$ is how far up (positive) or down (negative) from the x-axis the point sits.

**Order matters.** $(3, 5)$ and $(5, 3)$ are different points. A trick to remember: "Along the corridor, then up the stairs" - x first (sideways), then y (vertical).

## The four quadrants

- **Q1** (top-right): both positive.
- **Q2** (top-left): x negative, y positive.
- **Q3** (bottom-left): both negative.
- **Q4** (bottom-right): x positive, y negative.

Points exactly on an axis don't belong to any quadrant - they sit on the boundary. The y-axis is the line $x = 0$; the x-axis is the line $y = 0$.

## Plotting a point

To plot $(3, -2)$:

1. Start at the origin.
2. Move $3$ units to the **right** (positive x).
3. Move $2$ units **down** (negative y).
4. Mark the point with a small cross.

Always start at the origin, do x first, then y. Reverse the order and you'll plot the wrong point.

## Worked example

State the coordinates of each point and the quadrant it sits in:

(a) Point $A$ is $4$ units right of the y-axis and $3$ units above the x-axis.

(b) Point $B$ is $5$ units left of the y-axis and on the x-axis.

(c) Point $C$ is on the y-axis, $2$ units below the origin.

**Step 1 (a).** Right of y-axis → $x > 0$. Above x-axis → $y > 0$. So $A = (4, 3)$, in Q1.

**Step 2 (b).** Left of y-axis → $x < 0$. On x-axis → $y = 0$. So $B = (-5, 0)$ - on the negative x-axis (no quadrant).

**Step 3 (c).** On y-axis → $x = 0$. Below origin → $y < 0$. So $C = (0, -2)$ - on the negative y-axis (no quadrant).

**Answer (a):** $A = (4, 3)$, Q1. **[B1]**

**Answer (b):** $B = (-5, 0)$, on the x-axis. **[B1]**

**Answer (c):** $C = (0, -2)$, on the y-axis. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping x and y.** $(3, 5)$ ≠ $(5, 3)$. Always x first, then y.
- **Counting from the wrong axis.** The x-coordinate measures distance from the **y-axis**, not from where you currently are.
- **Forgetting the sign.** $-3$ is to the LEFT of the y-axis. $-2$ is BELOW the x-axis.
- **Calling axis points "in a quadrant".** A point with $x = 0$ or $y = 0$ sits on an axis - no quadrant.

## Quick summary

- Coordinates are written $(x, y)$ - x first.
- Origin is $(0, 0)$, the meeting point of the axes.
- Four quadrants, counter-clockwise from top-right: Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4.
- The y-axis is $x = 0$; the x-axis is $y = 0$.
