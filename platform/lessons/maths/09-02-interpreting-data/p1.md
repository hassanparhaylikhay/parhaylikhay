## Why this matters

After organising data (9.1), the next skill is READING it back: a frequency table, a bar chart, a pie chart, a pictogram. Exam questions ask things like "how many students scored more than 50?", "which day had the fewest visitors?", or "what fraction prefer chai?". Worth $1$ to $3$ marks each, almost always part of a longer multi-part question.

The skill is patient reading. Tables and charts are designed to be READ, your job is to look carefully, identify the right cell or bar, and write the number.

## Read a frequency table

Find the row whose label matches the question. Read the frequency. Done.

For "TOTAL FREQUENCY of A AND B" type questions: add the two row frequencies.

For "FRACTION that chose X" type questions: divide that row's frequency by the total.

For "MOST POPULAR" type questions: find the row with the largest frequency.

## Read a bar chart

Each bar's HEIGHT (or LENGTH for horizontal bars) gives the value for that category. To read a height:

1. Find the top of the bar.
2. Trace across to the y-axis with a ruler (or your finger).
3. Read the value on the axis.

If the top of the bar is BETWEEN two gridlines, estimate. The mark scheme accepts answers within roughly half a gridline.

## Read a pie chart

Slice angles are PROPORTIONAL to frequencies. So:

$$\dfrac{\text{angle of slice}}{360°} = \dfrac{\text{frequency of category}}{\text{total frequency}}$$

If a question gives you the angle and asks for the count:

$$\text{count} = \dfrac{\text{angle}}{360°} \times \text{total}$$

If a question gives you the count and asks for the angle:

$$\text{angle} = \dfrac{\text{count}}{\text{total}} \times 360°$$

This is the most common pie-chart question on the paper.

## Read a pictogram

A pictogram uses pictures as units. The KEY (legend) tells you how many items each picture stands for. Half-pictures count as half.

Always check the key first. A common trap: the key says "🚗 = $10$ cars", students sometimes count pictures and forget to multiply.

## Worked example: pie chart angle

A survey of $60$ students about favourite sport. The cricket sector contains $25$ students. Find the angle of the cricket sector.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=stats-pie-angle" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Cricket fraction.** $\dfrac{25}{60} = \dfrac{5}{12}$.

**Step 2: Apply to $360°$.**

$$\text{angle} = \dfrac{25}{60} \times 360° = \dfrac{5}{12} \times 360° = 150°$$

**Answer:** Cricket angle $= \boxed{150°}$. **[M1 for $\tfrac{25}{60} \times 360$; A1 for $150°$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

The same idea reversed: if you are given the angle ($150°$) and the total ($60$), the cricket count is $\tfrac{150}{360} \times 60 = 25$.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading a bar wrong** by not checking the y-axis scale (e.g., gridlines every 2 vs every 5).
- **Forgetting the pictogram key**. Each picture might be 4, 10, or 100 items.
- **Reading a pie slice as a percentage** when the question asks for an angle, or vice versa.
- **Skipping the total** when the question gives you only fractions.

## Quick summary

- **Frequency table**: read off the row.
- **Bar chart**: bar HEIGHT = value for that category.
- **Pie chart**: angle $/$ $360°$ = count $/$ total.
- **Pictogram**: check the KEY, multiply count by key value.
- Reading off charts gives estimates, not exact values.
