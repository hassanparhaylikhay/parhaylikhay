## Why this matters

The exam often gives you TWO data sets and asks you to compare them. "Compare boys'' and girls'' test scores", "compare January and July temperatures", "compare two factories'' production times". Worth $2$ to $3$ marks. The marker is looking for TWO things in your comparison:

1. **A statement about the centre** (mean or median, whichever is given).
2. **A statement about the spread** (range or IQR).

Missing one of these loses half the marks. This page sets up the standard comparison structure.

## The standard comparison structure

Use this template for every comparison answer:

> "$[\text{Group A}]$ has a [HIGHER / LOWER] [mean / median] than $[\text{Group B}]$, so on average $[\text{interpret}]$.
>
> $[\text{Group A}]$ has a [LARGER / SMALLER] [range / IQR] than $[\text{Group B}]$, so $[\text{interpret}]$."

The first sentence is the CENTRE comparison. The second is the SPREAD comparison. Both are needed.

**Example**: "Boys had a higher median test score ($72$ vs $65$), so on average boys did better. Boys had a larger range ($30$ vs $18$), so their scores were more spread out."

## Interpretations that score marks

When you say "Group A has a higher mean", you need to follow up with WHAT THAT MEANS in context. Some standard phrasings:

- "Group A typically [scored higher / took longer / had more / was older]"
- "Group A had more variability"
- "Group B was more consistent" (smaller spread)
- "Group A''s scores were more concentrated around the centre" (smaller spread)

Avoid weak statements like "Group A is better" or "Group B is the winner", these are not statistical comparisons.

## Same average, different spread (the classic trap)

Two classes both averaged $72\%$ on a test. But Class A had scores ranging from $50$ to $90$, while Class B had scores from $65$ to $80$. Are the classes the same?

The MEAN is the same, yes. But the SPREAD is very different. Class A had some students well below and well above average. Class B was much more uniform.

This is the exam''s favourite trap: "the means are equal, so the data is the same", NO. Always check spread before declaring two data sets identical.

## Worked example: compare two classes

Class A test scores ($n=10$): $52, 58, 64, 70, 70, 74, 78, 82, 86, 90$. Mean = $72.4$. Range = $38$.

Class B test scores ($n=10$): $66, 68, 70, 70, 72, 72, 74, 76, 78, 80$. Mean = $72.6$. Range = $14$.

Compare the two classes.

**Step 1: Centres.** Class A mean $= 72.4$, Class B mean $= 72.6$. The means are very close, Class B''s is slightly higher.

**Step 2: Spreads.** Class A range $= 38$, Class B range $= 14$. Class A''s range is much larger.

**Step 3: Comparison statement.**

The two classes have very similar mean scores ($72.4$ vs $72.6$), so on average they performed equally well. Class A has a LARGER range than Class B ($38$ vs $14$), meaning Class A''s scores were more spread out. Class B''s scores were more consistent around the mean.

**Answer:** Both classes have similar averages; Class A is more spread out, Class B is more consistent. **[B1 for the comparison of means; B1 for the comparison of ranges, with interpretation]**

Full marks $= 2$.

The marker wants TWO comparisons. One mark each. Both need a sentence of interpretation tying the number to the real-world meaning.

## When to use median + IQR instead of mean + range

If a data set has OUTLIERS (extreme values far from the rest), the mean and range get badly distorted. The MEDIAN and IQR are more robust, they ignore the extremes.

The exam usually gives you whichever measure is appropriate. If the question gives medians, compare medians; if it gives means, compare means. Always match the centre and spread measures the question hands you.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing only the centre** (or only the spread). Half the marks gone.
- **Making non-statistical comparisons** ("Class A is better"). Re-state in numbers.
- **Forgetting the context.** Always follow up "higher mean" with WHAT IT MEANS for the situation.
- **Using "always"** instead of "typically" or "on average". A higher mean doesn''t mean every person in Group A beats every person in Group B.
- **Mixing measures** (comparing Group A''s mean to Group B''s median). Use the SAME measure for both groups.

## Quick summary

- A comparison needs **TWO statements**: one about centre, one about spread.
- Phrasing: "Group A has higher/lower [mean] than Group B, so [interpret]; Group A has larger/smaller [range] than Group B, so [interpret]."
- "Typically" and "on average" are the right hedges, not "always".
- Match the measures: median ↔ IQR; mean ↔ range.
