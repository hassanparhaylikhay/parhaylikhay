## Why this matters

Pythagoras' theorem is powerful but has one big limitation: **it only relates the three sides**. There is no slot in $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$ for an angle. So if a Cambridge question gives you one side and one angle in a right triangle and asks for another side, Pythagoras alone is stuck.

Trigonometry is the tool that closes that gap. Each angle has its own "ratio fingerprint" that links the sides. Once you can use sin, cos, and tan, every right triangle becomes solvable from any two pieces of information (as long as at least one is a side).

If you know **two sides** of a right triangle, Pythagoras finds the third. You have already done this in [Unit 6.1](../06-01-pythagoras/p1).

There is no way to plug $35°$ into $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$. The formula simply doesn't see angles.

## Why this gap exists

Pythagoras gives you the **shape constraint** (one of the angles is $90°$, so the three sides obey $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$). But knowing the right angle alone doesn't pin down the triangle's proportions. Two right triangles can have the same hypotenuse but very different leg lengths, depending on the size of the acute angle.

Trigonometry fills that gap. For each acute angle $\theta$, there are three fixed numbers, $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$, $\tan\theta$, that connect the sides. Plug in any one side plus $\theta$ and you can compute the others.

## Preview: the new tool

In [p2](p2) you will see the three trig ratios in action. The core insight is that **the ratio of any two sides of a right triangle depends ONLY on the angle**, not on the size of the triangle. Two triangles with the same shape (i.e. the same acute angle) have identical sin, cos, and tan values.

Once you can use these ratios, the previously stuck problem above solves in two steps:

$$\sin 35° = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{10} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{opp} = 10\sin 35° \approx 5.74$$

$$\cos 35° = \dfrac{\text{adj}}{10} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{adj} = 10\cos 35° \approx 8.19$$

That is what trigonometry buys you. The next part shows what the three ratios actually are and why they only depend on the angle.

## What you can already deduce in any right triangle

Even before you touch sin, cos, tan, two facts hold for every right triangle:

- The two acute angles **add to $90°$**. (Because all three angles add to $180°$.)
- The hypotenuse is always the **longest side**, and is always opposite the right angle.

So if Cambridge tells you one acute angle, you immediately know the other. And if it tells you two of the three sides, Pythagoras gives you the third. These two facts plus the three trig ratios cover every right-triangle question on the paper.

## Common mistakes

- **Trying to use Pythagoras when only one side is known.** Pythagoras needs two sides.
- **Confusing the right angle with the angle in a question.** The right angle is fixed at $90°$. The angle Cambridge gives you (often called $\theta$) is one of the two acute angles.
- **Forgetting that "the angle" depends on which vertex you stand at.** opp/adj swap if you switch the angle to the other acute vertex. Always pick which angle you're working from before naming sides.

## Quick summary

- Pythagoras relates **sides only**: it needs two sides to find a third.
- When the question gives you **one side and one angle**, Pythagoras alone is stuck.
- Trigonometry (sin, cos, tan) fills the gap by tying each angle to a fixed ratio of sides.
- The two acute angles in a right triangle always sum to $90°$ (they are complementary).
- The hypotenuse is always opposite the right angle, and always the longest side.
