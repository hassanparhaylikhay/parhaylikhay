## Why this matters

Some functions don't behave like polynomials. A reciprocal graph $y = a/x$ shoots off to infinity near $x = 0$ and flattens to zero far away from the origin. A square-root graph rises slowly. Cambridge tests these "non-polynomial" curves explicitly : the syllabus lists $y = ax^n$ for $n = -2, -1, -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, 1, 2, 3$ as required shapes.

The skill: recognise the shape from the equation, recognise the equation from the shape, and read off values without confusing one with another.

This is the one you'll see most often. Two branches : one in the first quadrant, one in the third (when $a > 0$).

**Key feature: two asymptotes.**

- As $x \to \infty$, $y \to 0$ : the curve approaches but never touches the $x$-axis (horizontal asymptote $y = 0$).
- As $x \to 0$, $y \to \pm \infty$ : the curve shoots up (or down) without touching the $y$-axis (vertical asymptote $x = 0$).

The curve never crosses either axis. That's the defining behaviour of $y = a/x$.

If $a < 0$, the branches sit in the second and fourth quadrants instead. Same shape, mirror-flipped.

Different from $y = a/x$:

- Both branches are **above** the $x$-axis (when $a > 0$) : because $x^2$ is always positive.
- Symmetric about the $y$-axis.
- Same asymptotes ($x = 0$ and $y = 0$), but the curve drops to zero faster than $a/x$ does.

Where you see this in real Cambridge problems: gravitational/light intensity scenarios, or any "inversely proportional to the square" situation (linked to topic 2.8).

- Only defined for $x \geq 0$ (you can't take a real square root of a negative number).
- Starts at the origin, rises but flattens out.

Cambridge sometimes pairs this with $y = ax^2$ in the same plot to compare growth rates.

## Composite functions: sums of $ax^n$

The syllabus also lists "sums of no more than three" $ax^n$ terms. Examples:

- $y = x^2 + \dfrac{4}{x}$ : a parabola plus a reciprocal
- $y = x^3 + x - 4$ : a cubic plus a line minus a constant
- $y = 2x + \dfrac{3}{x^2}$ : a line plus an inverse-square term

You won't usually be asked to sketch these from memory : you'll be given a printed grid and a table of values to complete (back to p1's process). The skill is just substitution and careful arithmetic.

## Worked example

A function is given by $y = \dfrac{6}{x}$. It is plotted on the grid for $1 \leq x \leq 6$.

(a) Complete the table.

| $x$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ | $6$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $y$ | $6$ | $3$ | ? | $1.5$ | $1.2$ | ? |

(b) State the value of $y$ as $x$ increases without limit.

**Step 1 (a): missing values.**

$$x = 3: y = \dfrac{6}{3} = 2$$

$$x = 6: y = \dfrac{6}{6} = 1$$

**Step 2 (b).** As $x \to \infty$, $y = 6/x \to 0$. The curve approaches the $x$-axis as a horizontal asymptote.

**Answer:** **(a)** $y = 2$ and $y = 1$. **(b)** $y$ approaches $0$. **[B1 for each correct $y$; B1 for the asymptote behaviour]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing $y = a/x$ with branches in Q1 and Q2 (incorrect). Both branches are "diagonal opposites" : Q1 and Q3.
- Forgetting that $y = a/x^2$ stays positive on both sides : students sometimes draw it like $y = a/x$ by mistake.
- Plotting $\sqrt{x}$ for negative $x$ : it's undefined. The curve only exists on the right.
- Mixing up "asymptote" and "intercept". Asymptote = curve approaches but never touches. Intercept = curve actually crosses the axis. $y = a/x$ has asymptotes, not intercepts.

## Quick summary

- $y = a/x$: hyperbola with two branches in Q1 and Q3 (for $a > 0$).
- $y = a/x^2$: two branches both above the $x$-axis (for $a > 0$).
- $y = a\sqrt{x}$: only $x \geq 0$, starts at origin, rises slowly.
- All three have $x = 0$ and/or $y = 0$ as asymptotes.
- Sums of these terms are plotted via tables of values, same as in p1.
