## Why this matters

A polynomial like $y = x^3 - 3x + 2$ doesn't draw itself. You can't plot a cubic from a quick sketch the way you can plot a straight line : the curve bends, has multiple turning points, and your eye will lie about where it goes.

Cambridge tests this directly on Paper 2 every series. You'll be given a function, a table of $x$-values with one or two $y$-values blank, and a printed grid. You compute the missing $y$-values, plot every $(x, y)$ point, and join them with a single smooth curve. The mark scheme awards points for accuracy and for the word "smooth" — so this is mechanical and worth doing carefully.

## The three-step process

For ANY function $y = f(x)$ you're asked to plot, follow exactly this:

1. **Build a table of values.** For each $x$ in the given range, compute $y = f(x)$.
2. **Plot every point on the grid.** Read the scale carefully : 1 square is rarely 1 unit on both axes.
3. **Join the points with a single smooth curve.** No ruler. No "connect-the-dots" straight segments.

That's it. The maths is in step 1 (substitution); the marks are in steps 2 and 3 (accuracy + smoothness).

## Building a table of values

You'll typically be given a table like this with one or two blanks:

| $x$ | $-3$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $y$ | $-16$ | ? | $4$ | $2$ | $0$ | $4$ | $20$ |

For the missing value at $x = -2$ in $y = x^3 - 3x + 2$:

$$y = (-2)^3 - 3(-2) + 2 = -8 + 6 + 2 = 0$$

Always show the substitution clearly : Cambridge awards a B1 for each correct missing value, and the working is your safety net if you slip on the arithmetic.

## Plotting points: read the scale

Before you plot a single point, find the scale on each axis. Common Cambridge scales:

- $x$-axis: 2 small squares = 1 unit (so $x = 3$ is 6 squares right of $0$)
- $y$-axis: 1 small square = 2 units (so $y = -16$ is 8 squares below $0$)

Plot each $(x, y)$ pair as a small cross or dot at the correct position. Cambridge's mark scheme grants:

- **B1** for 2 correct points
- **B2** for 4 correct points
- **B3** for 6 correct points

Off-by-one-square mistakes lose marks fast. Count squares twice for every point.

## Drawing the smooth curve

Now connect your points with **one smooth curve** : like running your pencil along a soft track, not zig-zagging between dots.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: never use a ruler.** A cubic does not pass through its plotted points in straight segments. The mark scheme's wording is literally "smooth curve" : a connect-the-dots polyline costs you a B mark even if every point is plotted correctly.

</div>

If two adjacent points sit far apart, plot an extra value to fill in the gap (Cambridge sometimes asks you to do this implicitly when the curve bends sharply).

## Worked example

The function $y = x^3 - 4x^2 + 12$ is given. The table below has one blank.

| $x$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $y$ | $7$ | ? | $9$ | $4$ | $3$ | $12$ |

(a) Complete the table.

(b) State, with a reason, the shape you expect the curve to have between $x = 0$ and $x = 4$.

**Step 1 (a): missing value at $x = 0$.**

$$y = 0^3 - 4(0)^2 + 12 = 12$$

**Step 2 (b).** $y$ starts at $12$, drops to $4$ at $x = 2$, drops further to $3$ at $x = 3$, then rises again to $12$ at $x = 4$. The curve has a **minimum somewhere between $x = 2$ and $x = 3$**.

**Answer:** **(a)** $y = 12$. **(b)** A smooth curve with a minimum between $x = 2$ and $x = 3$. **[B1 for table; B1 for shape with reason]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Mis-reading the $y$-axis scale (e.g. plotting $y = -3$ at $-3$ squares when the scale is $0.5$ per square).
- Treating $-x^2$ as $(-x)^2$ when computing $y$. Brackets matter: $-2^2 = -4$, but $(-2)^2 = +4$.
- Connecting points with a ruler instead of a smooth curve.
- Forgetting to plot every point in the table : a missing point loses you a B mark even if your curve looks right.

## Quick summary

- Table of values first: substitute every $x$ into $y = f(x)$.
- Read the scale before plotting.
- Plot every point as a small cross or dot.
- Join with **one smooth curve** : never use a ruler on a cubic, parabola, or curve.
