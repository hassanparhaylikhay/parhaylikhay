## Why this matters

The whole point of plotting a curve isn't just to look at it : Cambridge wants you to **use** the curve to solve equations.

If you've drawn $y = x^3 - 3x + 2$ on a grid, you've effectively solved an infinite family of cubic equations at once. Want to know where $x^3 - 3x + 2 = 0$? Look at where the curve crosses the $x$-axis. Want $x^3 - 3x + 2 = 5$? Draw $y = 5$ and read the intersection.

This is THE highest-value technique in this sub-topic, because Cambridge gives you up to **4 marks** for getting it right (1 for the line, 2 for the solutions, 1 for accuracy).

## Three things you might be asked

### 1. Find the roots: solve $f(x) = 0$

The **roots** of $f(x) = 0$ are the $x$-coordinates where the curve $y = f(x)$ crosses the $x$-axis. No extra drawing needed : just read off where $y = 0$.

**Example.** Curve crosses at $x = -1.5$, $x = 0.5$, $x = 2.0$. Roots: $x = -1.5$, $0.5$, $2.0$.

### 2. Solve $f(x) = k$ (a constant)

Draw the **horizontal line** $y = k$ on the same grid. Read the $x$-coordinates where the curve meets that line.

**Example.** To solve $x^3 - 3x + 2 = 4$, draw $y = 4$. Read intersections.

### 3. Solve a different equation by drawing a clever line

This is the marks-rich one. You're given a curve $y = f(x)$ already drawn, and a different equation to solve. You **rearrange** the new equation to match the form $f(x) = (\text{some line})$, draw that line, and read the intersections.

The recipe is short, and it's the only one Cambridge tests on this:

## The rearrangement technique

Suppose the curve drawn is $y = x^3 - 3x + 2$, and you're asked to solve

$$x^3 - 7x + 1 = 0$$

**Step 1: rearrange so the left-hand side matches the curve expression.**

The curve has $x^3 - 3x + 2$ on the LHS. The new equation has $x^3 - 7x + 1$ on the LHS. Their difference is

$$(x^3 - 3x + 2) - (x^3 - 7x + 1) = 4x + 1.$$

So if I add $4x + 1$ to both sides of $x^3 - 7x + 1 = 0$, the LHS becomes the curve expression:

$$x^3 - 3x + 2 = 4x + 1$$

**Step 2: identify the line.** The right-hand side is the equation of a straight line: $y = 4x + 1$.

**Step 3: draw $y = 4x + 1$ on the same grid as the cubic.** Read the $x$-coordinates of the intersection points : these are exactly the solutions of $x^3 - 7x + 1 = 0$, because at each intersection both sides of $x^3 - 3x + 2 = 4x + 1$ hold.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Shortcut for finding the line.** Take the new equation $g(x) = 0$ and the curve $y = f(x)$. Add $f(x) - g_\text{LHS}(x)$ to both sides of $g(x) = 0$. The left-hand side becomes $f(x)$; whatever's on the right is your line.

For our example: $f(x) - g_\text{LHS}(x) = (x^3 - 3x + 2) - (x^3 - 7x + 1) = 4x + 1$. Add it to both sides → $f(x) = 4x + 1$.

</div>

## Worked example

The graph of $y = x^3 - 3x + 2$ has been drawn for $-3 \leq x \leq 3$.

(a) Use the graph to solve $x^3 - 3x + 2 = 0$.

(b) Use the graph to solve $x^3 - 7x + 1 = 0$ by drawing a suitable line.

**Step 1 (a).** Roots of the cubic are where the curve crosses the $x$-axis. Reading from the graph the curve touches the axis at $x = 1$ (a repeated root, where the curve dips to the axis and bounces back) and crosses at $x = -2$.

So $x = -2$ and $x = 1$ (repeated). Cambridge accepts values within their tolerance band (often $\pm 0.1$).

**Step 2 (b): find the line.**

Take the new equation $x^3 - 7x + 1 = 0$ and add $4x + 1$ to both sides so the LHS becomes the curve expression:

$$x^3 - 3x + 2 = 4x + 1$$

The line to draw is $y = 4x + 1$.

**Step 3 (b): read the intersections.** Reading from the printed graph where the line meets the cubic: $x \approx -2.7$, $x \approx 0.2$, $x \approx 2.5$. Cambridge accepts ranges (e.g. $-2.8$ to $-2.6$, $0.1$ to $0.2$, $2.4$ to $2.7$).

**Answer:** **(a)** $x = -2$ and $x = 1$ (repeated). **(b)** $x \approx -2.7$, $0.2$, $2.5$. **[M2 for line $y = 4x + 1$ correctly drawn; A2 for all 3 intersections]**

Full marks $= M2A2 = 4$.

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the wrong line. Do the algebra carefully : subtract the new equation from the curve equation and let the cubic terms cancel.
- Reading intersections to the wrong precision. Cambridge accepts a tolerance band; read the values to the nearest $0.1$ where the gridlines allow.
- Reporting only $1$ or $2$ solutions when there are $3$. Cubics can have up to three real roots : check the whole curve, not just the centre.
- Drawing $y = 4x + 1$ as a "short line" through just the visible intersection. The mark scheme awards M2 for a **fully ruled** line across the grid; M1 for a partial sketch.

## Quick summary

- Roots of $f(x) = 0$ live where $y = f(x)$ crosses the $x$-axis : no extra drawing.
- $f(x) = k$: draw the horizontal line $y = k$ and read intersections.
- $f(x) = g(x)$ via rearrangement: isolate $f(x)$, identify the resulting line, draw it.
- Use a ruler. Read all intersections. Report each $x$-coordinate.
