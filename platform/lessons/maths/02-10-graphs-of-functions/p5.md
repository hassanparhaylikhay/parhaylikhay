## Why this matters

A line has the same gradient everywhere : pick any two points and rise/run. A curve doesn't. The gradient changes from point to point. So how do you ask "how steep is the curve **right here**"?

You replace the curve, locally, with the straight line that just kisses it at that point : the **tangent**. Then you compute the tangent's gradient using rise/run, the same way you'd do for any straight line.

Cambridge tests this on Paper 2 inside graph questions. M1 for drawing the tangent at the named point; A1 for the gradient value within a tolerance.

## What a tangent is

A **tangent** at a point $P$ on a curve is a straight line that touches the curve at $P$ and has the same direction as the curve does there.

- The tangent meets the curve at $P$.
- Just to either side of $P$, the curve drifts away from the tangent, but very slowly.
- The tangent's gradient = the curve's gradient AT $P$.

Different points on the same curve have different tangents.

## The four-step process

For "estimate the gradient of $y = f(x)$ at $x = a$":

1. **Locate the point** $(a, f(a))$ on the printed curve.
2. **Draw a straight line** that just touches the curve at that point : try to make the line "kiss" the curve, equally close on both sides of the touch point.
3. **Pick two points on the tangent** (NOT on the curve) : usually one on each side of the touch point, as far apart as possible while still on the printed grid.
4. **Compute rise/run** between those two points using the grid scale.

The result is your gradient.

## Why a bigger triangle is better

Rise and run are read off the printed grid. Each reading has an error of about half a small square. If you use a small rise/run triangle, those errors dominate. A bigger triangle dilutes the error.

So always pick two tangent points that are as far apart as the printed graph allows. A $5 \times 10$ rise/run triangle is far better than a $1 \times 2$ one.

## Worked example

A graph of $y = x^2$ is drawn. Estimate the gradient at $x = 2$.

**Step 1: locate the point.** At $x = 2$, $y = 4$. So the touch point is $(2, 4)$.

**Step 2: draw a tangent.** Place a ruler so it touches the curve at $(2, 4)$ and has the same slope direction. (For $y = x^2$ at $x = 2$, the curve is rising steeply.)

**Step 3: pick two points on the tangent.** Suppose your tangent passes through $(0, -4)$ and $(4, 12)$ (both on the printed grid).

**Step 4: compute gradient.**

$$\text{gradient} = \dfrac{12 - (-4)}{4 - 0} = \dfrac{16}{4} = 4$$

**Answer:** Gradient $\approx 4$. **[M1 for tangent drawn; A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 2$.

(For reference, the true gradient of $y = x^2$ at $x = 2$ is $4$ exactly. Tangent estimation gives the right answer here when the line is drawn carefully.)

## Where this comes up

- **Distance-time graphs**: gradient at a point = instantaneous speed.
- **Speed-time graphs**: gradient at a point = instantaneous acceleration.
- **Population vs time**: gradient at a point = growth rate at that moment.

Anywhere you see a curve and you're asked "how fast is something changing **right now**", the answer is "draw a tangent and find its gradient".

## Common mistakes

- **Using points on the curve** instead of on the tangent. The tangent is a separate straight line. Use only the line you drew, not the curve.
- **Drawing a chord, not a tangent.** A chord cuts the curve at two points; a tangent only touches at one. Cambridge does NOT accept a chord-based estimate as the gradient at a point.
- **Picking too-close tangent points.** Errors from reading the grid dominate the answer. Pick widely-spaced points on your tangent.
- **Forgetting the scale.** If the $y$-axis is "$1$ square = $2$ units", a rise of $6$ squares is a rise of $12$ units : double-check.

## Quick summary

- Gradient at a point on a curve = gradient of the **tangent** at that point.
- Process: locate point → draw tangent → pick two well-spaced points on the tangent → rise/run.
- Bigger rise/run triangle = more accurate estimate.
- On d-t and v-t graphs, tangent gradient = instantaneous speed / acceleration.
