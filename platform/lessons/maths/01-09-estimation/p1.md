## Why this matters

Cambridge wraps rounding into almost every calculator question with phrases like "give your answer to 2 decimal places" or "correct to 3 significant figures". Mark schemes are strict: the right value rounded the wrong way costs the accuracy mark. Get the mechanical rule sharp and you stop dropping easy marks.

## What "decimal places" means

A **decimal place** (d.p.) is one of the digits to the **<span style="color:#fff067">right</span>** of the decimal point.

So $3.472$ has $3$ decimal places. $0.04$ has $2$ decimal places (the leading $0$ doesn't count, and the $0$ between the dot and the $4$ DOES count).

## The rounding rule

To round to **<span style="color:#00abfa">$k$ decimal places</span>**:

1. **Look at the digit immediately AFTER the $k$th decimal place.** This is the **decider**.
2. **Decider $\ge 5$ $\to$ round UP** (add $1$ to the $k$th digit).
3. **Decider $\lt 5$ $\to$ round DOWN** (leave the $k$th digit alone).
4. **Drop everything after the $k$th place.**

That's the entire rule. The decider is one digit and one digit only.

## Carrying when you round up

If rounding up turns a $9$ into a $10$, you carry to the previous digit (and possibly further). Treat it like normal arithmetic.

**Example.** Round $5.96$ to 1 d.p.

- Decider is $6$ ($\ge 5$), so round up the $9$.
- $9 + 1 = 10$, write $0$ and carry $1$ to the integer column.
- $5 + 1 = 6$. Final answer: $6.0$.

**Example.** Round $0.0998$ to 2 d.p.

- Decider is $9$ ($\ge 5$), so round up the $9$ in the 2nd d.p.
- Carries up: $0.0998 \to 0.10$.

Note: keep the trailing zero. "Round to 2 d.p." means **show 2 digits after the decimal point**, even if one of them is a zero.

## A subtle case: only ONE decider matters

Each rounding decision uses **only the digit immediately after** the cut, not the whole tail.

**Example.** $12.4949$ to 2 d.p.

- Cut: $12.49\,|\,49$.
- Decider is $4$ (the next digit), NOT $49$ or $4949$.
- $4 \lt 5$, so round DOWN. Answer is $12.49$.

The chain of nines after the $4$ is a red herring. Many students mistakenly do "successive rounding" ($12.4949 \to 12.495 \to 12.50$), which is **wrong**. Always look at the SINGLE digit after the cut and decide once.

**Trap: don't round in stages.** $12.4949$ to 2 d.p. is $12.49$, not $12.50$. Cambridge will set this trap. Look ONLY at the digit immediately after the cut, never at multiple digits or chained rounding.

## Worked example

A length is measured as $1.0476$ metres. Round to:

(i) $1$ d.p.
(ii) $2$ d.p.
(iii) $3$ d.p.

**Part (i).** Cut at 1 d.p.: $1.0\,|\,476$. Decider $= 4$ ($\lt 5$). Round down: $\boxed{1.0}$ m.

**Part (ii).** Cut at 2 d.p.: $1.04\,|\,76$. Decider $= 7$ ($\ge 5$). Round up: $\boxed{1.05}$ m.

**Part (iii).** Cut at 3 d.p.: $1.047\,|\,6$. Decider $= 6$ ($\ge 5$). Round up: $\boxed{1.048}$ m. **[B1 each]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Looking at more than one decider digit. Only the digit IMMEDIATELY after the cut counts.
- Successive rounding. $12.4949 \to 12.495 \to 12.50$ is wrong; it's $12.49$.
- Dropping the trailing zero. "$5.96$ to 1 d.p." is $6.0$, not $6$. Cambridge marks the form.
- Mishandling the carry. $4.99$ to 1 d.p.: decider $9$ rounds up the $9$ to $10$, which carries up. Answer is $5.0$, not $4.10$.

## Quick summary

- $k$ d.p. means show $k$ digits after the decimal point.
- The **decider** is the single digit immediately after the cut.
- Decider $\ge 5$ <b style="color:#0fee89">round up</b>; decider $\lt 5$ <b style="color:#ff4670">round down</b>.
- If a 9 rounds up, carry like normal arithmetic.
- Keep trailing zeros so the answer shows the requested number of decimal places.
