## Why this matters

Cambridge often asks for an answer "correct to 3 significant figures", especially in trigonometry and calculator questions. Significant figures (s.f.) measure **how precise** a number is, regardless of where the decimal sits. They behave differently from decimal places, particularly for very big or very small numbers, and students lose marks every year confusing the two.

## What counts as a significant figure

The **first significant figure** is the **<span style="color:#fff067">first non-zero digit</span>** when reading the number left to right. After that, every digit counts: zeros included.

| Number | s.f. | Digits |
|---|---|---|
| $4\,827$ | $4$ | $4, 8, 2, 7$ |
| $0.004\,371$ | $4$ | $4, 3, 7, 1$ (leading zeros don't count) |
| $5.07$ | $3$ | $5, 0, 7$ (zero between non-zeros DOES count) |
| $4.20$ | $3$ | $4, 2, 0$ (trailing zero AFTER decimal IS significant) |

The rules in summary:

- **Leading zeros** (in numbers less than 1, like $0.0072$) are NOT significant. They just place the decimal.
- **Zeros between non-zero digits** are always significant.
- **Trailing zeros after a decimal** (like the $0$ in $4.20$) are significant: they signal precision.
- **Trailing zeros in a whole number** are ambiguous unless context clarifies. Cambridge usually counts them when the question is unambiguous.

## Rounding to a given number of s.f.

The method is the same as decimal places: find the cut, look at the next digit, round up or down.

1. **Count** $k$ significant digits, starting at the first non-zero digit.
2. **Look at the digit just after the $k$th s.f.**
3. **$5$ or more $\to$ round up; $4$ or less $\to$ round down.**
4. **Fill in zeros** for the dropped digits if the number needs them to stay the right size.

That last step is what makes s.f. different from d.p. for big numbers: you have to keep the number roughly as big as it was.

## Small-number example

For numbers less than 1, the leading zeros are placeholders. Round only the significant part.

**Example.** $0.004\,371$ to 2 s.f.

- First non-zero digit: $4$. So 1st s.f. is $4$, 2nd s.f. is $3$.
- Cut: $0.0043 \,|\, 71$. The next digit is $7$ ($\ge 5$). Round up.
- Answer: $0.0044$. (Note: small numbers don't need extra zeros at the end.)

## Rounding to the nearest 10, 100, 1000

Same idea, framed differently. "Nearest 1000" means: keep the thousands digit, decide using the hundreds digit.

**Example.** $5\,764$ to the nearest thousand.

- Thousands digit: $5$.
- Look at the hundreds digit: $7$ ($\ge 5$). Round up.
- Result: $6\,000$.

Same pattern for any place value: nearest ten, look at the ones digit; nearest hundred, look at the tens; and so on.

## Worked example

A car's distance from home is $14\,963$ m. Round to:

(i) the nearest hundred metres
(ii) $3$ significant figures
(iii) $2$ significant figures

**Part (i).** Hundreds place: $9$. Look at the tens digit: $6$ ($\ge 5$). Round up the $9$, which carries: $14\,963 \to 15\,000$. **Answer: $\boxed{15\,000}$ m.**

**Part (ii).** Cut at 3 s.f.: $149\,|\,63$. The next digit is $6$ ($\ge 5$). Round up: $149 \to 150$. Add zeros to keep the size: $150 \to 15\,000$. **Answer: $\boxed{15\,000}$ m.**

(In this case (i) and (ii) gave the same answer: rounding to nearest 100 happens to equal 3 s.f. for a 5-digit number.)

**Part (iii).** Cut at 2 s.f.: $14\,|\,963$. The next digit is $9$ ($\ge 5$). Round up: $14 \to 15$. Add zeros: $15\,000$. **Answer: $\boxed{15\,000}$ m. [B1 each]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Counting leading zeros as significant. $0.005\,2$ has $2$ s.f., not $4$.
- Treating $0$ between digits as not significant. $5.07$ has $3$ s.f.; the middle $0$ counts.
- Forgetting to add zeros for big numbers. Rounding $4\,827$ to 2 s.f. gives $4\,800$, not $48$.
- Using d.p. rules when asked for s.f. (or vice versa). Always re-read the question.

## Quick summary

- 1st s.f. is the first <b style="color:#fff067">non-zero</b> digit. Counting moves right from there.
- Look at the next digit: $5$ or more $\to$ round up; $4$ or less $\to$ round down.
- Add zeros so the rounded number stays roughly the right size (only matters for numbers $\ge 10$).
- s.f. $\neq$ d.p. They count from different reference points.
