## Why this matters

Cambridge directly rewards estimation in two ways: (i) "estimate" questions where you round to $1$ s.f. and compute, and (ii) every other question, where a rough estimate catches calculator errors before you write the answer down. A good estimate takes 5 seconds and can save 2 marks.

## The estimation routine

The standard approach for an "estimate" question:

1. **Round every number to $1$ significant figure.**
2. **Compute the simplified expression** by hand.
3. **State the estimate**, often using the $\approx$ symbol.

The point is to make the arithmetic doable in your head while staying in the right ballpark.

## How to round when estimating

Round each value to **$1$ s.f.** unless the question says otherwise. The fastest way:

- For numbers $\ge 1$: keep the first non-zero digit, fill the rest with zeros. $41.3 \to 40$, $9.79 \to 10$, $382 \to 400$.
- For numbers $\lt 1$: keep the first non-zero digit. $0.765 \to 0.8$, $0.0421 \to 0.04$, $0.31 \to 0.3$.
- For squares, cubes, roots: round inside first, then take the power. $\sqrt{398}$: round $398 \to 400$, then $\sqrt{400} = 20$.

For roots especially, **pick the round number that has a nice root**. $\sqrt{83}$: round to $81$ (a perfect square), so $\sqrt{83} \approx 9$.

## Sanity-checking other answers

Even when the question doesn't say "estimate", you should estimate **before writing the final answer**. It catches:

- **Decimal-point errors**: a calculator slip might give $7.97$ when the answer should be $\approx 80$.
- **Sign errors**: an estimate of $+15$ catches a written answer of $-15$.
- **Wrong operation**: if you accidentally divided when you meant to multiply, the estimate will be wildly off.

**Example.** A student computes $19.7 \times 4.1$ and writes $7.97$. Estimate: $20 \times 4 = 80$. The written answer is off by a factor of $10$. Re-check.

## Estimating with squares, cubes, roots

If the expression contains $a^2$, $a^3$, or $\sqrt{a}$:

1. Round $a$ to $1$ s.f. first.
2. Apply the power or root.

**Example.** $\sqrt{83}$. Round $83 \to 81$ (a perfect square). $\sqrt{81} = 9$. So $\sqrt{83} \approx 9$.

**Example.** $4.7^3$. Round $4.7 \to 5$. $5^3 = 125$. So $4.7^3 \approx 125$.

For perfect-square or perfect-cube targets, deliberately round to the **closest perfect power** even if that's not the nearest s.f. round. The reason: it makes the arithmetic exact and the estimate sharp.

**Trap: round each value SEPARATELY, not in stages.** For $41.3 \div (9.79 \times 0.765)$, don't compute exactly first then round; round each input to 1 s.f., THEN compute. The whole point is to keep arithmetic simple. Also: don't over-round small values to $0$. $0.765 \to 0.8$, not $0$.

## Worked example

Estimate $\dfrac{(7.94)^2}{0.213}$ correct to 1 s.f.

**Step 1: Round each value to 1 s.f.**

- $7.94 \to 8$
- $0.213 \to 0.2$

**Step 2: Apply the power.**

$$8^2 = 64$$

**Step 3: Compute the quotient.**

$$\dfrac{64}{0.2} = \dfrac{640}{2} = 320$$

**Step 4: Round to 1 s.f.**

$$320 \approx 300$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{\approx 300}$. **[M1 for rounding each value; A1 for the final estimate]**

Full marks $= 2$.

(The true value, by calculator, is $\approx 296$, so the estimate is excellent.)

## Common mistakes

- Rounding $0.765 \to 0$ or $0.0421 \to 0$. Keep the first non-zero digit; small numbers get $1$-s.f. values like $0.8$ or $0.04$.
- Rounding only some values. The rule is: round EVERY number in the expression.
- Computing exactly first and then rounding. That's not estimation; it's just rounding the answer.
- Forgetting to apply the operation correctly. After rounding, redo the BIDMAS order on the simpler numbers.

## Quick summary

- Estimate $=$ round each value to <b style="color:#fff067">1 s.f.</b>, then compute exactly with the rounded numbers.
- Use $\approx$ for the final answer to signal it's an estimate.
- For roots and powers, choose round values that match a perfect power.
- Estimate before writing any final calculator answer to catch decimal/sign/operation errors.
