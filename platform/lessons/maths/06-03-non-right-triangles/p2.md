## What the sine rule says

For any triangle (right-angled or not), each side divided by the sine of its opposite angle gives the same number:

$$\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}$$

That single equation is the sine rule. Three equal ratios. Pick any two of them, set them equal, and you have an equation linking two sides and two angles.

## When to use it

The sine rule needs **a complete pair** (a side with its opposite angle) plus **one more piece** of information.

If both your knowns and your unknown sit on opposite (angle, side) pairs, the sine rule fits. If they don't, the cosine rule is the right tool.

## Worked example: find a side

A triangle has $\angle A = 55°$, $\angle B = 80°$, and side $a = 8$ cm. Find side $b$.

<iframe src="/widgets/trig-non-right-step-explorer.html?preset=sine-rule-find-side" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Pair $a$ with $A$ and $b$ with $B$, then solve:

$$\dfrac{8}{\sin 55°} = \dfrac{b}{\sin 80°} \;\Rightarrow\; b = \dfrac{8\sin 80°}{\sin 55°} \approx 9.62\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $\dfrac{8}{\sin 55°} = \dfrac{b}{\sin 80°}$ or equivalent; A1 for $b = 9.62$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: find an angle

A triangle has side $a = 7$, side $b = 10$, and $\angle A = 35°$. Find $\angle B$ (assume $B$ is acute).

<iframe src="/widgets/trig-non-right-step-explorer.html?preset=sine-rule-find-angle" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Pair $a$ with $A$ and $b$ with $B$:

$$\dfrac{7}{\sin 35°} = \dfrac{10}{\sin B}$$

$$\sin B = \dfrac{10\sin 35°}{7} \approx 0.819$$

$$B = \sin^{-1}(0.819) \approx 55.0°$$

**[M1 for sine-rule setup; M1 for $\sin B = 10\sin 35°/7$; A1 for $B = 55.0°$]**

Full marks $= 3$.

The note on the last step matters: $\sin B = 0.819$ also has a solution at $B = 180° - 55° = 125°$, because sine gives the same value at any pair of angles that adds to $180°$. Cambridge will sometimes tell you which case applies (or the diagram makes it obvious). The next part covers this **ambiguous case** in detail.

The method mark (M1) comes from writing the sine-rule equation correctly. Always write it before substituting, even if you can do the arithmetic in your head.

## Common mistakes

- **Mismatching the pairs.** $a$ with $A$, $b$ with $B$, $c$ with $C$. Always.
- **Setting up upside-down.** $\sin A$ goes under $a$, not over. The denominator of each ratio is the sine.
- **Stopping at $\sin B$.** The question wants $B$, not $\sin B$. Apply $\sin^{-1}$.
- **Forgetting the obtuse alternative.** If the question allows an obtuse triangle, $B$ could be $180° - \sin^{-1}(\text{value})$.
- **Calculator in radians.** Sine of an angle in degrees should look right (between $-1$ and $1$, peaking at $90°$).

## Quick summary

- The sine rule: $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}$. Provided on the formula sheet.
- Use it when you have a complete (side, opposite-angle) pair plus one more piece.
- To find a side: pair-it-up, rearrange, multiply.
- To find an angle: pair-it-up, rearrange, then $\sin^{-1}$.
- Watch the second solution: if $B$ could be obtuse, the diagram disambiguates.
