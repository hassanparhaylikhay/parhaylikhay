## Why this case is "ambiguous"

When you know two sides and the angle **opposite one of them**, the sine rule sometimes gives **two different triangles** that fit the same numbers. Both are valid, and you need to know which one the question wants.

Why does this happen? Because the sine function gives the **same value at angles that add to $180°$**: $\sin 30° = \sin 150°$, $\sin 60° = \sin 120°$, and so on. So when the sine rule produces something like $\sin B = 0.857$, the equation has two solutions in the range that matters: $B \approx 59°$ and $B \approx 121°$. If both keep the angle sum below $180°$, both make real triangles.

## When does it happen?

Not every setup with two sides and a non-included angle is ambiguous. Three scenarios:

    <div>· \(\sin B < 1\) <b>and</b> the obtuse alternative still keeps \(A + B < 180°\) → <span style="color:#00abfa">two triangles</span> (this is the ambiguous case).</div>
    <div>· \(\sin B < 1\) <b>but</b> the obtuse alternative pushes \(A + B \geq 180°\) → <span style="color:#00abfa">one triangle</span> (acute solution only).</div>

The shortcut: if your known angle $A$ is already large (e.g. $A = 110°$), the obtuse alternative for $B$ would push the angle sum past $180°$, so only the acute solution is valid. If $A$ is small (e.g. $A = 30°$), both alternatives may fit.

## Worked example: identifying both solutions

A triangle has $a = 6$, $b = 8$, $\angle A = 40°$. Find all possible values of $\angle B$.

<iframe src="/widgets/trig-non-right-step-explorer.html?preset=ambiguous-case" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Set up the sine rule:

$$\dfrac{6}{\sin 40°} = \dfrac{8}{\sin B}$$

$$\sin B = \dfrac{8\sin 40°}{6} \approx 0.857$$

The first solution:

$$B_{1} = \sin^{-1}(0.857) \approx 59.0°$$

The second:

$$B_{2} = 180° - 59.0° = 121.0°$$

Check both keep the angle sum below $180°$:

- With $B_{1} = 59°$: $A + B_{1} = 40° + 59° = 99° < 180°$. ✓ Valid.
- With $B_{2} = 121°$: $A + B_{2} = 40° + 121° = 161° < 180°$. ✓ Valid.

So both triangles are possible.

## How to decide which one the question wants

The question gives you the answer in one of three ways:

1. **The diagram.** A printed triangle with $B$ visibly acute or obtuse points to one solution. This is the most common case in Cambridge.
2. **An explicit phrase** like "given that $\angle B$ is acute" or "the obtuse triangle".
3. **A geometric constraint** like "$B$ must be less than $C$". Use the constraint to pick the case.

If the question gives no diagram and no clue, then state both solutions and explain why both are valid. Cambridge will accept this if the question genuinely is ambiguous.

## Common mistakes

- **Reporting only the calculator's value.** $\sin^{-1}$ on a calculator gives one answer (in $[-90°, 90°]$). The obtuse alternative needs an extra step: $180°$ minus that value.
- **Checking the obtuse one, finding it pushes past $180°$, but reporting it anyway.** Always verify the angle sum stays below $180°$.
- **Assuming the question is ambiguous when the diagram resolves it.** If a diagram is given, follow it.
- **Confusing the two "two-sides-and-an-angle" cases.** Two sides with the angle **between** them is the cosine rule case, not the sine rule. The ambiguous case is two sides with the angle **opposite** one of them.

## Quick summary

- Two sides and the angle opposite one of them is the only setup where two triangles can fit.
- The sine rule gives $\sin B$; both $\sin^{-1}(\text{value})$ and $180° - \sin^{-1}(\text{value})$ are candidates.
- Check each: a candidate is valid only if the angle sum stays below $180°$.
- The diagram, an explicit hint, or context usually disambiguates.
- If the known angle is **between** the two known sides instead, that is the cosine-rule case, not this one.

The cosine rule (next part) handles the cases where the sine rule cannot — when the angle is between the two known sides, or when all three sides are known.
