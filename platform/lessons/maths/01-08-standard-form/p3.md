## Why this matters

On Paper 2 (calculator) Cambridge often gives values in standard form and expects you to compute with them, sometimes pages of working. On Paper 1 (no calculator) the questions are simpler but you still need to know which rules of indices apply when standard-form numbers are multiplied, divided, or added. This part stitches together what you learned in 1.7 with the standard-form notation from p1 and p2.

## Multiplying and dividing: use the index rules

When you multiply $(\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n_1}}) \times (\textcolor{#fff067}{A_2} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n_2}})$, multiply the **<span style="color:#fff067">A</span> parts** and **<span style="color:#0fee89">add the indices</span>** (Rule 1 from 1.7):

$$(\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n_1}}) \times (\textcolor{#fff067}{A_2} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n_2}}) \;=\; (\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1 \times A_2}) \times 10^{\textcolor{#0fee89}{n_1 + n_2}}$$

For division, divide the $A$ parts and **<span style="color:#ff4670">subtract</span>** the indices:

$$\dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n_1}}}{\textcolor{#fff067}{A_2} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n_2}}} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1}}{\textcolor{#fff067}{A_2}} \times 10^{\textcolor{#ff4670}{n_1 - n_2}}$$

## Adjusting if $A$ goes outside the range

After multiplying or dividing, the new $A$ might land outside the $1 \le A \lt 10$ range. If so, **fix it by shifting one place** and adjusting the index:

- $A \ge 10$: divide $A$ by 10, add 1 to the index.
- $A \lt 1$: multiply $A$ by 10, subtract 1 from the index.

**Example.** $(5 \times 10^3) \times (4 \times 10^6)$:

$$5 \times 4 = 20, \qquad 3 + 6 = 9 \quad\implies\quad 20 \times 10^9$$

But $A = 20$ is too big. Shift: $20 \times 10^9 = 2.0 \times 10^{10}$. **Final answer: $2 \times 10^{10}$.**

**Example.** $(2 \times 10^5) \times (3 \times 10^{-9})$:

$$2 \times 3 = 6, \qquad 5 + (-9) = -4 \quad\implies\quad 6 \times 10^{-4}$$

That one's fine: $A = 6$ is already between 1 and 10.

## Adding and subtracting: same power first

Adding or subtracting standard-form numbers is **only easy when the indices are equal**. If they are, just add or subtract the $A$ parts and keep the index:

$$(\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}) + (\textcolor{#fff067}{A_2} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}) \;=\; (\textcolor{#fff067}{A_1 + A_2}) \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$$

If the indices differ, **rewrite one of them so both have the same power of 10** before adding.

**Example.** $(3 \times 10^4) + (2 \times 10^3)$.

The smaller-index term is $2 \times 10^3 = 0.2 \times 10^4$ (shift $A$ down by 10, add 1 to the index).

$$(3 \times 10^4) + (0.2 \times 10^4) \;=\; 3.2 \times 10^4$$

This is exactly the same as adding the ordinary numbers $30\,000 + 2\,000 = 32\,000$, just with the powers of $10$ pulled out.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: you cannot add standard-form numbers by adding indices.** $(3 \times 10^4) + (2 \times 10^5) \neq 5 \times 10^9$. The "add indices" rule is only for MULTIPLICATION. For addition you must convert to a common power first, OR convert both to ordinary numbers and add directly.

</div>

## Worked example

Light travels at $3 \times 10^8$ metres per second. How far does it travel in $5\,000$ seconds? Give your answer in standard form.

**Step 1: Write both quantities in standard form.** $5\,000 = 5 \times 10^3$.

**Step 2: Multiply the $A$s.** $3 \times 5 = 15$.

**Step 3: Add the indices.** $8 + 3 = 11$.

**Step 4: Combine.** $15 \times 10^{11}$ metres.

**Step 5: Adjust to standard form.** $A = 15 \gt 10$, so shift: $15 \times 10^{11} = 1.5 \times 10^{12}$.

**Answer:** $\boxed{1.5 \times 10^{12}}$ metres. **[M1 for the multiplication; M1 for the index addition; A1 for the standard-form adjustment]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Adding indices when adding numbers. $(3 \times 10^4) + (5 \times 10^4) = 8 \times 10^4$, NOT $8 \times 10^8$.
- Forgetting to convert when indices differ for addition. $(3 \times 10^4) + (2 \times 10^3)$ is NOT $5 \times 10^7$.
- Leaving $A$ outside $1 \le A \lt 10$. After computing $5 \times 4 \times 10^9 = 20 \times 10^9$, shift to $2 \times 10^{10}$.
- For division, subtracting in the wrong order. $\tfrac{8 \times 10^7}{4 \times 10^3}$ has index $7 - 3 = 4$, not $3 - 7 = -4$.

## Quick summary

- <b style="color:#0fee89">Multiply</b>: $A_1 \times A_2$, add indices.
- <b style="color:#ff4670">Divide</b>: $A_1 / A_2$, subtract indices.
- <b style="color:#00abfa">Add/subtract</b>: only with the same power of 10, or convert one term first.
- After any operation, check that $1 \le A \lt 10$ and shift if not.
