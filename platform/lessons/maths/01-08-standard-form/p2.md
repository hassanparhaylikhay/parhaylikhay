## Why this matters

The reverse direction is just as tested. Cambridge will give you a number in standard form and ask you to write it as an ordinary decimal, or compare two numbers in standard form to see which is bigger. Both are mechanical once you trust the rule about which way the decimal moves.

## The reverse direction

Going from $\textcolor{#fff067}{A} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$ back to ordinary form just means **moving the decimal point**:

- If $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ is **positive**, move the decimal **<span style="color:#0fee89">right</span>** $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ places (the number gets bigger).
- If $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ is **negative**, move the decimal **<span style="color:#ff4670">left</span>** $|\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}|$ places (the number gets smaller).

Notice this is the **opposite** of the rule from p1. Going to standard form, a big number moves the decimal LEFT; coming back, the decimal moves RIGHT. The direction reverses but the count is the same.

## Comparing two numbers in standard form

To compare $A_1 \times 10^{n_1}$ and $A_2 \times 10^{n_2}$:

1. **Compare the indices first.** The bigger index wins, regardless of $A$.
2. **If the indices are equal**, compare the $A$ values directly.

The direction of "bigger" with negative indices catches students out. A LESS negative index is a bigger number: $10^{-2} = 0.01$ is much bigger than $10^{-5} = 0.000\,01$.

| Comparison | Bigger | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $3 \times 10^5$ vs $9 \times 10^4$ | $3 \times 10^5$ | bigger index $5 \gt 4$ |
| $4 \times 10^{-2}$ vs $5 \times 10^{-3}$ | $4 \times 10^{-2}$ | bigger index $-2 \gt -3$ |
| $7.1 \times 10^3$ vs $7.2 \times 10^3$ | $7.2 \times 10^3$ | same index, bigger $A$ |

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: a more negative index is SMALLER.** $10^{-7}$ is tinier than $10^{-3}$ because more decimal places mean a smaller number. Don't read the bigger absolute value of the index as "bigger number". Read the index as the actual integer it is: $-7 \lt -3$, so $10^{-7} \lt 10^{-3}$.

</div>

## Worked example

Write the diameter of a hydrogen atom, approximately $1.06 \times 10^{-10}$ metres, as an ordinary decimal.

**Step 1: Identify the index.** $n = -10$, so move the decimal LEFT $10$ places.

**Step 2: Move and pad.** Starting from $1.06$, the decimal moves $10$ places left. That puts $9$ zeros between the new decimal and the $1$.

$$1.06 \times 10^{-10} \;=\; 0.000\,000\,000\,106$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.000\,000\,000\,106}$ metres. **[B1 for the conversion]**

Full marks $= 1$.

## Common mistakes

- Moving the decimal the wrong direction. Positive index $\to$ right (bigger). Negative index $\to$ left (smaller).
- Counting digits instead of decimal places. For $3.6 \times 10^4$ the decimal moves $4$ places, NOT $4$ digits past the last existing digit.
- Forgetting the extra zeros. $3.6$ already has $1$ digit after the decimal; moving 4 places right needs $3$ extra zeros: $36\,000$.
- Reading "more negative index" as "bigger number". $10^{-2} \gt 10^{-5}$, not the other way around.

## Quick summary

- Positive index $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$: move decimal $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ places <b style="color:#0fee89">right</b>.
- Negative index: move decimal $|\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}|$ places <b style="color:#ff4670">left</b>.
- Add zeros to fill any gap left by the move.
- Comparing: bigger index $\to$ bigger number. Same index $\to$ compare $A$.
