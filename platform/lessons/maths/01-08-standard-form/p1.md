## Why this matters

Standard form is how scientists write very big or very small numbers without painful zeros. The mass of an electron, the speed of light, the population of Pakistan: all of these get a single clean form. Cambridge tests it directly (Paper 1 and 2) and indirectly (any calculator question may want the answer "in standard form"). Get the rules sharp now and you stop dropping easy marks.

## What standard form looks like

A number is in **standard form** when it is written as

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{A} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$$

where:

- **<span style="color:#fff067">A</span>** is a number with $1 \le \textcolor{#fff067}{A} \lt 10$ (one non-zero digit before the decimal point)
- **<span style="color:#00abfa">n</span>** is an integer (positive, zero, or negative)

That single rule is what most students forget. **<span style="color:#fff067">A</span> must sit between 1 and 10**, including 1 but not 10. So $4.3 \times 10^3$ is fine; $43 \times 10^2$ is the same number but NOT in standard form.

## Converting an ordinary number to standard form

The process is always the same, regardless of whether the number is huge or tiny:

1. Place the decimal point right after the **first non-zero digit**. That gives you $\textcolor{#fff067}{A}$.
2. Count how many places the decimal has **moved**.
3. The count is $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$. **Big number $\to$ positive $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$. Small number $\to$ negative $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$.**

The direction-rule, in one line: **move LEFT $\to$ <span style="color:#0fee89">positive</span> index. Move RIGHT $\to$ <span style="color:#ff4670">negative</span> index.**

## Quick examples (work through these)

| Ordinary | Move | Standard form |
|---|---|---|
| $52\,000$ | left 4 | $5.2 \times 10^4$ |
| $607$ | left 2 | $6.07 \times 10^2$ |
| $0.0008$ | right 4 | $8 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| $0.000\,12$ | right 4 | $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| $9$ | none | $9 \times 10^0$ |

The last row is a useful corner case. A single-digit number like $9$ is technically already in standard form: $A = 9$, $n = 0$. Cambridge usually won't bother with it, but it shows the system is consistent.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: $A$ must be at least $1$ and strictly less than $10$.** $0.43 \times 10^4$ is the same number as $4.3 \times 10^3$, but only the second is in standard form because $0.43 \lt 1$. Likewise $43 \times 10^2$ is wrong because $43 \gt 10$. If you write something like that on the paper, expect to lose the accuracy mark.

</div>

## Worked example

Write the population of Pakistan, approximately $241\,000\,000$, in standard form.

**Step 1: Place the decimal after the first non-zero digit.** That gives $A = 2.41$.

**Step 2: Count the moves.** The original decimal sits at the end (after the rightmost zero). Moving it to between the $2$ and $4$ takes $8$ places left.

**Step 3: Write it out.** $A = 2.41$, $n = 8$ (positive because we moved left).

**Answer:** $\boxed{2.41 \times 10^8}$. **[B1 for $2.41$ as the coefficient; B1 for $10^8$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving $A$ outside the $1 \le A \lt 10$ range. $43 \times 10^2$, $0.5 \times 10^6$, $12 \times 10^{-3}$ are all WRONG even though the numerical value is correct.
- Wrong sign on the index. Big number $\to$ positive; small number (less than 1) $\to$ negative. If you wrote $0.0072 = 7.2 \times 10^3$ you flipped the sign.
- Off-by-one on the count. Count the places the decimal MOVES, not the digits between original and new position. For $4300$ the decimal moves from after the last $0$ to between the $4$ and $3$, which is $3$ places.
- Forgetting standard form even applies to single-digit numbers. $9 = 9 \times 10^0$, not "doesn't apply".

## Quick summary

- Standard form: $\textcolor{#fff067}{A} \times 10^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$ with $1 \le \textcolor{#fff067}{A} \lt 10$ and $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ an integer.
- Place the decimal after the first non-zero digit.
- <b style="color:#0fee89">Move left</b> $\to$ positive $n$. <b style="color:#ff4670">Move right</b> $\to$ negative $n$.
- The count of decimal moves is $|n|$.
