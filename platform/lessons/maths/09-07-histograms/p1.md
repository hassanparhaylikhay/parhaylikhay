## Why this matters

A histogram is the right chart when:

1. The data is CONTINUOUS (heights, weights, times, etc.).
2. The classes have UNEQUAL WIDTHS.

A bar chart with unequal widths would mislead: a wide bar would look "bigger" than a narrow one even if both held the same frequency. The histogram fixes this by adjusting HEIGHTS via frequency density, so that AREA = frequency.

Histogram questions appear on most papers, worth $2$ to $4$ marks. The exam typically asks one of:

- Calculate frequency densities and complete a histogram.
- Read a frequency from a given histogram (area calculation).

## The key formula

$$\boxed{\text{frequency density} = \dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}}}$$

Used in both directions:

$$\text{frequency} = \text{frequency density} \times \text{class width}$$

Frequency density is what you put on the y-axis. Class width determines the bar''s width on the x-axis. The bar''s AREA then represents the frequency.

## Reading a histogram backwards

Given a finished histogram, the exam may ask: "How many values fall in class $30$-$50$?" Answer:

$$\text{frequency} = \text{frequency density} \times \text{class width} = 0.8 \times 20 = 16$$

The AREA of the bar IS the frequency. That''s the rule.

## Common mistakes

- **Plotting raw frequency** on the y-axis instead of frequency density.
- **Using equal-width bars** when classes are unequal.
- **Forgetting that AREA = frequency**.
- **Confusing histograms with bar charts**. Bar charts have gaps and equal widths; histograms have NO gaps and varying widths.

## Quick summary

- **Histogram** = chart for continuous data with possibly unequal class widths.
- **Frequency density** $= \dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}}$.
- Bar **AREA** $=$ frequency.
- No gaps between bars; widths reflect class widths; heights reflect density.
