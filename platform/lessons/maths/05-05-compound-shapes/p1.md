## Why this matters

A **compound shape** is built from simpler shapes (rectangles, triangles, parts of circles) joined together or with parts removed. Cambridge questions hide a 4-mark item behind these every paper.

Two big skills:

1. **Area**: split into simpler parts, add (or subtract) the areas.
2. **Perimeter**: trace the OUTSIDE only. Internal join lines do not count.

You already have all the simple-shape formulas from 5.2 and 5.3. Compound questions are about applying them in the right combination. **No new formula** in this part: just careful decomposition.

In this part:

- Splitting a compound shape into rectangles and triangles.
- The "subtract a piece" trick.
- Why perimeter ≠ sum of part-perimeters.
- Reading the question for what is asked.

## The split-or-subtract decision

Two ways to handle any compound shape.

**Split it** into simple shapes that fit together. Add the areas:

$$\text{total area} = A_1 + A_2 + A_3 + \ldots$$

**Subtract a missing piece**. Take a bounding rectangle and remove the gap:

$$\text{total area} = A_\text{big} - A_\text{cut}$$

Both methods give the same answer. Pick the one that involves less arithmetic. For an L-shape: subtracting one corner is usually faster than splitting into two rectangles. For a T-shape: splitting into the cap + stem is usually faster.

**Step 1: split into the cap and the stem.** Cap $= 10 \times 3 = 30$ cm². Stem $= 3 \times 6 = 18$ cm².

**Step 2: add.**

$$\text{area} = 30 + 18 = \boxed{48}\;\text{cm}^2$$

Full marks $= 2$. Notice the dashed line where the cap meets the stem; that internal line is NOT part of the outline.

## Perimeter: trace the outline

The perimeter of a compound shape is the **outside boundary** only. Add up every line you would walk along going once around the outside.

For the L-shape ($10 \times 8$ minus a $5 \times 4$ corner):

Going clockwise from the bottom-left:

- Bottom: $10$ across.
- Up the right side: $4$ (just the lower part).
- Inward: $5$ (this is part of the outline at the notch).
- Up: $4$ (the rest, going up to the top of the cut).
- Top: $5$ across (the part still left).
- Down the left: $8$.

Sum: $10 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 5 + 8 = 36$ cm.

A shortcut: this L-shape has the **same perimeter** as the original $10 \times 8$ rectangle. Why? Because the notch added two inner segments ($5 + 4$), but removed the same total from the original outline. **For axis-aligned rectangular notches, the perimeter equals the bounding rectangle's perimeter.**

That shortcut works only for "Manhattan" cuts (right-angled corners). Triangular or curved cuts change the perimeter.

## Worked example: rectangle with a triangle cut

A $12 \times 8$ rectangle has a right-angled triangle removed from one corner. The triangle has legs $4$ and $3$. Find the area and perimeter.

**Area.**

$$\text{rectangle} - \text{triangle} = 12 \times 8 - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 3 = 96 - 6 = \boxed{90}\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for the formula; A1 for $90$]**

**Perimeter.** Tracing the outline:

- Bottom: $12$.
- Right side: $8 - 3 = 5$ (we lost $3$ from the cut).
- The hypotenuse of the cut: $\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$.
- Top: $12 - 4 = 8$.
- Left side: $8$.

Sum: $12 + 5 + 5 + 8 + 8 = \boxed{38}\;\text{cm}$.

**[M2 for tracing the outline including the hypotenuse; A1 for $38$]**

The triangular cut **changes** the perimeter, because the diagonal is shorter than the two legs it replaces. The original rectangle had perimeter $40$; this compound shape has $38$.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the cut piece as if it adds area.** A "missing corner" reduces the area.
- **Including internal join lines in the perimeter.** The dashed line where two parts meet is INSIDE; it's not part of the outline.
- **Forgetting a side when tracing.** Walk slowly. Count each side once. Number them as you go (1, 2, 3, ...).
- **Using $\frac{1}{2} ab$ when the triangle is not right-angled.** The "$\frac{1}{2}$ base × height" formula needs the perpendicular height, not a slant.

## Quick summary

| Skill | Method |
|---|---|
| Area (split) | break into known shapes, add the areas |
| Area (subtract) | bounding shape − missing piece |
| Perimeter | trace the OUTSIDE only |
| Cut piece adds to perimeter? | yes for triangles/curves; **no** for rectangular notches |

The whole game in this part: **decompose, calculate, add or subtract, sanity-check**. The four formulas you already know (rectangle, triangle, trapezium, circle) cover almost every compound shape Cambridge has ever asked.
