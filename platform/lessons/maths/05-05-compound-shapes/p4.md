## Why this matters

Some 3D solids are formed by **removing** something from another solid. The two big patterns:

1. **Frustum**: a cone (or pyramid) with the tip cut off, parallel to the base. The cut leaves a flat disc at the top where the tip used to be.
2. **Drilled / hollow**: a solid block with a smaller solid removed (a cylinder drilled through a cuboid, a sphere with a smaller sphere hollowed out).

In both cases:

- Volume: **subtract** the removed piece from the original.
- Surface area: **carefully enumerate** the new exposed faces. The cut-piece is gone, but its old "opening" plus a fresh inner face usually remain.

In this part:

- Frustum: similar triangles to find the missing dimension, then subtract.
- Drilled solids: cylindrical hole through a cuboid, hole through a sphere.
- Hollow solids: an inner shape removed from the centre.

## Frustum geometry: similar triangles

When you slice a cone parallel to its base, the small cone (the "tip") is **similar** to the original. The ratio of any pair of corresponding lengths is the same.

If the large cone has radius $R$ and height $H$, and you cut at height $h$ from the apex, the small cone has:

- Height $h$ from the apex.
- Radius $r = R \times \tfrac{h}{H}$ (linear ratio).

The frustum is what's left below the cut: it has

- Bottom radius: $R$.
- Top radius: $r$.
- Height of frustum: $H - h$.

## Worked example: frustum volume by subtraction

<iframe src="/widgets/solid-step-explorer.html?preset=frustum-vol" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

A solid is formed by removing a small cone from the top of a large cone. They share the same apex pointing **down**. Large cone: height $21$ cm, diameter $18$ cm. Small cone: height $14$ cm. Show that the volume of the solid is $399\pi$ cm³.

**Step 1: similar triangles for the small cone's radius.** Large radius $= 18 \div 2 = 9$. Scale factor $= 14 / 21 = 2/3$. Small radius $= 9 \times \tfrac{2}{3} = 6$ cm.

**[M1 for the scale-factor reasoning]**

**Step 2: large cone volume.**

$$V_\text{large} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi(9)^2(21) = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi(81)(21) = 567\pi\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi(9)^2(21)$ seen]**

**Step 3: small cone volume.**

$$V_\text{small} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi(6)^2(14) = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi(36)(14) = 168\pi\;\text{cm}^3$$

**Step 4: subtract.**

$$V = 567\pi - 168\pi = \boxed{399\pi}\;\text{cm}^3 \;\checkmark$$

**[A2 for correct subtraction with no errors]**

Full marks $= 3$. This is **Cambridge Winter 2023 Paper 22, Q10(a)(i)**, and it's a "show that" question, so the working must be clean for full marks.

## Worked example: frustum surface area

<iframe src="/widgets/solid-step-explorer.html?preset=frustum-sa" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Continuing the same solid (large cone with tip removed, both pointing down): find the total surface area.

The exposed surfaces are:

- The **outer curved surface** of the large cone.
- The **inner curved surface** of the small cone (the cavity wall).
- The **annular ring** at the top (a flat ring between the small cone's circle and the large cone's circle, where the cut was made).

**Step 1: slant heights.**

$$l_\text{large} = \sqrt{H^2 + R^2} = \sqrt{21^2 + 9^2} = \sqrt{441 + 81} = \sqrt{522}$$

$$l_\text{small} = \sqrt{14^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{196 + 36} = \sqrt{232}$$

(Or by similar triangles, $l_\text{small} = \tfrac{2}{3} l_\text{large} = \tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{522}$.)

**[M2 for both slants; M1 for one]**

**Step 2: each surface.**

- Outer curved: $\pi R l_\text{large} = \pi(9)\sqrt{522}$
- Inner curved: $\pi r l_\text{small} = \pi(6)\sqrt{232}$
- Annular ring: $\pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(81 - 36) = 45\pi$

**[M2 for both curved-surface terms; M1 for the annular ring]**

**Step 3: sum.**

$$\text{SA} = 9\pi\sqrt{522} + 6\pi\sqrt{232} + 45\pi \approx 1074\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[A1 for value in range $1070$–$1075$]**

Full marks $= 6$. This is **Cambridge Winter 2023 Paper 22, Q10(a)(ii)**.

The mark scheme accepts a range because the slant heights involve irrational numbers and intermediate rounding produces tiny variations.

## Frustum the "ordinary" way (truncated cone, tip removed from the TOP)

The classic frustum is a bucket shape: large circle at the bottom, smaller circle at the top, curved side. To find the SA:

- Curved side: $\pi(R + r) \times l_\text{slant}$, where $l_\text{slant}$ is the slant of the FRUSTUM (not the original cone). For a frustum with bottom $R$, top $r$, vertical height $h$: $l_\text{slant} = \sqrt{(R-r)^2 + h^2}$.
- Bottom flat: $\pi R^2$.
- Top flat: $\pi r^2$.

$$\text{SA} = \pi(R + r) l_\text{slant} + \pi R^2 + \pi r^2$$

This formula is **not on the formula sheet**. The cleanest way is still the subtraction method (full cone − small cone). Use whichever sticks.

## Worked example: cuboid with cylindrical hole

<iframe src="/widgets/solid-step-explorer.html?preset=cuboid-drilled" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

A cuboid measures $10 \times 8 \times 6$ cm. A cylindrical hole of radius $2$ cm is drilled all the way through, parallel to the $6$ cm edge. Find the volume and total surface area.

**Volume.**

$$V = V_\text{cuboid} - V_\text{cylinder} = 10 \times 8 \times 6 - \pi(2)^2(6) = 480 - 24\pi\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for the cuboid; M1 for the cylinder; A1 for $480 - 24\pi$]**

**Surface area.** Now things get interesting. The drilled hole:

- **Removes** two circles (top and bottom of the cuboid where the hole goes in/out): $2 \times \pi(2)^2 = 8\pi$ cm².
- **Adds** the inner cylindrical wall (the curved surface of the hole): $2\pi(2)(6) = 24\pi$ cm².

The cuboid's original SA is $2(10\times 8 + 8\times 6 + 10\times 6) = 2(80 + 48 + 60) = 376$ cm².

Adjusted SA:

$$\text{SA} = 376 - 8\pi + 24\pi = 376 + 16\pi\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for cuboid SA; M1 for $-8\pi + 24\pi$ adjustment; A1 for $376 + 16\pi$]**

Full marks $= 6$.

The hole **REMOVES** less area than it ADDS (because the inner wall is bigger than the two end-circles), so the SA goes UP. That can feel counter-intuitive: drilling a hole adds to the boundary.

## Hollow solids

A "hollow sphere" or "hollow cylinder" has a smaller version of the same shape removed from inside. The outer shell stays.

For a hollow sphere (outer radius $R$, inner radius $r$):

- Volume: $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi(R^3 - r^3)$
- Outer surface: $4\pi R^2$
- Inner surface: $4\pi r^2$
- Total SA (assuming the inside is exposed somewhere, like through a small hole): $4\pi(R^2 + r^2)$

Cambridge questions on this are rare, but the principle (subtract the inner volume; add the inner surface) is the same as drilled solids.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting similar triangles.** When the tip is cut off a cone, the small cone's radius is NOT given directly: you have to compute it from the height ratio.
- **Counting both end-circles AND the inner wall on a drilled solid.** The drilled holes go IN at one face and OUT at another. Those two end-circles are removed from the original SA. The inner wall is added.
- **Using the original cone's slant for the inner surface of a frustum.** The inner surface uses the SMALL cone's slant ($l_\text{small}$), not the large one's.
- **Forgetting the annular ring.** A frustum (made by removing the tip) has a ring at the top where the cut happened. Don't forget it; it's its own SA term.

## Quick summary

| Operation | Volume | SA |
|---|---|---|
| Frustum (cone − tip) | $V_\text{large} - V_\text{small}$ | curved-large + curved-small + annular ring |
| Drilled solid (e.g. cuboid + cyl hole) | $V_\text{block} - V_\text{hole}$ | block-SA − 2 end-circles + inner curved |
| Hollow shell (e.g. hollow sphere) | $V_\text{outer} - V_\text{inner}$ | outer SA + inner SA |

The two-line recipe: **subtract for volume; add the new inner faces (and remove the old ones the hole used to occupy) for SA**. With the simple-shape formulas from 5.4 and the subtraction principle, every "part of a solid" question collapses to a few terms.
