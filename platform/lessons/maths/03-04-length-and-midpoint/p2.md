## Why this matters

The **midpoint** of a segment is the point exactly halfway between its two endpoints. Cambridge tests this directly ("find the midpoint") and uses it as a stepping stone in bigger problems (perpendicular bisectors, parallelograms, etc.).

It's the easiest formula in unit 3: just average the x-coordinates and average the y-coordinates.

## The formula

For $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$:

$$M \;=\; \left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\; \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$

**Average each coordinate.** That's it. The midpoint's x is halfway between $x_1$ and $x_2$; its y is halfway between $y_1$ and $y_2$.

## Worked example

Find the midpoint of $A(-2, 3)$ and $B(6, -5)$.

**Step 1: average the x's.**

$$\dfrac{-2 + 6}{2} = \dfrac{4}{2} = 2$$

**Step 2: average the y's.**

$$\dfrac{3 + (-5)}{2} = \dfrac{-2}{2} = -1$$

**Step 3: write as a coordinate.**

**Answer:** Midpoint is $(2, -1)$. **[M1 substitute; A1 simplify]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Reverse problem: find an endpoint given midpoint

Sometimes you know one endpoint and the midpoint; the other endpoint is the unknown.

**Trick:** if $M$ is the midpoint of $A$ and $B$, then $B = 2M - A$ (componentwise). This comes from rearranging the formula:

$$M_x = \dfrac{x_A + x_B}{2} \;\Rightarrow\; x_B = 2M_x - x_A$$

Same for y.

**Example.** $A = (1, 4)$, midpoint $M = (5, 2)$. Find $B$.

$$x_B = 2(5) - 1 = 9 \qquad y_B = 2(2) - 4 = 0$$

So $B = (9, 0)$.

## Common mistakes

- **Subtracting instead of adding.** Midpoint uses sum/2, not difference/2 (that's the gradient).
- **Forgetting the divide-by-2.** Adding the coordinates and stopping there gives you twice the midpoint.
- **Mixing up which is x and which is y.** Average all x-values together; average all y-values together. Don't cross them.
- **Computing the midpoint as the average of the two distances.** It's the average of the **coordinates**, point-for-point.

## Quick summary

- Midpoint of $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$ is $\left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$.
- Average the x's; average the y's.
- Reverse: given $M$ and one endpoint, the other is $2M - A$.
