## Why this matters

There are two ways to talk about the probability of an event.

The first way is **theoretical**. If a die is fair, every face has the same chance, so $P(\text{rolling a } 6) = \tfrac{1}{6}$. You did not have to roll any dice to know that. It comes from counting equally likely outcomes.

The second way is **experimental**. Throw a drawing pin into the air. Sometimes it lands point-up, sometimes point-down. The shape is not symmetric, so you cannot count "equally likely" sides. You have to actually drop the pin many times and count. If it lands point-up $61$ times out of $100$, you would say the probability is **about** $0.61$, based on that experiment. That is the **relative frequency**.

This page sets up the relationship between the two:

- Theoretical probability is the long-run target.
- Relative frequency is what you actually observe.
- The more trials you do, the closer the second number gets to the first.

This relationship matters because real exam questions look like: "Sara spins her spinner $80$ times and records the results in a table. Find the relative frequency of red." The skill is simply $\dfrac{\text{red count}}{80}$, simplified. That is a $1$- or $2$-mark question that you should never miss.

## The formula

The **relative frequency** of an outcome in an experiment is the fraction of trials where that outcome happened:

$$\text{relative frequency} = \dfrac{\text{number of times the outcome occurred}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$

This is the same shape as the probability formula, but the numbers come from actual results, not from counting equally likely outcomes.

## The law of large numbers

The widget at the top of this page shows a striking pattern. After a few spins, the observed relative frequency for each colour bounces around all over the place. After a hundred spins, the bounce gets smaller. After a thousand spins, the observed line is hugging the theoretical line.

This is the **law of large numbers**. It says: as the number of trials grows, the relative frequency settles down toward the theoretical probability. The longer you experiment, the more confident you can be about the underlying probability.

The opposite is also true and is the trap many students fall into: with only a few trials, the relative frequency can be far from the theoretical value. Five heads in a row from a fair coin is normal. Calling the coin biased after only five tosses would be wrong.

## When do you use relative frequency?

In two specific situations on the exam:

1. **The setup is not symmetric.** A drawing pin, a tossed bottle cap, a real-world event ("does this car start on the first try?"). There are no "equally likely outcomes" to count, so you have to gather data and report relative frequency.
2. **The question explicitly says "estimate the probability from these results"** even if the setup looks symmetric. Use the observed count divided by the trials.

When neither applies, use the theoretical probability. Counting equally likely outcomes is always more accurate than running an experiment, as long as you trust the setup is fair.

Find the relative frequency of red.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-relative-frequency" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:580px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Write the formula.**

$$\text{relative frequency} = \dfrac{\text{count of the outcome}}{\text{total trials}}$$

**Step 2: Substitute.** Count of red $= 12$; total trials $= 80$.

$$\text{rel freq}(\text{red}) = \dfrac{12}{80}$$

**Step 3: Simplify.** $\gcd(12, 80) = 4$, so divide both by $4$.

$$\dfrac{12}{80} = \dfrac{3}{20}$$

**Answer:** Relative frequency of red $= \boxed{\dfrac{3}{20}}$. **[B1 for $\tfrac{12}{80}$; B1 for $\tfrac{3}{20}$ simplified]**

Full marks $= 2$.

Decimal: $0.15$. The three relative frequencies are $\tfrac{12}{80}, \tfrac{35}{80}, \tfrac{33}{80}$, which add to $\tfrac{80}{80} = 1$. That is a useful sanity check: the relative frequencies for all the outcomes always add to $1$, since every trial contributed to exactly one count.

## Common mistakes

- **Dividing total by count.** $\dfrac{80}{12}$ is upside down. The formula is count over total, not total over count.
- **Not simplifying.** $\tfrac{12}{80}$ is correct but $\tfrac{3}{20}$ is the tidy form. Some markers take a mark off for unsimplified answers.
- **Treating relative frequency as exact probability.** It is an ESTIMATE, especially after only a few trials. The shorthand "$P(\text{red}) \approx \tfrac{3}{20}$" is more honest than "$P(\text{red}) = \tfrac{3}{20}$" when there are only $80$ trials.
- **Declaring bias from too few trials.** $7$ heads in $10$ tosses is normal random variation, not evidence of a biased coin. You need hundreds or thousands of trials to claim bias confidently.
- **Confusing relative frequency with frequency.** "Frequency" alone means the count ($12$). "Relative frequency" means the fraction ($\tfrac{12}{80} = \tfrac{3}{20}$). Re-read the question.

## Quick summary

- **Relative frequency** $= \dfrac{\text{count of the outcome}}{\text{total trials}}$. Same formula shape as probability, but the numbers come from actual results.
- **Theoretical probability** counts equally likely outcomes; **relative frequency** counts what actually happened.
- **Law of large numbers**: the more trials, the closer relative frequency gets to the true probability.
- All relative frequencies in an experiment add to $1$. Useful as a check.
- After only a few trials, the value can be far from the truth. Resist the urge to call bias.
