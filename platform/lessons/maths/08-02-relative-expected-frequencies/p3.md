## Spin many times, then decide

<iframe src="/widgets/spinner-experiment.html" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:740px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:6px auto 18px" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Set up a spinner with EQUAL sectors (say $2, 2, 2$ for red, blue, green). Spin $+10$. Are the three counts close to equal? Probably not exactly. Now spin $+1000$. They should be very close. **That tight matching after many trials is what "fair" looks like in practice**, even though no single experiment ever produces exactly equal counts.

Now make the spinner UNFAIR: try $5, 1, 0$ (only red and blue, with red much bigger). Spin $+1000$. The counts now should be roughly $833, 167, 0$. **That's biased**: red dominates because its sector is larger.

## Why this matters

Three vocabulary words you need to read and write in exam answers:

- **Fair**: each outcome is equally likely.
- **Biased**: the outcomes are NOT all equally likely.
- **Random**: each item has the same chance of being chosen (essentially the same as "fair").

The exam asks two flavours of question. First, "given an experiment's results, is the spinner fair?" This is a judgement call based on how close the observed counts are to what we would expect for a fair spinner. Second, "for a fair spinner, what is the probability of each outcome?" That is a simple theoretical-probability question: $\tfrac{1}{n}$ for each of $n$ equal sectors.

Both questions are worth $1$ to $2$ marks. The key skill is comparing observed to expected and writing a one-sentence justification.

## How to test for fairness from data

You cannot tell from a single trial. You cannot tell from a handful. With ENOUGH trials, the long-run pattern shows up clearly. Here is the recipe:

1. **Find the expected count** for each outcome, assuming the spinner is fair.
2. **Compare observed vs expected.** Are the differences small (within normal random variation) or large?
3. **Write your verdict**: "consistent with a fair spinner" or "appears biased".

The key insight is that small differences are NORMAL even from a fair spinner. With $100$ spins of a fair $4$-sector spinner, you might see counts like $22, 27, 25, 26$. Those are not all $25$ but they are CLOSE. That is fine.

If you saw $5, 10, 80, 5$ from $100$ spins, the spinner is clearly biased toward whatever sector got $80$. Even random variation cannot explain a gap that wide.

The marker is looking for a sensible comparison plus a one-line conclusion. You do not need formal statistics. A sentence like "the four counts are all close to the expected $25$, so the spinner appears fair" is enough.

## Worked example: judge a spinner

A spinner has four equal-sized sectors: red, blue, green, yellow. It is spun $100$ times. Results: red $18$, blue $26$, green $27$, yellow $29$. Do the results suggest the spinner is fair?

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p3-fair-or-biased" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:600px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Find the expected count if fair.** Four equal sectors mean $P(\text{each}) = \tfrac{1}{4}$. Expected count $= \tfrac{1}{4} \times 100 = 25$ for each colour.

**Step 2: Compare.** Observed: $18, 26, 27, 29$. Expected: $25, 25, 25, 25$.

- Red: $18$ vs $25$, off by $7$ (lower).
- Blue: $26$ vs $25$, off by $1$.
- Green: $27$ vs $25$, off by $2$.
- Yellow: $29$ vs $25$, off by $4$.

The biggest gap is for red, $7$ below the expected count. That is a noticeable difference but not extreme: red has come up about $18\%$ of the time when the fair value is $25\%$. Three colours are very close to $25$.

**Step 3: Write the verdict.** The observed counts are not far enough from $25$ to suggest the spinner is biased. With only $100$ spins, this much variation is normal. The data is consistent with a fair spinner.

**Answer:** The spinner appears **fair**. **[B1 for $25$ as the expected count; B1 for a sensible comparison plus the conclusion]**

Full marks $= 2$.

If the same experiment were repeated and the results were $5, 30, 32, 33$, the verdict would be different: red is way below the expected $25$ (only $5$ versus $25$), so the spinner appears biased AGAINST red.

## A common trap: small samples

Five tosses of a fair coin produce $5$ heads. Is the coin biased?

The probability of $5$ heads in $5$ tosses of a fair coin is $\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^5 = \tfrac{1}{32} \approx 3\%$. Unusual, but not impossible. From this experiment alone, you cannot say the coin is biased. The exam wants you to say "more trials are needed".

Compare with this: a coin tossed $1000$ times, lands heads $700$ times. That is well beyond what a fair coin would produce. Now you can confidently say the coin is biased.

**Rule of thumb**: only declare bias when the differences are LARGE OR the experiment had MANY trials (ideally both). With a small experiment, the safe answer is always "consistent with fair, but more trials would help".

## Common mistakes

- **Declaring bias from a small experiment.** "$7$ heads in $10$ tosses, so the coin is biased" is wrong reasoning. Random variation is too big at $n = 10$ to draw conclusions.
- **Comparing percentages without context.** Yes, $70\% \neq 50\%$, but the question is whether the difference is BIGGER than normal variation, not just non-zero.
- **Forgetting that "fair" $=$ equal sector sizes.** Some questions sneak in unequal sector sizes; then the expected counts are NOT all the same.
- **Confusing "fair" with "random".** They mean similar things. "Random" applies to selecting an item from a set; "fair" applies to a spinner, coin, or die.
- **Writing a verdict without a comparison.** The marker wants to see the EXPECTED counts written down. Just saying "looks fair" without justification loses the mark.

## Quick summary

- **Fair**: every outcome equally likely. Probability $\tfrac{1}{n}$ for $n$ equal sectors.
- **Biased**: outcomes NOT equally likely.
- **Random**: each item equally likely to be picked.
- **Testing fairness**: compute expected counts, compare with observed, write a one-sentence verdict.
- Small samples wobble a lot. Declare bias only when differences are big OR trials are many.
- The marker wants a CALCULATION (expected counts) plus a JUDGEMENT (one-sentence verdict).
