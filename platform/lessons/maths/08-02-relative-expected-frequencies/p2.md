## Why this matters

You know that a fair die has $P(\text{six}) = \tfrac{1}{6}$. But what does that mean for an actual experiment? If you roll the die $60$ times, how many sixes should you expect?

Not exactly $10$, but on average $10$. Over many experiments of $60$ rolls each, the AVERAGE number of sixes will settle around $10$. Any single experiment will land near this number but not exactly on it.

The formula is short and lives in your head for the rest of the unit:

$$\text{expected count} = p \times n$$

where $p$ is the probability of the event in a single trial, and $n$ is the number of trials. The expected count is the answer to "if I did this experiment many times, what would be the average outcome?"

Expected-frequency questions are worth $1$ to $2$ marks each on a paper, often as part (b) or (c) of a longer probability question. They are also part of how you DECIDE whether a real experiment looks fair (next page).

## The formula

If an event has probability $p$ in a single trial, and the experiment is repeated $n$ times, the expected number of times the event happens is:

$$\boxed{\text{expected count} = p \times n}$$

$p$ can be a fraction, decimal, or percentage. $n$ is a positive whole number. The product is an estimate of the count; it does not have to be a whole number.

## Why $p \times n$?

The intuition is direct. The probability $p$ is the **fraction of trials** in which the event happens. If you do $n$ trials, then $p$ of them produce the event. That is $p \times n$.

Worked through with numbers: a die has $P(\text{six}) = \tfrac{1}{6}$. Roll it $60$ times. The fraction of rolls that should come up six is $\tfrac{1}{6}$, and a sixth of $60$ is $10$. So we expect $10$ sixes.

The same logic with decimals: a coin has $P(\text{heads}) = 0.5$. Toss it $24$ times. Half of the tosses should be heads, so we expect $0.5 \times 24 = 12$ heads.

## Expected counts do not have to be whole numbers

Suppose a spinner has $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac{1}{3}$ and you spin it $20$ times. Expected reds $= \tfrac{1}{3} \times 20 = \tfrac{20}{3} \approx 6.67$.

You will never observe $6.67$ reds in a single experiment, because the actual count is always an integer ($6, 7, 8, \ldots$). But over many experiments of $20$ spins each, the AVERAGE count will be $6.67$. That is what the expected frequency tells you.

Your exam paper accepts the exact decimal or the fraction. Do NOT round to a whole number unless the question asks for it.

## Worked example: predict from probability

A coin is weighted so that $P(\text{heads}) = 0.7$. The coin is tossed $50$ times. Calculate the expected number of heads.

## When the probability comes from an experiment

What if the question gives you the results of an experiment and asks how many of the next $N$ trials would be a particular outcome?

This is a two-step question. First, compute the **relative frequency** as your estimate of $p$. Then multiply by the new number of trials.

**Example shape**: "A spinner is spun $80$ times. Red comes up $20$ times. Estimate how many reds in the next $400$ spins."

Step 1: relative frequency of red $= \tfrac{20}{80} = \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.25$.

Step 2: expected reds in $400$ spins $= 0.25 \times 400 = 100$.

The relative frequency is your best estimate of the underlying probability. Multiply that estimate by the new $n$.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to multiply by $n$**. Writing "expected $= p$" or "$= 0.7$" instead of the full product.
- **Using the wrong $p$**. If the question gives you experimental data, your $p$ is the relative frequency. If the question says "the probability is $0.4$", use $0.4$ directly. Read carefully.
- **Rounding too early**. $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 20 = 6.67$, not $7$. Only round if the question says so.
- **Multiplying probabilities instead of multiplying $p$ by $n$**. $p \times n$ is one product; do not square $p$ or anything like that.
- **Mixing percentages and decimals carelessly**. If $p = 30\% = 0.3$ and $n = 200$, the answer is $0.3 \times 200 = 60$, not $30 \times 200 = 6000$.

## Quick summary

- **Formula**: expected count $= p \times n$.
- $p$ is the probability of the event in a single trial.
- $n$ is the number of trials.
- The expected count is a LONG-RUN AVERAGE. Any single experiment will land near it but not exactly on it.
- Expected counts can be non-integer values; do not round unless asked.
- If $p$ is unknown but you have data, estimate $p$ from the relative frequency first.
