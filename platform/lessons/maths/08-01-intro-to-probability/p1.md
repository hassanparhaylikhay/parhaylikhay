## Why this matters

Imagine flipping a fair coin. What is the probability it lands on heads? Half the time it does, half the time it doesn't. So the probability is $\tfrac{1}{2}$, or $0.5$, or $50\%$. All three are correct answers to the same question.

Now imagine spinning a fair five-sector spinner with colours red, red, red, blue, yellow. What is the probability the arrow lands on red? Three out of five sectors are red, so the probability is $\tfrac{3}{5}$, or $0.6$, or $60\%$.

Probability is just a number from $0$ to $1$ that says how likely something is. The whole topic of Unit 8 sits on top of this one idea, so getting comfortable with the scale, and switching freely between fractions, decimals, and percentages, makes everything that follows easier.

In your exam, probability questions make up around $5$ marks per paper. The marks are within reach for any student who is comfortable with fractions. The skill that the exam tests is reading the question carefully and writing the probability in the form the question asks for.

## The scale runs from 0 to 1

A probability is a number between $0$ and $1$, inclusive.

- $P(\text{event}) = 0$ means the event is **impossible**. It never happens.
- $P(\text{event}) = 1$ means the event is **certain**. It always happens.
- Anything in between is a regular event, more or less likely depending on where it sits on the scale.

## Three ways to write a probability

The same probability can be written as a **fraction**, a **decimal**, or a **percentage**. All three are correct, and your calculator can switch between them in one button press.

The exam usually accepts whichever form you write, but if the question says "give your answer as a fraction" you must give a fraction. A decimal or percentage answer in that case loses the accuracy mark, not the method mark. **When in doubt, write the fraction.**

## Notation

A probability question gives an event a short name, then asks for its probability. The notation looks like:

$$P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$$

Read $P(\text{event})$ as "the probability of the event". So $P(\text{heads}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ means "the probability of getting heads is one half". Examples you will see on the paper:

- $P(A)$ where $A$ is some event the question defined (often something like "a head", "a red marble", "a prime number").
- $P(\text{exactly one})$ for a multi-stage situation.
- $P(\text{both})$, $P(\text{at least one})$ for two-event situations (those are 8.3 territory).

The bracket holds a short description of the event. Treat the whole expression as one chunk of notation.

## Worked example: place a probability on the scale

A fair six-sided die is rolled. Find the probability of rolling an even number.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-place-on-scale" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:620px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: List the equally likely outcomes.** A fair die has six faces, numbered $1$ to $6$. Each face is equally likely to come up.

**Step 2: Count the favourable outcomes.** The even faces are $2, 4, 6$. That is $3$ favourable outcomes out of $6$ total.

**Step 3: Write the probability.**

$$P(\text{even}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{total}} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$$

**Answer:** $P(\text{even}) = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{2}}$. **[B1 for $\tfrac{3}{6}$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}$]**

Full marks $= 1$.

This is "even chance" on the scale: $0.5$, or $50\%$, the midpoint. A fair coin lands heads with this same probability.

## A second example: place a fraction on the scale

A spinner has $5$ equal sectors: $2$ red, $2$ blue, $1$ green. Find $P(\text{red})$. State whether red is more or less likely than blue, and place each probability on the scale.

**Step 1: Count favourable and total.** Red sectors: $2$. Total sectors: $5$.

$$P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{2}{5} = 0.4 = 40\%$$

**Step 2: Compare with blue.** Blue also has $2$ sectors out of $5$, so $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{2}{5}$ as well. **Red and blue are equally likely**, both at $0.4$ on the scale, slightly to the left of "even chance".

**Answer:** $P(\text{red}) = \boxed{\dfrac{2}{5}}$. Red and blue are equally likely.

The point: even without a calculator, $\tfrac{2}{5} = 0.4$ sits a little below the halfway mark on the scale, so "red" is slightly less likely than "even chance". That mental picture is what the scale is for.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing a probability greater than $1$.** Anything above $1$ is automatic zero. If a calculation gives $\tfrac{7}{5}$, something earlier went wrong.
- **Writing a probability less than $0$.** A negative answer also means something went wrong, often a wrong subtraction in a complement question.
- **Using a percentage when a fraction was asked for.** "Give your answer as a fraction" means a fraction. Don't lose the accuracy mark.
- **Not simplifying.** $\tfrac{3}{6}$ is correct but $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is preferred. Some markers ignore the simplification, some take a mark off. Always simplify.
- **Mixing decimal and percent.** $0.6$ and $60\%$ are equal, but $0.6\%$ is $0.006$. Always write the percent sign $\%$ explicitly, and never the decimal sign without context.

## Quick summary

- **Probabilities live between $0$ and $1$**, inclusive. Never above $1$, never below $0$.
- **Five landmark values**: impossible ($0$), unlikely ($\tfrac{1}{4}$), even ($\tfrac{1}{2}$), likely ($\tfrac{3}{4}$), certain ($1$).
- **Three forms**: fraction, decimal, percentage. All correct unless the question specifies.
- **Notation**: $P(\text{event}) = \dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}$.
- **Always simplify** the fraction.
