## Why this matters

Almost every probability question on the paper begins by telling you about a bag, a list, a table, or a diagram. The first job is to count two things accurately: the **favourable outcomes** (the ones we care about) and the **total outcomes** (all the equally likely possibilities). Then the probability is the ratio:

$$P(A) = \dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$

Get the count right, and the rest is just dividing. Get it wrong, and every later step is wrong with it.

This page works through the three places the count comes from on a typical paper:

1. **A short list** of values (like hours of TV for $9$ children).
2. **A bag, set, or spinner** with colour groups or labels.
3. **A two-way table** or a **Venn diagram**.

## Worked example: from a list

The number of hours nine children watched TV last week were:

$$1,\quad 2,\quad 2,\quad 2,\quad 6,\quad 7,\quad 7,\quad 8,\quad 10$$

A child is chosen at random. Find the probability that the child watched **more than $6$ hours**.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p2-children" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:620px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Count the total.** Nine children in the list, so the denominator is $9$.

**Step 2: Count the favourable.** "More than $6$" is strictly greater than $6$. The $6$ itself does NOT count. Children at $7, 7, 8, 10$ qualify. That is $4$ children.

**Step 3: Write the probability.**

$$P(\text{more than 6 hours}) = \dfrac{4}{9}$$

$\gcd(4, 9) = 1$, so the fraction is already in lowest terms.

**Answer:** $P(\text{more than 6 hours}) = \boxed{\dfrac{4}{9}}$. **[B1 for $\tfrac{4}{9}$]**

Full marks $= 1$.

A common slip on this question type: counting the $6$ as if it were $\geq 6$. Hand on the paper, point at each number, decide YES or NO before counting up.

## Worked example: from a bag

A bag contains ten cards: $3$ green squares, $1$ red square, $4$ green triangles, $2$ red triangles. A card is chosen at random. Find $P(\text{the card is green})$.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p2-cards" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:600px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Identify the total.** Ten cards in total. So the denominator is $10$.

**Step 2: Identify the favourable.** "Green" includes BOTH green squares AND green triangles. That is $3 + 4 = 7$ cards.

**Step 3: Write the probability.**

$$P(\text{green}) = \dfrac{7}{10}$$

$\gcd(7, 10) = 1$, so already simplified.

**Answer:** $P(\text{green}) = \boxed{\dfrac{7}{10}}$. **[B1 for $\tfrac{7}{10}$]**

Full marks $= 1$.

The shape of the card (square vs triangle) was a distractor. Read the question carefully: it asked for "green", not "green square". If it had said "green square", the count would have been $3$ out of $10$, giving $\tfrac{3}{10}$.

A few quick probabilities from this table:

- $P(\text{cricket}) = \dfrac{23}{40}$. Read across the cricket column total.
- $P(\text{Year 10}) = \dfrac{20}{40} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. Read across the Year 10 row total.
- $P(\text{plays cricket AND is in Year 10}) = \dfrac{12}{40} = \dfrac{3}{10}$. Read the single intersecting cell.

The trap: don't divide by a row or column total when the question wants the GRAND total. Always check what the denominator should be.

## Reading a Venn diagram (one or two sets)

A Venn diagram lays out a sample space as a rectangle, with circles (one or more) inside it showing membership in sets. To find a probability, count how many items are in the FAVOURABLE region and divide by the count in the WHOLE rectangle.

Read the diagram by adding the counts in the relevant regions, then dividing by $25$:

- $P(A) = \dfrac{8 + 7}{25} = \dfrac{15}{25} = \dfrac{3}{5}$. The whole $A$ circle counts both "$A$ only" and "$A$ and $B$".
- $P(B) = \dfrac{7 + 5}{25} = \dfrac{12}{25}$.
- $P(A \cap B) = \dfrac{7}{25}$. The overlap region alone.
- $P(\text{neither}) = \dfrac{5}{25} = \dfrac{1}{5}$. The bit outside both circles.

The set notation $A \cap B$ ("$A$ and $B$" / "in both") and $A \cup B$ ("$A$ or $B$" / "in at least one") shows up properly in 8.3. For now, just know that the overlap region is the "and" region.

## Common mistakes

- **Misreading the qualifier.** "At least $7$" includes $7$; "more than $7$" does not. The most common one-mark slip on the paper.
- **Forgetting to add ALL groups for the denominator.** A bag with $3$ red, $5$ blue, $2$ yellow has $10$ marbles, not $5$ or $8$. The denominator is everything.
- **Reading the wrong row total in a two-way table.** Double-check whether the question asks for a single category, an intersection, or a row/column total.
- **Missing the "neither" region in a Venn diagram.** Items outside both circles still count toward the total but not toward $A$ or $B$ individually.
- **Not simplifying.** $\tfrac{6}{9}$ is correct but $\tfrac{2}{3}$ is preferred. Some markers ignore the simplification, some take a mark off.

## Quick summary

- **Probability** $= \dfrac{\text{favourable count}}{\text{total count}}$. Two careful counts.
- **The TOTAL** is the WHOLE sample space (every possibility), not just the others.
- **Qualifiers matter**: "at least" includes the value, "more than" does not.
- **Two-way table**: cells are intersections; margins are single categories; corner is the grand total.
- **Venn diagram**: count the favourable region(s), divide by the grand total.
- **Always simplify** the fraction.
