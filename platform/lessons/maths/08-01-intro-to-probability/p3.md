## Why this matters

Sometimes the "favourable" outcomes are easy to count: red marbles in a bag, even numbers on a die. Other times the favourable outcomes are scattered or hard to pin down: "the result is NOT a $1$", "at least one card is red", "no green marbles among three draws".

When that happens, flip the question on its head. The event $A$ and its complement $A'$ between them cover every possibility, with no overlap. So:

$$P(A) + P(A') = 1$$

Rearranged, that gives the shortcut:

$$P(A) = 1 - P(A')$$

If counting $A'$ is easier than counting $A$, count $A'$ instead and subtract from $1$. The same answer falls out, often after far less arithmetic.

This identity is one of the most powerful shortcuts in the whole probability section of your exam. It saves time on $1$-mark questions and can be the difference between full marks and zero marks on a hard $4$-mark question.

## Notation: A and A prime

The complement of $A$ is written $A'$, read as "$A$ prime" or "$A$ complement". Some textbooks use a bar: $\overline{A}$. Both mean the same thing. Your exam uses the prime notation $A'$, so we use that throughout the unit.

## Why P(A) + P(A') always equals 1

Every possible outcome lands in EXACTLY ONE of $A$ or $A'$. There is no outcome that is both in $A$ and not in $A$. There is no outcome that is in neither. So together, $A$ and $A'$ cover the whole sample space.

Since the whole sample space has probability $1$ (something will definitely happen), the two pieces must add up to $1$:

$$P(A) + P(A') = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad P(A') = 1 - P(A)$$

That single identity is the whole topic of this part of the lesson.

## Worked example: from a given probability

A weather app says the probability that it rains in Karachi tomorrow is $\tfrac{2}{5}$. Find the probability that it does NOT rain in Karachi tomorrow.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p3-complement" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Name the events.** Let $A$ be the event "it rains tomorrow". Then $A'$ is the event "it does not rain tomorrow".

**Step 2: Apply the complement rule.** $P(A) = \tfrac{2}{5}$, so

$$P(A') = 1 - P(A) = 1 - \dfrac{2}{5}$$

**Step 3: Subtract.** Rewrite $1$ as $\tfrac{5}{5}$ so both fractions share a denominator.

$$P(A') = \dfrac{5}{5} - \dfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{3}{5}$$

**Answer:** $P(\text{no rain}) = \boxed{\dfrac{3}{5}}$. **[B1 for $1 - \tfrac{2}{5}$ or $\tfrac{3}{5}$]**

Full marks $= 1$.

Sanity check: probabilities should be between $0$ and $1$. $\tfrac{3}{5} = 0.6$, which is fine. And $\tfrac{2}{5} + \tfrac{3}{5} = 1$, confirming the two add to $1$ as required.

## When the complement is the FAST route

Sometimes you are asked for $P(A)$ directly, but $A$ involves several cases that are tedious to count. If $A'$ has fewer cases, find $P(A')$ first, then subtract from $1$.

**Example shape**: "Three counters are drawn from a bag of red and blue. Find the probability that AT LEAST ONE is blue."

The event "at least one blue" has many sub-cases (one blue, two blue, three blue). The complement "no blue" has just one case (all three are red). Counting one case is far easier than counting three. So:

$$P(\text{at least one blue}) = 1 - P(\text{no blue})$$

This pattern shows up on the harder probability questions in 8.3, where the complement turns a four-line answer into a two-line answer. The mark scheme rewards it: markers expect the complement on "at least one" wording.

## A second example: by direct count and by complement

A bag has $20$ marbles: $7$ red and $13$ that are not red. Find $P(\text{not red})$ by (a) direct counting and (b) the complement.

**Method (a) Direct counting.** Non-red marbles: $13$. Total marbles: $20$.

$$P(\text{not red}) = \dfrac{13}{20}$$

**Method (b) Complement.** $P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{7}{20}$, so

$$P(\text{not red}) = 1 - \dfrac{7}{20} = \dfrac{20}{20} - \dfrac{7}{20} = \dfrac{13}{20}$$

**Answer:** $P(\text{not red}) = \boxed{\dfrac{13}{20}}$. Both methods agree, as they must.

For this single-stage example, either method takes about the same time. The complement only saves time when the favourable outcome is hard to count directly, but the principle is the same: you may pick whichever method is fastest, as long as the working is shown.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting that probabilities sum to $1$**, not to $100$. $P(A) + P(A') = 1$, not $100$. If your work uses percentages, then yes, $P(A)\% + P(A')\% = 100\%$, but the underlying number is $1$.
- **Reversing the subtraction.** $P(A) - 1$ gives a negative number. The rule is $1 - P(A)$.
- **Mismatched denominators.** $1 - \tfrac{2}{5}$ needs you to rewrite $1$ as $\tfrac{5}{5}$ before subtracting. Don't try to subtract from a whole number directly without converting.
- **Mistaking "not red" for "blue".** If a bag has red, blue, and green, then "not red" means BLUE or GREEN, not just blue. Always include every non-red colour.
- **Using the complement when the direct count is simpler.** A single-mark question rarely needs the complement trick. Save the technique for the harder cases where it actually helps.

## Quick summary

- **Complement** $A'$: the event that $A$ does NOT happen.
- **Key identity**: $P(A) + P(A') = 1$.
- **Shortcut**: $P(A) = 1 - P(A')$ when $A'$ is easier to count.
- **Wording flags**: "not", "at least one", "none", "fail" usually mean use the complement.
- **Subtraction setup**: rewrite $1$ as a fraction matching the denominator first.
