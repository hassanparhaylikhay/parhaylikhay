## See the length

Drag the pink dot anywhere. The yellow arrow is the vector $\mathbf{a}$. The blue dashed leg is how far across, the orange dashed leg is how far up or down. The yellow number on the arrow is the **magnitude**, $|\mathbf{a}|$, the length of the arrow itself. Watch how it always equals $\sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}$ as you move the dot. That is the only rule on this page.

## Why this matters

You walk $3$ blocks east, then $4$ blocks north, to get from your house to school. You walked $7$ blocks total, but the school is not $7$ blocks away as the crow flies. The direct distance, the length of the diagonal you would draw on a map, is $5$ blocks. That diagonal is the **magnitude** of the vector that takes you from home to school.

Every vector has two facts: a direction (which way it points) and a magnitude (how long it is). On this page we only care about the magnitude. The shortcut is **Pythagoras**: square each component of the column, add, take the square root. Done.

Magnitude questions on your exam are worth $2$ marks: M1 for setting up the square root of $x^{2} + y^{2}$, A1 for the value. Get the formula on paper before you reach for the calculator and the method mark is locked in.

The column $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$ already tells you the two legs of the right triangle: top number is the across-leg, bottom number is the up-leg. The arrow itself is the hypotenuse. So **Pythagoras** is the entire formula, applied to the column.

## Notation: the two upright bars

When you see $|\mathbf{a}|$ or $|\vec{AB}|$, the two vertical lines mean "length of" or "size of". So:

- $|\mathbf{a}|$ reads as "the magnitude of $\mathbf{a}$"
- $|\vec{AB}|$ reads as "the length of the arrow from $A$ to $B$"

The bars are the same symbol you use for the absolute value of a number: $|-5| = 5$. The idea is the same: ignore the direction, keep the size. For a number, that means drop the minus sign. For a vector, that means use Pythagoras.

## Negatives disappear when you square

A common worry: what about $|\mathbf{v}|$ when one or both components are negative? You square each component before adding, so the sign disappears. $(-3)^{2} = 9$, exactly the same as $3^{2} = 9$. That is why magnitude is never negative: it is the length of an arrow, and arrows do not have negative lengths.

## Whole numbers vs surds

Not every magnitude is a tidy whole number. Most are not. When $x^{2} + y^{2}$ is a perfect square ($25, 169, 100$) the answer is a whole number. When it is not, the exam either wants the surd form $\sqrt{37}$ or a decimal to $3$ significant figures, like $6.08$. Read the question:

- "Find $|\mathbf{a}|$ giving your answer in simplest surd form" $\Rightarrow$ leave it as $\sqrt{37}$, simplifying if possible (for example $\sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}$).
- "Find $|\mathbf{a}|$, correct to $3$ significant figures" $\Rightarrow$ punch into the calculator: $\sqrt{37} = 6.0827\ldots \approx 6.08$.
- No instruction either way $\Rightarrow$ give the decimal to $3$ s.f. unless the answer is whole.

## Worked example: a tidy answer

The vector $\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $|\mathbf{a}|$.

<iframe src="/widgets/magnitude-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-magnitude-3-4" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:680px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Write the formula with the column components in place.** The top number of the column is $3$, the bottom is $4$. Square both, add, take the root.

$$|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{3^{2} + 4^{2}}$$

**Step 2: Crunch the squares.** $3^{2} = 9$ and $4^{2} = 16$, so

$$|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25}$$

**Step 3: Take the square root.** $\sqrt{25} = 5$, exactly.

**Answer:** $|\mathbf{a}| = \boxed{5}$. **[M1 for $\sqrt{3^{2} + 4^{2}}$; A1 for $5$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: with negatives, surds, and rounding

This one is taken straight from a recent paper. $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -6 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $|\mathbf{v}|$, giving your answer correct to $3$ significant figures.

<iframe src="/widgets/magnitude-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-magnitude-negatives" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:680px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Set up Pythagoras.** Square each component first; the brackets keep the negatives visible.

$$|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{(-1)^{2} + (-6)^{2}}$$

**Step 2: Square (the sign disappears).** $(-1)^{2} = 1$ and $(-6)^{2} = 36$, so

$$|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{1 + 36} = \sqrt{37}$$

**Step 3: Decimal to $3$ s.f.** $\sqrt{37} = 6.0827\ldots$, which rounds to $6.08$.

**Answer:** $|\mathbf{v}| = \boxed{6.08}$. **[M1 for $\sqrt{(-1)^{2} + (-6)^{2}}$; A1 for $6.08$ or $\sqrt{37}$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

Notice how the method mark comes from writing $\sqrt{(\text{top})^{2} + (\text{bottom})^{2}}$ with the actual numbers in place. Even if your calculator misbehaves and you compute the final number wrong, that first line is worth one mark on its own.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the square root.** $|\mathbf{a}|^{2}$ is the squared magnitude, not the magnitude. The most common single-mark slip on a $2$-mark question.
- **Keeping the negative.** Writing $\sqrt{-1 + 36}$ for $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -6 \end{pmatrix}$. The squaring throws away the sign: $(-1)^{2} = +1$.
- **Adding the components instead of squaring.** $|\mathbf{a}| \neq x + y$. The vector $\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$ has magnitude $5$, not $7$.
- **Mismatching the answer form.** Leaving the answer as $\sqrt{37}$ when the question asks for $3$ s.f., or rounding to $6.08$ when the question asks for surd form.
- **Dropping units.** If the vector represents a real displacement ($\,\text{m}$, $\,\text{km}$), the magnitude needs the same units. Lose the unit, lose the accuracy mark.

## Quick summary

- The magnitude of $\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$ is $|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}$.
- Pythagoras on the column. Square each component first (negatives become positive), add, take the square root.
- Magnitude is always $\geq 0$.
- Read the question: surd form, $3$ s.f., or whole number, your choice depends on the wording.
- The method mark is earned by writing the square root expression in full before computing it.
