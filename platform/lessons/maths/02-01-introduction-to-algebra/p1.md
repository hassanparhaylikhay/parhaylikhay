## Why this matters

You text a friend, "I have $x$ rupees, Hassan has $50$ rupees more than me." That $x$ is algebra. It's a letter you've used to stand for a number that you haven't decided yet. Algebra is just maths with these "stand-ins" for numbers, and the rules are surprisingly few.

Cambridge starts every algebra question by assuming you know a handful of conventions: what $3a$ actually means, why $a^2$ is different from $2a$, what a coefficient is, and which terms can be combined. None of it is hard, but if you mix any of it up you lose marks for the rest of the question. This part is the rulebook.

## Letters as stand-ins for numbers

In arithmetic, every number has a fixed value: $7$ is always $7$. In algebra, a letter (like $x$, $a$, or $n$) stands for a number that we don't know yet, or a number that can change.

- "I have $x$ rupees": $x$ is unknown, but it has *some* value.
- "$n$ is any whole number": $n$ can be $1, 2, 3, \dots$.
- "$v = u + at$": $v, u, a, t$ are letters that stand for whatever values you plug in.

That's all. The maths still works the same way; you're just doing it without committing to specific numbers yet.

Two things students mix up the most:

- $a^2$ vs $2a$. **$a^2 = a \times a$** (a square). **$2a = a + a$** (doubled). For $a = 5$: $a^2 = 25$ but $2a = 10$. Different answers, completely different operations.
- $a + b$ vs $ab$. **$a + b$** stays as a sum, you can't simplify it unless you know the values. **$ab$** is a product. Don't write $a + b$ as $ab$.

## Coefficient: the number in front

The **coefficient** is whatever number sits multiplied with the letter.

- In $7x$, the coefficient of $x$ is $\textcolor{#00abfa}{7}$.
- In $-3y$, the coefficient of $y$ is $\textcolor{#00abfa}{-3}$ (the minus belongs to the coefficient).
- In just $x$, the coefficient is $\textcolor{#00abfa}{1}$ (we don't write the $1$, but it's there).
- In $\tfrac{x}{4}$, the coefficient is $\textcolor{#00abfa}{\tfrac{1}{4}}$ (because $\tfrac{x}{4} = \tfrac{1}{4} \times x$).

The coefficient tells you "how many of that letter" you've got. If you have $7x$, you've got seven $x$'s.

## Like terms: same letter, same power

Two terms are **like terms** if they use the same letter raised to the same power.

| Term A | Term B | Like? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| $2x$ | $5x$ | yes | both $x^1$ |
| $3a$ | $-a$ | yes | both $a^1$ |
| $x^2$ | $4x^2$ | yes | both $x^2$ |
| $2x$ | $3y$ | no | different letters |
| $2x$ | $2x^2$ | no | different powers of $x$ |
| $4xy$ | $7xy$ | yes | both $xy$ |
| $5xy$ | $5x$ | no | one has the extra $y$ |

The rule is simply: if the **letter part is identical**, they're like terms.

## Combining like terms

When two like terms are added or subtracted, you just **add or subtract the coefficients**. The letter stays put.

$$2x + 5x \;=\; (2+5)x \;=\; 7x$$

$$8a - 3a \;=\; 5a$$

$$x + x \;=\; 2x \quad (\text{coefficient of } x \text{ is } 1)$$

Unlike terms cannot be combined. $2x + 3y$ stays as $2x + 3y$. There is no shorter form.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: $a + a$ is NOT $a^2$.** $a + a$ doubles the value, so $a + a = 2a$. $a^2$ multiplies $a$ by itself, which is a different operation. For $a = 4$: $a + a = 8$ but $a^2 = 16$. Always check yourself with a small number.

</div>

## Worked example

Simplify $4x + 2y - x + 5y$.

**Step 1: Group the like terms together.** $x$-terms are $4x$ and $-x$. $y$-terms are $2y$ and $5y$.

$$4x + 2y - x + 5y \;=\; (4x - x) + (2y + 5y)$$

**Step 2: Combine each group.**

$$4x - x \;=\; 3x \qquad 2y + 5y \;=\; 7y$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{3x + 7y}$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 1$.

## Common mistakes

- Writing $a^2$ as $2a$. They look similar but mean different things. $a^2 = a \times a$; $2a = a + a$.
- Combining unlike terms. $2x + 3y$ does NOT equal $5xy$ or $5$. It stays as $2x + 3y$.
- Dropping the sign. In $4x - x$, the $-$ belongs to the second $x$ (coefficient $-1$). So $4x - x = 3x$, not $4x$.
- Treating just $x$ as having "no coefficient". Its coefficient is $1$. So $x + 5x = 6x$, not $5x$.

## Quick summary

- A letter stands for a number, known or unknown.
- $3a = 3 \times a$, $ab = a \times b$, $a^2 = a \times a$. No multiplication sign needed when letters and numbers sit side by side.
- The **coefficient** is the number in front of the letter (default is $1$ if nothing is shown).
- **Like terms** have the same letter raised to the same power. Combine them by adding or subtracting the coefficients.
- Unlike terms cannot be combined.
