## Why this matters

Pakistan railways announce "Train arrives in $30$ minutes, travelling at $80$ km/h", and your phone calculates the distance still to go using $d = vt$. Every formula you've ever seen is just an instruction: *plug values in here, the answer comes out there*. Cambridge gives you formulas in physics-style questions all the time, and the only skill you need is careful substitution.

The mechanics are exactly the substitution from p2. The new bit is **knowing which value goes with which letter**. Cambridge always tells you, but only if you read the question carefully.

## Expression vs formula

A small but useful distinction:

- An **expression** like $3x + 5$ has no equals sign. You can simplify it or substitute into it.
- A **formula** like $v = u + at$ uses an equals sign to *define* one quantity in terms of others. You substitute into the right-hand side and the equation tells you the value of the left-hand side.

You'll see formulas in physics questions (motion, force, energy), in geometry (area, volume, perimeter), and in finance (interest, exchange rates). The substitution rules are identical to plain expressions; you just have to keep track of more letters.

## Common Cambridge formulas

The syllabus assumes you can substitute into any formula a question gives you. The question always lists which letters are which. You don't memorise these; the **question gives them to you**.

| Formula | What it computes | Letters |
|---|---|---|
| $v = u + at$ | final velocity | initial $u$, acceleration $a$, time $t$ |
| $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ | distance travelled | as above |
| $A = \pi r^2$ | area of a circle | radius $r$ |
| $C = 2\pi r$ | circumference of a circle | radius $r$ |
| $V = \pi r^2 h$ | volume of a cylinder | radius $r$, height $h$ |
| $E = mgh$ | gravitational energy | mass $m$, gravity $g$, height $h$ |
| $F = ma$ | force | mass $m$, acceleration $a$ |

Each letter is a **slot**. Identify which value goes in which slot, then substitute exactly as in p2.

## Multi-step formulas

When the formula has powers, fractions, or several terms, substitute carefully and let BIDMAS guide the order.

**Example.** $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ with $u = 0$, $a = 10$, $t = 4$.

**Step 1: Compute $ut$.**

$$ut \;=\; (0)(4) \;=\; 0$$

**Step 2: Compute $\tfrac{1}{2}at^2$.** Apply the index first.

$$t^2 \;=\; (4)^2 \;=\; 16$$

$$\tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}(10)(16) \;=\; 80$$

**Step 3: Add the two parts.**

$$s \;=\; 0 + 80 \;=\; 80$$

When you substitute and the formula has more than one term, work each term separately then combine. Keeps everything tidy.

## Answers in terms of π

Many Cambridge geometry questions ask for the answer "in terms of $\pi$", which means **don't compute $\pi$ as a decimal**, leave it as the symbol.

**Example.** $A = \pi r^2$ with $r = 4$.

$$A \;=\; \pi (4)^2 \;=\; 16\pi$$

The answer is $16\pi$, not $50.27$. If the question asks to "$3$ s.f.", then evaluate: $16\pi \approx 50.3$.

## Worked example

The temperature conversion formula is $C = \tfrac{5}{9}(F - 32)$, where $F$ is degrees Fahrenheit and $C$ is degrees Celsius. Convert $F = 50$ to Celsius.

**Step 1: Substitute $F = 50$.**

$$C \;=\; \tfrac{5}{9}(50 - 32)$$

**Step 2: Brackets first (BIDMAS).**

$$50 - 32 \;=\; 18$$

$$C \;=\; \tfrac{5}{9}(18)$$

**Step 3: Multiply.**

$$\tfrac{5}{9} \times 18 \;=\; \tfrac{5 \times 18}{9} \;=\; \tfrac{90}{9} \;=\; 10$$

**Answer:** $C = \boxed{10}$ degrees Celsius. **[M1 for the substitution; A1 for the answer]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Substituting in the wrong slot. $u = 5$ goes where $u$ is, not where $a$ is. Read the question's letter labels carefully.
- Skipping the brackets. $a t^2$ with $a = 10$, $t = 4$ gives $10 \times (4)^2 = 10 \times 16 = 160$, not $40^2$.
- Computing $\pi$ as a decimal when the question asks "in terms of $\pi$". Leave it as $\pi$.
- Adding instead of multiplying. $at$ is $a \times t$, not $a + t$.

## Quick summary

- A formula assigns a value to one letter using others. Substitute each letter with its given value, then evaluate.
- Read the question carefully: which value goes in which slot.
- Apply **BIDMAS** after substituting, especially when powers or fractions appear.
- "In terms of $\pi$" means leave $\pi$ as a symbol; don't decimalise.
