## The cuboid is the simplest 3D shape

Most Cambridge 3D questions are built around a cuboid (a box). Three lengths fully describe it: length $l$, width $w$, height $h$. Every diagonal length, every angle, and every corner-to-corner question follows from those three numbers.

You will see two kinds of diagonals:

Try the example dimensions $6 \times 4 \times 3$. The base diagonal is $\sqrt{6^{2}+4^{2}} = \sqrt{52} \approx 7.21$. The space diagonal is $\sqrt{6^{2}+4^{2}+3^{2}} = \sqrt{61} \approx 7.81$. The space diagonal is always **longer** than any face diagonal of the same cuboid (because it has the extra $h^{2}$ in the square root).

## Why the formula works (the two-step picture)

The space diagonal isn't a new piece of mathematics, it's plain Pythagoras applied twice in succession:

<iframe src="/widgets/3d-step-explorer.html?preset=cuboid-space-diagonal" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:600px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Step through the explorer above. You'll see:

1. The base diagonal first: a right triangle with legs $l$ and $w$, hypotenuse $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}$.
2. Then a new right triangle in the vertical plane: legs $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}$ (along the base) and $h$ (up the back edge), hypotenuse $d$ (the space diagonal).

So:

$$d^{2} = (\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}})^{2} + h^{2} = l^{2} + w^{2} + h^{2}$$

$$d = \sqrt{l^{2} + w^{2} + h^{2}}$$

Same formula. The point is that you can rebuild it from scratch if you forget, just remember "Pythagoras twice".

## Worked example: a packing-box diagonal

A rectangular box is $30$ cm long, $20$ cm wide, $15$ cm tall. A thin rod is packed diagonally from one corner to the opposite corner. What is the maximum length of rod that fits?

The rod's length equals the box's space diagonal:

$$d = \sqrt{30^{2} + 20^{2} + 15^{2}} = \sqrt{900 + 400 + 225} = \sqrt{1525}$$

$$d \approx 39.05\;\text{cm}$$

So a rod up to about $39.05$ cm fits.

**[M1 for $d^{2} = 30^{2}+20^{2}+15^{2}$ or two-step Pythagoras; A1 for $d \approx 39.05$ cm]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: reverse problem (find a dimension)

A cuboid has a space diagonal of $25$ cm and a base diagonal of $20$ cm. Find its height.

Use the second step in reverse. The space diagonal, base diagonal, and height form a right triangle with the space diagonal as the hypotenuse:

$$\text{space diag}^{2} = \text{base diag}^{2} + h^{2}$$

$$25^{2} = 20^{2} + h^{2}$$

$$h^{2} = 625 - 400 = 225$$

$$h = 15\;\text{cm}$$

(The cuboid happens to be the $(15, 20, 25)$ family, which is $(3, 4, 5)$ multiplied by $5$.)

**[M1 for $25^{2} = 20^{2} + h^{2}$; A1 for $h = 15$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## A useful fact about cubes

For a cube of side $a$, all three dimensions are equal. So the space diagonal simplifies to:

$$d = \sqrt{a^{2} + a^{2} + a^{2}} = \sqrt{3a^{2}} = a\sqrt{3}$$

Memorise that one. Cube of side $5$ has space diagonal $5\sqrt{3} \approx 8.66$. Cube of side $10$ has space diagonal $10\sqrt{3} \approx 17.32$. Saves time when a Cambridge question uses a cube.

## Common mistakes

- **Computing the face diagonal when the question wants the space diagonal**, or vice versa. Re-read carefully: "from one corner to the opposite corner" usually means the space diagonal.
- **Sign errors when reverse-solving**: $h^{2} = d^{2} - (\text{base diag})^{2}$, not the other way around. The space diagonal is always the longest.
- **Forgetting to take the square root.** The formula gives $d^{2}$; the final step is $\sqrt{\;\;}$.
- **Mixing units.** All three dimensions need the same unit before squaring.

## Quick summary

- Face diagonal (across one face): $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}$.
- Space diagonal (corner to opposite corner through the interior): $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}+h^{2}}$.
- Two-step picture: base diagonal first, then up to the top corner.
- For a cube of side $a$: space diagonal $= a\sqrt{3}$.
- Reverse problems: rearrange $h^{2} = d^{2} - (\text{base diag})^{2}$ to find a missing dimension.
