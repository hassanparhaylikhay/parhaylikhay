## Why this matters

Cambridge 3D questions look intimidating but they all use the same two tools you already know: **Pythagoras** and **SOH-CAH-TOA**. The new skill is **spotting the right 2D triangle hidden inside the 3D shape**. Once you can find it, you draw it flat on the page, label the sides, and solve like any 2D right-triangle problem.

This part is the bridge. The next three parts apply it to specific 3D shapes: cuboids, then pyramids, then Cambridge-style multi-step problems.

## The new tool: 3D Pythagoras

Every 3D Pythagoras problem is really just **two ordinary 2D Pythagoras problems stacked**. Try the widget below. Switch between the modes:

In **base diagonal** mode, the green line is the diagonal across the bottom face. That's plain 2D Pythagoras: $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}$.

Switch to **space diagonal** mode. The yellow line is the corner-to-corner diagonal of the whole cuboid. To find its length:

1. **First Pythagoras** finds the base diagonal across the bottom face (the green dashed line).
2. **Second Pythagoras** combines that base diagonal with the vertical edge to get the space diagonal.

The result simplifies to one clean formula:

$$d = \sqrt{l^{2} + w^{2} + h^{2}}$$

You can drag the cuboid to rotate it. Try it from different angles, the diagonal looks different but its length doesn't change.

## The other tool: angle between a line and a plane

When a 3D question asks "find the angle that this line makes with the base", the trick is the same: drop a perpendicular, find the right 2D triangle, then use sin/cos/tan.

Switch the widget above to **line-plane angle** mode. The yellow line is the space diagonal, the green dashed line is its **projection** onto the base (the diagonal's "shadow" if light were shining straight down), and the pink dashed line is the vertical drop between them. Those three lines form a right triangle in a vertical plane.

The angle $\theta$ between the space diagonal and the base is the angle at the bottom vertex of this triangle:

$$\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{vertical drop}}{\text{projection}} = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}}$$

So you can take inverse tan of that ratio to get the angle. That's the entire technique.

The "drawing it flat" step is the one students skip and regret. Always draw the 2D triangle separately. It costs five seconds and saves you from confusing your own diagram.

## A simple example: the cuboid space diagonal

A cuboid is $6$ cm long, $4$ cm wide, and $3$ cm tall. Find the length of its space diagonal.

Using the formula directly:

$$d = \sqrt{6^{2} + 4^{2} + 3^{2}} = \sqrt{36 + 16 + 9} = \sqrt{61} \approx 7.81\;\text{cm}$$

But if you forget the formula on the day, build it step by step:

- Base diagonal first: $\sqrt{6^{2} + 4^{2}} = \sqrt{52}$.
- Then space diagonal: $\sqrt{(\sqrt{52})^{2} + 3^{2}} = \sqrt{52 + 9} = \sqrt{61} \approx 7.81$.

Same answer. The second approach is safer because each step is plain 2D Pythagoras.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the flat sketch.** A 3D diagram is hard to label cleanly. Draw the 2D triangle separately, labelled, before substituting numbers.
- **Forgetting which side is which in the inner triangle.** For "angle with the base", the projection is adjacent, the vertical drop is opposite, the 3D line is the hypotenuse.
- **Adding $h$ in the wrong place.** $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}+h^{2}}$ is the space diagonal. $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}$ is just the base diagonal. Different problems want different things.
- **Mistaking the apex's perpendicular foot.** In a right pyramid the apex sits above the **centre** of the base, not above a corner.

## Quick summary

- Every 3D problem reduces to one or more 2D right triangles. The skill is spotting them.
- 3D Pythagoras for a cuboid space diagonal: $d = \sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}+h^{2}}$.
- Angle between a 3D line and a plane: project the line onto the plane, then use the right triangle formed by the line, its projection, and the perpendicular drop.
- Always draw the flat 2D triangle on rough paper before substituting.
