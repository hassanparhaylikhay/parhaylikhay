## The setup

"Find the angle that line $L$ makes with plane $P$" sounds harder than 2D angle problems, but it's not. The trick is to **collapse the 3D into a single 2D right triangle** and then use $\tan$ exactly as you would on a flat page.

The right triangle has three sides:

Then $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{perpendicular drop}}{\text{projection}}$ and $\theta = \tan^{-1}$ of that.

For a cuboid with length $l$, width $w$, height $h$, the angle that the space diagonal makes with the base is:

$$\tan\theta = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}}$$

Read that carefully. The denominator is the **base diagonal** (the projection of the space diagonal onto the bottom face), and the numerator is just the vertical height. Take $\tan^{-1}$ to get $\theta$.

## Worked example: cuboid space diagonal with the base

A cuboid is $6$ cm long, $4$ cm wide, $5$ cm tall. Find the angle that the space diagonal makes with the base.

<iframe src="/widgets/3d-step-explorer.html?preset=cuboid-line-plane-angle" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:600px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Step-by-step:

1. **Project onto the base.** The shadow of the space diagonal is the base diagonal: $\sqrt{6^{2}+4^{2}} = \sqrt{52} \approx 7.21$.
2. **Identify the right triangle.** The space diagonal is the hypotenuse, the base diagonal is adjacent (lying flat in the base plane), and the vertical edge of length $h = 5$ is opposite.
3. **Use tan**: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{5}{\sqrt{52}}$.
4. **Inverse tan**: $\theta = \tan^{-1}(5/\sqrt{52}) \approx 34.7°$.

**[M1 for $\tan\theta = 5/\sqrt{52}$ or equivalent; A1 for $\theta = 34.7°$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Why $\tan$ and not $\sin$ or $\cos$?

You CAN use any of the three. They all give the same angle. But $\tan$ is usually the fastest because you can read the two legs (vertical and projection) directly from the dimensions of the cuboid without taking another square root.

If you want to use $\sin$ instead: $\sin\theta = \dfrac{h}{d}$ where $d$ is the full space diagonal. But you'd have to compute $d$ first via $\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}+h^{2}}$. More work.

Same with $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\text{projection}}{d}$. Two computations.

$\tan$ uses only the two legs of the inner triangle, which come straight from the cuboid's dimensions. Use it whenever possible.

## What "angle with a plane" actually means

A subtle point: the "angle between a line and a plane" is defined as the **smallest** angle between the line and any line in the plane. That happens to always be the angle between the line and its projection (the shadow). So that's why the right-triangle setup gives the correct angle automatically.

If you imagine sweeping out all the lines in the plane that touch the line's foot, the projection is the one that the line makes the smallest angle with. All others make bigger angles.

## A useful constraint: $\theta$ is always between $0°$ and $90°$

The angle between a line and a plane is always acute. If your calculator returns something obtuse, you've taken $\tan$ the wrong way round, the perpendicular drop should be the opposite side, not the adjacent.

If $\theta = 0°$: the line is parallel to (or lying in) the plane.

If $\theta = 90°$: the line is perpendicular to the plane.

For most Cambridge problems, $\theta$ is somewhere in between, typically $20°$ to $60°$.

## Common mistakes

- **Computing $\tan\theta$ with the full space diagonal in the denominator.** It's the **projection** (base diagonal) that goes in the denominator, not the space diagonal itself.
- **Confusing the projection with the line itself.** They are different lengths. The projection is shorter (it's the line tilted down onto the plane).
- **Trying to use a 2D triangle that isn't right-angled.** The inner triangle IS right-angled (the perpendicular drop is at $90°$ to the projection). Always.
- **Dropping the perpendicular from the wrong endpoint.** Drop it from the END of the line that's NOT on the plane.

## Quick summary

- Angle between a line and a plane = angle the line makes with its projection (shadow) onto the plane.
- The three sides of the inner triangle: the line (hypotenuse), the projection (adjacent), the perpendicular drop (opposite).
- Use $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{drop}}{\text{projection}}$, then $\tan^{-1}$.
- For a cuboid's space diagonal with the base: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{l^{2}+w^{2}}}$.
- The angle is always between $0°$ and $90°$.
