## Why this matters

If $1$ kg of mangoes costs Rs. $400$, then $3$ kg costs Rs. $1200$ and $5$ kg costs Rs. $2000$. The cost is **directly proportional** to the weight: every extra kilo adds the same Rs. $400$. Cambridge tests this on Paper 1 ("find the constant of proportionality") and Paper 2 (multi-step word problems), and the language matters: $\propto$ means "is proportional to", and you need to translate that symbol into a clean equation.

## What direct proportion means

Two quantities are **directly proportional** if one is always a constant multiple of the other. Written algebraically:

$$y \;\propto\; x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y = \textcolor{#fff067}{k} \, x$$

where $\textcolor{#fff067}{k}$ is the **constant of proportionality**: the number that's the same no matter which pair of values you check.

If you double $x$, you double $y$. Triple $x$, triple $y$. Half $x$, half $y$. The ratio $\dfrac{y}{x}$ stays equal to $k$ at every pair of values.

## The recipe (3 steps)

To solve any direct-proportion problem:

1. **Translate the proportion statement into an equation** with $k$ as the unknown. For "$y$ is directly proportional to $x$" write $y = kx$.
2. **Substitute the given pair of values** to find $k$.
3. **Use the equation** (with $k$ filled in) to find any other unknown.

**Example.** $y \propto x$. When $x = 3$, $y = 12$. Find $y$ when $x = 8$.

**Step 1.** $y = kx$.

**Step 2.** Substitute $(x, y) = (3, 12)$: $12 = 3k$, so $k = 4$.

**Step 3.** With $k = 4$, the equation is $y = 4x$. At $x = 8$: $y = 4(8) = 32$.

## Variants: square, cube, square root

Direct proportion isn't always linear. The variable on the right can be $x^2$, $x^3$, $\sqrt{x}$, etc. The recipe is identical; only the form changes.

| Statement | Equation | Example $k$-finding |
|---|---|---|
| $y \propto x$ | $y = \textcolor{#fff067}{k} \, x$ | linear |
| $y \propto x^2$ | $y = \textcolor{#fff067}{k} \, x^2$ | square |
| $y \propto x^3$ | $y = \textcolor{#fff067}{k} \, x^3$ | cube |
| $y \propto \sqrt{x}$ | $y = \textcolor{#fff067}{k} \, \sqrt{x}$ | square root |
| $y \propto \sqrt[3]{x}$ | $y = \textcolor{#fff067}{k} \, \sqrt[3]{x}$ | cube root |

**Example.** $y \propto x^2$. When $x = 3$, $y = 18$. Find $y$ when $x = 5$.

**Step 1.** $y = kx^2$.

**Step 2.** $18 = k(3)^2 = 9k$, so $k = 2$.

**Step 3.** $y = 2x^2$. At $x = 5$: $y = 2(25) = 50$.

**Example.** $y \propto \sqrt{x}$. When $x = 4$, $y = 6$. Find $y$ when $x = 9$.

**Step 1.** $y = k\sqrt{x}$.

**Step 2.** $6 = k\sqrt{4} = 2k$, so $k = 3$.

**Step 3.** $y = 3\sqrt{x}$. At $x = 9$: $y = 3\sqrt{9} = 9$.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: read the statement carefully.** "$y$ is proportional to $x$ squared" means $y \propto x^2$, so $y = kx^2$. NOT $y^2 = kx$. The squared (or rooted, or cubed) variable goes on the right side of the proportion.

</div>

## Worked example

The cost $C$ of a square mat is directly proportional to the square of its side length $s$. A mat with side $40$ cm costs Rs. $800$.

(a) Find the constant of proportionality.

(b) How much does a mat with side $50$ cm cost?

**Step 1: Write the equation.** $C = k s^2$.

**Step 2 (a): Substitute the known pair.** $C = 800$, $s = 40$.

$$800 = k (40)^2 = 1600 k$$

$$k = \dfrac{1}{2}$$

**Step 3 (b): Find $C$ at $s = 50$.**

$$C = \dfrac{1}{2} (50)^2 = \dfrac{1}{2} (2500) = 1250$$

**Answer:** **(a)** $\boxed{k = \dfrac{1}{2}}$. **(b)** $\boxed{\text{Rs. } 1250}$. **[M1 for setting up; M1 for $k$; A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting that $\propto$ means "$=$ a constant times". $y \propto x$ does NOT mean $y = x$; it means $y = kx$ for some $k$.
- Putting the squared variable on the wrong side. "$y$ is proportional to the square of $x$" means $y = kx^2$.
- Confusing direct proportion with linear functions $y = mx + b$. Direct proportion has $b = 0$ : the line passes through the origin.
- Forgetting to check units. If $x$ is in cm and $y$ in Rs., $k$ has units of Rs./cm.

## Quick summary

- **Direct proportion**: $y \propto x$ means $y = kx$ for some constant $k$.
- The recipe: write the equation, find $k$ from one pair, then use the equation.
- Variants: $y \propto x^2$, $y \propto x^3$, $y \propto \sqrt{x}$, $y \propto \sqrt[3]{x}$. Same recipe.
- Direct proportion graphs always pass through the **origin**.
