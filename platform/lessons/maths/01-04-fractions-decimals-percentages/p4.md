## Why this matters

Cambridge syllabus 1.4 explicitly names recurring decimals: you must know the **dot notation** and convert between fractions and recurring decimals both ways. This part covers the conventions tightly. The conversion algebra was first introduced back in 1.1; here we lock in the notation and the recall list.

## Dot notation, the Cambridge convention

A **single dot** above a digit means *that digit repeats forever*.

$$0.\dot{3} \;=\; 0.333\,333\,\ldots$$

When a **block** of digits repeats, dots sit on the **first and last** digits of the block.

Notice the difference between the second and third lines:

That single-dot-versus-bracketing-dots distinction is one of the most common Cambridge traps in this topic. Read carefully.

## Which fractions recur?

A fraction's decimal **terminates** if its simplified denominator's prime factors are only <span style="color:#00abfa;font-weight:600">2</span> and <span style="color:#fff067;font-weight:600">5</span>. Otherwise, it **recurs**.

The $\tfrac{1}{9}$ pattern is especially useful: $\tfrac{n}{9} = 0.\dot{n}$ for any single digit $n$. So $\tfrac{4}{9} = 0.\dot{4}$, $\tfrac{7}{9} = 0.\dot{7}$, etc.

## Recurring decimal → fraction

The algebraic method (covered in detail in 1.1 Part 7): name the decimal $x$, multiply by a power of 10 to shift one full period, subtract the original from the shifted version to cancel the recurring tail, then divide.

**Example.** Convert $0.\dot{6}$ to a fraction.

Let $x = 0.666\ldots$. One digit recurs, so multiply by $10$:

$$10x = 6.666\ldots$$
$$\;\;\;x = 0.666\ldots$$

Subtract:

$$9x = 6 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \tfrac{6}{9} = \tfrac{2}{3}$$

The full method (including mixed cases like $0.1\dot{4}$) is in 1.1 Part 7.

## Fraction → recurring decimal

Long division. Divide top by bottom and watch for a remainder that **repeats**: as soon as a remainder appears for the second time, the digits between the two appearances repeat forever.

**Example.** $\tfrac{1}{6}$:

$$1.000 \div 6 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.1 \text{ remainder } 4 \;\Rightarrow\; 40 \div 6 = 6 \text{ remainder } 4$$

The remainder $4$ has appeared once already, so the digit $6$ repeats forever:

$$\tfrac{1}{6} = 0.1\dot{6}$$

(The 1 doesn't recur because it came from a different remainder. Only the 6 onwards repeats.)

## Worked example

Write $0.\dot{1}\dot{2}$ as a fraction in its simplest form.

**Step 1: Set up.** Let $x = 0.121212\ldots$ (two-digit recurring block).

**Step 2: Multiply by $10^2 = 100$** to shift one full period.

$$100x = 12.121212\ldots$$

**Step 3: Subtract** to cancel the recurring tails.

$$100x - x \;=\; 12.121212\ldots - 0.121212\ldots \;=\; 12$$

$$99x = 12$$

**Step 4: Solve and simplify.**

$$x = \tfrac{12}{99} = \tfrac{4}{33}$$

(HCF of 12 and 99 is 3.)

**Answer:** $\boxed{\tfrac{4}{33}}$. **[M1 for the algebraic setup; A1 for the simplified fraction]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Writing $0.\dot{1}\dot{2}$ as $\tfrac{12}{100}$ instead of $\tfrac{12}{99}$. The $99$ comes from $100x - x$, not from "two decimal places".
- Forgetting to simplify. $\tfrac{12}{99}$ scores fewer marks than $\tfrac{4}{33}$ in most schemes.
- Confusing $0.\dot{1}\dot{2}$ (block "12" repeats) with $0.1\dot{2}$ (only the 2 repeats). They are *different numbers*.
- Treating $\tfrac{1}{7}$ as a "nice" recurring like $\tfrac{1}{3}$. Its block is six digits long: $0.\dot{1}4285\dot{7}$.

## Quick summary

- Single dot: that digit repeats. Two dots: bracket the recurring block.
- A fraction recurs iff its simplified denominator has any prime factor other than 2 or 5.
- Recurring → fraction: name $x$, multiply by $10^n$ for the $n$-digit block, subtract, simplify. (Full method in 1.1 Part 7.)
- Fraction → recurring: long division until a remainder repeats.
