## Why this matters

When a number is rounded, you don't know the exact value, only a range it could fall into. Cambridge tests this directly ("write the lower bound of...") and indirectly (calculations using bounds for things like perimeters, areas, and speeds). Get the rule sharp now and the rest of the topic is mechanical.

## What "to the nearest..." means

A measurement like "$14$ cm to the nearest cm" tells you two things:

1. The actual value is **somewhere in a range**.
2. The range is **half a unit** wide on either side of the stated value.

So "$14$ cm to the nearest cm" means the actual length could be anywhere from **<span style="color:#0fee89">$13.5$ cm</span>** up to (but not quite reaching) **<span style="color:#ff4670">$14.5$ cm</span>**. Anything in that range rounds to $14$.

## The bounds rule

For a value rounded **to the nearest $u$** (where $u$ is the rounding unit, e.g. cm, mm, $0.1$, $0.01$):

$$\text{lower bound} \;=\; \text{stated value} - \tfrac{u}{2}$$

$$\text{upper bound} \;=\; \text{stated value} + \tfrac{u}{2}$$

The size of the rounding unit tells you the half-unit you add or subtract. Some examples:

| Stated | Rounded to nearest | $u/2$ | Lower bound | Upper bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $14$ cm | cm | $0.5$ | $13.5$ | $14.5$ |
| $7.3$ kg | $0.1$ ($1$ d.p.) | $0.05$ | $7.25$ | $7.35$ |
| $4.50$ s | $0.01$ ($2$ d.p.) | $0.005$ | $4.495$ | $4.505$ |
| $250$ m | $10$ | $5$ | $245$ | $255$ |
| $5\,000$ | $1\,000$ | $500$ | $4\,500$ | $5\,500$ |

The pattern: **the half-unit is always half of the rounding precision**. To $1$ d.p. $\to$ half is $0.05$. To nearest hundred $\to$ half is $50$.

## Reading the precision from significant figures

If you're told "$x = 4\,800$ to $2$ s.f.", the rounding unit is the place value of the last significant digit. The 2nd s.f. of $4\,800$ is the $8$, sitting in the hundreds column. So the rounding unit is $100$, and the half-unit is $50$.

- Lower bound: $4\,800 - 50 = 4\,750$
- Upper bound: $4\,800 + 50 = 4\,850$

This generalises: find the place value of the last significant digit, then split that in half.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: the upper bound is "below" the value, not "the highest value that rounds to it".** Strictly, anything above $14.5$ rounds to $15$, but at exactly $14.5$ the convention is to round up. Cambridge accepts $14.5$ as the upper bound. So write the upper bound as $\text{stated} + \tfrac{u}{2}$ and use $\le$ on the lower side, $\lt$ on the upper side: $13.5 \le L \lt 14.5$.

</div>

## How to write a range

Cambridge sometimes asks for the range in inequality form. Use $\le$ on the lower side and $\lt$ on the upper:

$$\text{lower bound} \;\le\; \text{actual value} \;\lt\; \text{upper bound}$$

For "$14$ cm to the nearest cm": $13.5 \le L \lt 14.5$.

In words: at least $13.5$, less than $14.5$.

## Worked example

A book has thickness $T = 2.4$ cm correct to $1$ d.p. Write the upper and lower bounds, and state the range using inequality notation.

**Step 1: Identify the rounding unit.** $1$ d.p. $\to$ rounded to nearest $0.1$, so half-unit $= 0.05$.

**Step 2: Compute the bounds.**

$$\text{lower} \;=\; 2.4 - 0.05 \;=\; 2.35 \text{ cm}$$

$$\text{upper} \;=\; 2.4 + 0.05 \;=\; 2.45 \text{ cm}$$

**Step 3: Write as a range.**

$$2.35 \le T \lt 2.45$$

**Answer:** Lower $\boxed{2.35}$, upper $\boxed{2.45}$, range $2.35 \le T \lt 2.45$. **[B1 lower; B1 upper; B1 range]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Using the whole rounding unit instead of half. To nearest cm means $\pm 0.5$ cm, NOT $\pm 1$ cm.
- Mishandling the rounding unit when given d.p. or s.f. counts. $1$ d.p. is nearest $0.1$ (half $= 0.05$); $2$ d.p. is nearest $0.01$ (half $= 0.005$).
- Forgetting the s.f. case. $4\,800$ to $2$ s.f.: the rounding unit is $100$, not $1$.
- Using $\le$ on both sides. Strictly the upper is exclusive: $L \lt 14.5$, not $L \le 14.5$.

## Quick summary

- "To the nearest $u$" means the actual value sits in a range of width $u$ centred on the stated value.
- <b style="color:#0fee89">Lower bound</b> $=$ stated $- \tfrac{u}{2}$.
- <b style="color:#ff4670">Upper bound</b> $=$ stated $+ \tfrac{u}{2}$.
- Inequality form: lower $\le$ actual $\lt$ upper.
