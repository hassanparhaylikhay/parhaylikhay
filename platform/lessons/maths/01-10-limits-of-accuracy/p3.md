## Why this matters

Multiplication and division turn up everywhere in O-Level: areas, volumes, speeds, densities, percentages. Cambridge frequently asks you to find the upper or lower bound of these calculated quantities. The good news: there's a single rule that covers all four arithmetic operations.

## Multiplying: max × max, min × min

For a **product** $a \times b$ where both values are positive, the bounds match naturally:

- **Upper bound** of $a \times b$: $\textcolor{#ff4670}{a_{\max}} \times \textcolor{#ff4670}{b_{\max}}$.
- **Lower bound** of $a \times b$: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a_{\min}} \times \textcolor{#0fee89}{b_{\min}}$.

This is the same intuition as addition: to make the product big, both factors should be big. To make it small, both should be small.

## Dividing: opposite-bound rule (like subtraction)

For a **quotient** $\dfrac{a}{b}$ with both values positive, the bounds **swap on $b$**:

- **Upper bound** of $\dfrac{a}{b}$: $\dfrac{\textcolor{#ff4670}{a_{\max}}}{\textcolor{#0fee89}{b_{\min}}}$.
- **Lower bound** of $\dfrac{a}{b}$: $\dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{a_{\min}}}{\textcolor{#ff4670}{b_{\max}}}$.

Why? To make $\tfrac{a}{b}$ big, $a$ should be big AND $b$ should be small (because dividing by a smaller number makes the result bigger). To make $\tfrac{a}{b}$ small, $a$ should be small AND $b$ should be big.

This is the SAME pattern as subtraction. Whenever the second value is being used "against" the first (subtracted, divided by), use the opposite bound.

## The combined rule for all four operations

Cambridge questions usually involve one of these four. Here's the table you should burn in:

| Operation | Upper bound | Lower bound |
|---|---|---|
| $a + b$ | $a_{\max} + b_{\max}$ | $a_{\min} + b_{\min}$ |
| $a - b$ | $a_{\max} - b_{\min}$ | $a_{\min} - b_{\max}$ |
| $a \times b$ | $a_{\max} \times b_{\max}$ | $a_{\min} \times b_{\min}$ |
| $a \div b$ | $a_{\max} \div b_{\min}$ | $a_{\min} \div b_{\max}$ |

The pattern: **for $+$ and $\times$, both at the same extreme**. For $-$ and $\div$, **opposite extremes on $b$**.

## Worked example: speed

A car covers $D = 250$ m in $T = 12$ s. The distance is correct to the nearest metre and the time to the nearest second. Find the upper bound of the speed.

**Step 1: Identify the bounds.**

- $D_{\min} = 249.5$, $D_{\max} = 250.5$
- $T_{\min} = 11.5$, $T_{\max} = 12.5$

**Step 2: Apply the quotient rule** for upper bound.

$$\text{speed}_{\max} \;=\; \dfrac{D_{\max}}{T_{\min}} \;=\; \dfrac{250.5}{11.5}$$

**Step 3: Compute.** $\dfrac{250.5}{11.5} = 21.78\ldots$ m/s.

**Answer:** $\boxed{21.8}$ m/s (to $3$ s.f.). **[M1 for using the right bounds; A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: area

A square has side $s = 4.7$ cm to $1$ d.p. Find the lower bound of its area.

**Step 1: Bounds.** Half-unit $0.05$. $s_{\min} = 4.65$, $s_{\max} = 4.75$.

**Step 2: Apply the product rule.** Area $= s \times s$, so use both at min.

$$A_{\min} \;=\; 4.65^2 \;=\; 21.6225 \text{ cm}^2$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{21.6225}$ cm² (or $21.6$ to $3$ s.f.). **[M1 for using $s_{\min}^2$; A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 2$.

**Trap: a quotient flips the second-value bound, just like subtraction.** The classic exam mistake: "upper bound of speed = $\dfrac{D_{\max}}{T_{\max}}$". WRONG: the time should be at its MIN, not max. Big distance over short time gives the biggest speed.

## Common mistakes

- Using same-extreme bounds for division. $\dfrac{a}{b}$ upper is $\dfrac{a_{\max}}{b_{\min}}$, NOT $\dfrac{a_{\max}}{b_{\max}}$.
- Forgetting the rule applies to formulae like Area $= L \times W$, Speed $= \tfrac{D}{T}$, Density $= \tfrac{m}{V}$. Identify which operation is in play, then apply the rule.
- For squared values like $s^2$, treating only one $s$ as variable. $s^2 = s \times s$, so both factors are at the same extreme.
- Mixing operations carelessly. For $\tfrac{a + b}{c}$ upper bound: numerator at MAX (use $a_{\max} + b_{\max}$), denominator at MIN ($c_{\min}$). Combine the rules step by step.

## Quick summary

- <b style="color:#0fee89">Same extreme</b> for $+$ and $\times$.
- <b style="color:#ff4670">Opposite extreme on $b$</b> for $-$ and $\div$.
- For squared/cubed values, both copies of the variable take the same extreme.
- For mixed expressions, apply the rule operation by operation.
