## Why this matters

Cambridge always asks at least one question that hides a scale factor inside an area or volume problem. Two similar prisms with volumes given, find a length. Two similar cones with surface areas given, find a length. Or vice versa. The students who score full marks are the ones who remember a single rule:

- Lengths scale by $k$.
- Areas scale by $k^2$.
- Volumes scale by $k^3$.

If you forget the squaring or cubing, you lose every mark. If you square or cube the wrong way, you lose every mark. So learn the rule and learn how to switch between the three scale factors confidently.

## The three scale factors

Suppose two shapes are similar with **length scale factor** $k$. Then:

| Quantity | Scale factor |
|---|---|
| Length (side, perimeter, height, radius, diagonal) | $k$ |
| Area (face area, surface area, cross-section area) | $k^2$ |
| Volume (or capacity, or mass if the material is the same) | $k^3$ |

The official Cambridge syllabus states the volume relationship as

$$\dfrac{\text{Vol}_A}{\text{Vol}_B} \;=\; \left(\dfrac{\text{Len}_A}{\text{Len}_B}\right)^3$$

and the area version is the same idea with a square instead of a cube.

## Why squares and cubes?

A simple example. A square of side $1$ has area $1$. Double the side to $2$. Now the area is $4$, not $2$. The shape grew in two directions at once, so the area picks up a factor of $k$ twice over, giving $k^2$.

## Going forward and going backward

Going forward (length given, area or volume wanted): square or cube the length scale factor.

$$\text{new area} = k^2 \times \text{old area}, \qquad \text{new volume} = k^3 \times \text{old volume}$$

Going backward (area or volume given, length wanted): take the square root or cube root.

$$k = \sqrt{\dfrac{\text{new area}}{\text{old area}}}, \qquad k = \sqrt[3]{\dfrac{\text{new volume}}{\text{old volume}}}$$

## Similar cones and cylinders

The rule applies to all similar solids. For two similar cones (or cylinders, or pyramids), if the ratio of any pair of corresponding lengths is $k$, every length scales by $k$, every area by $k^2$, every volume by $k^3$.

## Worked example

Two similar containers are made of the same plastic. The smaller has volume $250$ cm$^3$ and surface area $100$ cm$^2$. The larger has volume $2\,000$ cm$^3$. Find the surface area of the larger container.

**Step 1: Find the volume ratio and use it to find the length scale factor.**

$$\dfrac{V_{\text{large}}}{V_{\text{small}}} \;=\; \dfrac{2\,000}{250} \;=\; 8$$

$$k^3 = 8, \qquad k = \sqrt[3]{8} = 2$$

**[M1 for taking the cube root of the volume ratio]**

**Step 2: Convert the length scale factor into the area scale factor.**

$$k^2 = 2^2 = 4$$

**Step 3: Apply the area scale factor to the smaller surface area.**

$$\text{new surface area} = k^2 \times \text{old surface area} = 4 \times 100 = 400$$

**[M1 for using $k^2$ on the area]**

**Answer:** Surface area of the larger container $= 400$ cm$^2$. **[A1]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## A second worked example: working backward

Two similar trophies are similar. The smaller is $12$ cm tall and weighs $108$ g. The larger weighs $364.5$ g. Both are solid and made of the same metal, so weight behaves like volume. Find the height of the larger trophy.

**Step 1: Use the weight (volume) ratio to find $k$.**

$$\dfrac{V_{\text{large}}}{V_{\text{small}}} \;=\; \dfrac{364.5}{108} \;=\; 3.375$$

$$k = \sqrt[3]{3.375} = 1.5$$

**Step 2: Apply $k$ to the length.**

$$\text{new height} = k \times \text{old height} = 1.5 \times 12 = 18$$

**Answer:** Height of the larger trophy $= 18$ cm.

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to square or cube. Using $k$ where you should use $k^2$ or $k^3$ is the single biggest mark-loser in this topic.
- Cubing in the wrong direction. If you cube-rooted a volume ratio of $\dfrac{8}{27}$, the length scale factor is $\dfrac{2}{3}$, not $\dfrac{27}{8}$. Match small-to-large with small-to-large.
- Mixing up area and volume. Read the question carefully: surface area uses $k^2$, volume uses $k^3$.
- Not noticing a question is about similar shapes. Phrases like "mathematically similar", "two similar prisms", or "a scale model of" are the trigger words.
- Treating mass or paint coverage incorrectly. Same material implies mass scales like volume ($k^3$). Paint or wallpaper covers area, so it scales like $k^2$.

## Quick summary

- Length: $k$. Area: $k^2$. Volume: $k^3$.
- Going forward: multiply by $k$, $k^2$, or $k^3$.
- Going backward: square root for length from area, cube root for length from volume.
- Same material: mass scales like volume.
- Paint coverage: scales like area.
