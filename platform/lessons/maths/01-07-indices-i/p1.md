## Why this matters

Indices are shorthand for repeated multiplication. They show up everywhere in O-Level: standard form (1.8), surds (1.18), exponential growth (1.17), area/volume scale factors (4.4), and most of Unit 2 algebra. Get the three core rules automatic and you save yourself huge amounts of working time.

## What an index actually means

In an expression like **<span style="color:#fff067">5</span>**$^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{3}}$, the **<span style="color:#fff067">5</span>** is the **base** and the $\textcolor{#00abfa}{3}$ is the **index** (also called the **power** or **exponent**). The index tells you how many copies of the base to multiply together.

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{5}^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{3}} \;=\; \textcolor{#fff067}{5} \times \textcolor{#fff067}{5} \times \textcolor{#fff067}{5} \;=\; 125$$

Cambridge expects you to recall **squares** $1^2$ to $15^2$ and **cubes** $1^3$, $2^3$, $3^3$, $4^3$, $5^3$, $10^3$ from memory. Memorising them stops you from wasting time on arithmetic in exam questions.

## The three rules

Almost every indices question reduces to one of these three rules. They only work when the **bases are the same**.

Why these rules work, in one line: a power is just repeated multiplication, so multiplying $\textcolor{#fff067}{a}^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{m}} \times \textcolor{#fff067}{a}^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$ glues $\textcolor{#00abfa}{m}$ copies of $\textcolor{#fff067}{a}$ next to $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ more, giving $\textcolor{#00abfa}{m+n}$ copies.

## Special index: zero

Anything (except $0$ itself) raised to the power $0$ equals $\textcolor{#0fee89}{1}$:

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{a}^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{0}} \;=\; \textcolor{#0fee89}{1}$$

The reason: by Rule 2, $\tfrac{a^m}{a^m} = a^{m-m} = a^0$. But $\tfrac{a^m}{a^m}$ is just a number divided by itself, which is $1$. So $a^0$ has to equal $1$ for the rules to be consistent.

So $7^0 = 1$, $(-3)^0 = 1$, $(\tfrac{1}{2})^0 = 1$. Cambridge will slip this in as a one-mark check.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: the rules need the SAME base.** $2^3 \times 3^2$ does NOT simplify to $6^5$ or anything else neat. Different bases ($2$ and $3$) means you have to compute each side: $8 \times 9 = 72$. Don't add indices when the bases are different.

</div>

## Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{4^3 \times 4^5}{4^2}$, leaving your answer as a power of $4$.

**Step 1: Top using Rule 1.** Same base, multiply, so add the indices.

$$4^3 \times 4^5 \;=\; 4^{3+5} \;=\; 4^8$$

**Step 2: Apply Rule 2 with the bottom.** Same base, divide, so subtract.

$$\dfrac{4^8}{4^2} \;=\; 4^{8-2} \;=\; 4^6$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{4^6}$. **[M1 for combining the top correctly; A1 for the final index]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying the bases together. $3^4 \times 3^2 = 9^6$. Wrong: keep the base, ADD the indices: $3^6$.
- Adding indices when the bases differ. $2^3 \times 3^2 \neq 6^5$. Different bases means you must compute each side numerically.
- Confusing Rules 1 and 3. $a^m \times a^n$ ADDS indices; $(a^m)^n$ MULTIPLIES them. Read the operation carefully.
- Forgetting that $a^0 = 1$. Especially in expressions like $7^2 \times 7^0$, where the $7^0$ silently equals $1$ and just leaves $7^2$.

## Quick summary

- A power $\textcolor{#fff067}{a}^{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$ means $\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}$ copies of $\textcolor{#fff067}{a}$ multiplied together.
- <b style="color:#00abfa">Rule 1</b>: $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$ (multiply $\to$ add).
- <b style="color:#0fee89">Rule 2</b>: $a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n}$ (divide $\to$ subtract).
- <b style="color:#fff067">Rule 3</b>: $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ (power of power $\to$ multiply).
- $a^0 = 1$ for any non-zero $a$.
- All three rules need the same base. Different bases means compute numerically.
