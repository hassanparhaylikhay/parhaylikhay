## Why this matters

If you have $6$ samosas and $9$ jalebis to share equally between $3$ people, each person gets $2$ samosas and $3$ jalebis. Written out: $6 + 9 = 3(2 + 3)$. You just **factored out** the $3$ from a sum. **Factorising** in algebra is exactly this idea applied to expressions with letters: spot the common piece, pull it out front, leave what remains inside the bracket.

Factorising is the reverse of expanding (2.2 p2). Cambridge tests three forms: pulling out a common factor, factorising by **grouping** when there are four terms, and recognising the two special patterns (**difference of two squares** and **perfect square**). Get fluent with all three and Paper 1's algebra section becomes routine.

## The reverse of expanding

Expanding $3(2x + 3)$ gives $6x + 9$. Factorising $6x + 9$ takes us back to $3(2x + 3)$. Same identity, two directions:

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{6x + 9} \;\xleftrightarrow{\text{factorise}}\; \textcolor{#0fee89}{3(2x + 3)}$$

Whenever you've factorised something, expand it back to check. If the expansion gives the original expression, you've factorised correctly.

## Common factor: pull out the biggest piece

Find the **highest common factor (HCF)** of every term, then pull it out front. The "factor" can be a number, a letter, or both.

**Number-only HCF.**

$$6x + 9 \;=\; 3(2x + 3) \qquad \text{HCF of } 6, 9 \text{ is } 3$$

$$10a + 15b \;=\; 5(2a + 3b)$$

$$12y - 8 \;=\; 4(3y - 2)$$

**Letter HCF.** If every term contains the same letter, that letter is part of the common factor.

$$x^2 + 7x \;=\; x(x + 7)$$

$$3a^2 - 6a \;=\; 3a(a - 2)$$

$$5x^2 + 10xy \;=\; 5x(x + 2y) \qquad \text{HCF: } 5 \text{ and } x$$

The rule: take the **lowest power** of each shared letter. $x^2$ and $x$ share $x$ (the lowest). $a^3$ and $a^2$ share $a^2$.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: factor FULLY.** $5x^2 + 10xy = 5(x^2 + 2xy)$ is a partial factorisation. The full factorisation is $5x(x + 2y)$, because $x$ is also common to both terms inside. Cambridge marks the FULL form as correct; partial gets only an M1.

</div>

## Difference of two squares (DOTS)

The pattern:

$$a^2 - b^2 \;=\; (a - b)(a + b)$$

This is one of the most-tested factorisations in O-level. Whenever you see "a square minus another square", apply it directly.

**Examples.**

- $x^2 - 9 = x^2 - 3^2 = (x-3)(x+3)$
- $a^2 - 16 = (a-4)(a+4)$
- $25 - y^2 = 5^2 - y^2 = (5-y)(5+y)$
- $4x^2 - 9 = (2x)^2 - 3^2 = (2x-3)(2x+3)$

The visual: a square of side $a$ with a smaller square of side $b$ removed leaves an L-shape. Cut and rearrange the L into a rectangle of dimensions $(a-b)$ by $(a+b)$. Same area, written two ways.

## Perfect square: $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = (a + b)^2$

The other named pattern. Whenever you see a quadratic that looks like (something)² + (twice the cross product) + (something else)², it factors as $(a+b)^2$ or $(a-b)^2$.

$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \;=\; (a + b)^2$$

$$a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \;=\; (a - b)^2$$

**Examples.**

- $x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2$ (because $2 \cdot x \cdot 3 = 6x$ matches)
- $x^2 - 10x + 25 = (x - 5)^2$
- $4x^2 + 12x + 9 = (2x + 3)^2$ (because $a = 2x$, $b = 3$, $2ab = 12x$ matches)

The check: square the proposed bracket and see if you get the original. $(x+3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$ ✓.

## Grouping: when there's no single common factor across all terms

Four-term expressions where the four terms split into two pairs, each pair sharing a factor.

$$ax + bx + ay + by$$

Group the first two and the last two:

$$x(a + b) + y(a + b)$$

Now $(a + b)$ is a common factor of both pieces. Pull it out:

$$(a + b)(x + y)$$

The factorised form. The "magic" is choosing the right grouping so the inside-bracket pieces match.

**Example.** Factorise $2x + 6 + xy + 3y$.

Group: $(2x + 6) + (xy + 3y)$.

- $2x + 6 = 2(x + 3)$
- $xy + 3y = y(x + 3)$

Now $(x + 3)$ is common: $(x + 3)(2 + y)$.

If your first grouping doesn't reveal a common bracket, try a different pairing.

## Worked example

Factorise fully: $5x^2 - 20$.

**Step 1: Look for a common factor first.** Both terms share $5$.

$$5x^2 - 20 \;=\; 5(x^2 - 4)$$

**Step 2: Look at what's inside the bracket.** $x^2 - 4$ is a difference of two squares.

$$x^2 - 4 \;=\; (x - 2)(x + 2)$$

**Step 3: Combine.**

$$5x^2 - 20 \;=\; 5(x - 2)(x + 2)$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{5(x - 2)(x + 2)}$. **[M1 for the common factor; A1 for the DOTS step]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at a partial factorisation. $5x^2 + 10xy = 5(x^2 + 2xy)$ is incomplete; the full form is $5x(x + 2y)$.
- Misreading DOTS. $a^2 + b^2$ does NOT factorise (sum of squares does not factor in real numbers). Only the difference does.
- Forgetting to look for a common factor before applying DOTS. $5x^2 - 20 = 5(x-2)(x+2)$, NOT $(5x-20)(x+1)$ or similar.
- Errors with signs in grouping. $ax - bx + ay - by$ groups as $x(a-b) + y(a-b) = (a-b)(x+y)$. Watch the signs inside each pair.

## Quick summary

- **Common factor**: take the HCF of all coefficients and the lowest power of every shared letter. Pull it out front.
- **DOTS**: $a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$. Only differences, not sums.
- **Perfect square**: $a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2 = (a \pm b)^2$.
- **Grouping**: pair the four terms, factor each pair, then pull out the common bracket.
- Always factor **FULLY**. Check by expanding back.
