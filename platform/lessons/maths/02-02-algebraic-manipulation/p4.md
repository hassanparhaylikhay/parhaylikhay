## Why this matters

Suppose the area of a rectangular plot is given by $x^2 + 7x + 12$ square metres. The factorisation $(x+3)(x+4)$ tells you the rectangle is $(x+3)$ by $(x+4)$. **Factorising a quadratic** is exactly that: finding the two pieces that multiply together to give the expression.

You'll meet quadratics throughout O-level: solving equations (2.5), sketching curves (2.11), function problems (2.12), areas in mensuration. Factorising is the fastest way in. Cambridge tests it on Paper 1 directly and uses it as a step inside longer Paper 2 questions. The patterns are limited and follow a clean recipe.

## The shape of a quadratic

A **quadratic** in $x$ has the form

$$ax^2 + bx + c$$

where $a, b, c$ are constants and $a \ne 0$. We've already seen one quadratic factorisation pattern (DOTS in p3). This part covers the general case: factorising into two brackets $(x + p)(x + q)$ or $(\alpha x + p)(\beta x + q)$.

## When $a = 1$: find two numbers

For $x^2 + bx + c$ (the leading coefficient is $1$), find **two numbers** $p$ and $q$ that:

- multiply to $c$
- add to $b$

Then $x^2 + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q)$.

**Why this works.** Expanding $(x + p)(x + q)$ gives $x^2 + (p+q)x + pq$. Matching coefficients with $x^2 + bx + c$ forces $p + q = b$ and $pq = c$. Find the pair, you've factorised.

**Example.** Factorise $x^2 + 7x + 12$.

Look for two numbers that multiply to $12$ and add to $7$. List the factor pairs of $12$: $1 \cdot 12$, $2 \cdot 6$, $3 \cdot 4$. The pair $3$ and $4$ adds to $7$. Done.

$$x^2 + 7x + 12 \;=\; (x + 3)(x + 4)$$

## Signs of the pair: read off from $b$ and $c$

Watch the signs of $c$ (the constant) and $b$ (the middle coefficient):

| sign of $c$ | sign of $b$ | $p$ and $q$ |
|---|---|---|
| $+$ | $+$ | both positive |
| $+$ | $-$ | both negative |
| $-$ | $+$ | one positive, one negative; positive bigger |
| $-$ | $-$ | one positive, one negative; negative bigger |

**Example.** Factorise $x^2 - 5x + 6$. $c = +6$, $b = -5$. Both negative: $-2$ and $-3$. Check: $(-2)(-3) = +6$ ✓, $(-2) + (-3) = -5$ ✓. So $(x-2)(x-3)$.

**Example.** Factorise $x^2 + 2x - 15$. $c = -15$, $b = +2$. Opposite signs, positive bigger: $+5$ and $-3$. Check: $(+5)(-3) = -15$ ✓, $5 + (-3) = +2$ ✓. So $(x+5)(x-3)$.

## When $a \ne 1$: split the middle term (the AC method)

For $ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \ne 1$, the simple "two-numbers" trick needs adjustment. The cleanest method is:

1. Multiply $a$ and $c$. Call the result $ac$.
2. Find two numbers that **multiply to $ac$** and **add to $b$**.
3. **Split the middle term** $bx$ into those two numbers as $x$-coefficients.
4. **Factor by grouping** (as in p3).

**Example.** Factorise $2x^2 + 7x + 3$.

**Step 1.** $a = 2$, $c = 3$, so $ac = 6$.

**Step 2.** Two numbers that multiply to $6$ and add to $7$: $1$ and $6$.

**Step 3.** Split: $2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 2x^2 + x + 6x + 3$.

**Step 4.** Group:

$$\underbrace{2x^2 + x}_{= x(2x + 1)} \;+\; \underbrace{6x + 3}_{= 3(2x + 1)}$$

$$= \;x(2x + 1) + 3(2x + 1) \;=\; (2x + 1)(x + 3)$$

Check by expanding: $(2x+1)(x+3) = 2x^2 + 6x + x + 3 = 2x^2 + 7x + 3$ ✓.

The AC method always works. With practice, you'll often "spot" the brackets by inspection for small coefficients, but the AC method is the reliable backup.

## Always check for a common factor first

Before applying any quadratic-factorising method, scan for a common factor across all terms.

**Example.** Factorise $3x^2 + 12x + 9$.

Common factor $3$: $3(x^2 + 4x + 3)$. Now factor the inside: two numbers multiplying to $3$, adding to $4$, namely $1$ and $3$. So $3(x + 1)(x + 3)$.

If you'd skipped the common factor and gone straight to AC, the numbers would be larger and the factorisation more error-prone.

## Cubics with a common $x$

Some Paper 1 questions look cubic but really hide a quadratic.

**Example.** Factorise $x^3 - 4x$ fully.

**Step 1: Common factor $x$.**

$$x^3 - 4x \;=\; x(x^2 - 4)$$

**Step 2: Recognise DOTS on $x^2 - 4$.**

$$x^2 - 4 \;=\; (x - 2)(x + 2)$$

**Combined:** $x^3 - 4x = x(x - 2)(x + 2)$.

**Example.** Factorise $2x^3 - 6x^2 - 8x$.

Common factor $2x$: $2x(x^2 - 3x - 4)$. Then factor the quadratic: numbers multiplying to $-4$, adding to $-3$: $-4$ and $1$. So $2x(x - 4)(x + 1)$.

**Trap: factor a common $x$ before quadratic-style methods.** $x^3 + 5x^2 + 6x$ is NOT a quadratic; you can't split-the-middle-term it directly. Pull out $x$ first to get $x(x^2 + 5x + 6) = x(x+2)(x+3)$.

## Worked example

Factorise fully: $x^2 - 5x + 6$.

**Step 1: Identify $b$ and $c$.** $b = -5$, $c = +6$.

**Step 2: Find the two numbers.** Multiply to $+6$, add to $-5$. Both negative: $-2$ and $-3$. Check: $(-2)(-3) = 6$ ✓ and $(-2) + (-3) = -5$ ✓.

**Step 3: Write the factorisation.**

$$x^2 - 5x + 6 \;=\; (x - 2)(x - 3)$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{(x - 2)(x - 3)}$. **[B1]** for $1$ mark, or **[M1 for the pair; A1 for the brackets]** if working is required.

Full marks $= 1$ to $2$ depending on the question.

## Common mistakes

- Sign errors in the pair. For $x^2 - 5x + 6$, the pair is $-2, -3$, not $2, 3$. Always double-check by expanding.
- Forgetting to factor a common piece first. $3x^2 + 12x + 9$ should start with the common $3$.
- Confusing the AC method's split. After finding the two numbers, split the middle term and group; don't try to "guess" the brackets directly with $a \ne 1$.
- Missing DOTS hidden inside a cubic. $x^3 - 9x = x(x^2 - 9) = x(x-3)(x+3)$. The middle step is essential.

## Quick summary

- $x^2 + bx + c$: find two numbers multiplying to $c$, adding to $b$. Brackets are $(x + p)(x + q)$.
- Sign pattern: $c > 0$ and $b > 0$ → both positive; $c > 0$ and $b < 0$ → both negative; $c < 0$ → opposite signs.
- $ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \ne 1$: AC method. Split middle term, factor by grouping.
- Always check for a **common factor first** (especially for cubics).
- Verify any factorisation by expanding back to the original.
