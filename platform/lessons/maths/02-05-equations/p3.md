## Why this matters

Two friends together earn Rs. $5\,000$ in a day. One earns Rs. $1\,000$ more than the other. Find each person's earnings. You'd write:

$$x + y = 5000, \qquad x - y = 1000$$

Two equations, two unknowns. **Simultaneous equations**: a pair of equations that must hold at the same time. Cambridge tests these directly on Paper 1 (algebraic) and Paper 2 (in word problems and graph contexts).

The geometric picture is what makes these click: each linear equation in two unknowns is a **line on the coordinate plane**. The solution to a pair of simultaneous equations is the **point where the two lines cross**.

## Three solving methods

### 1. Elimination (the workhorse)

When the two equations have **opposite coefficients** on one variable (or you can scale them so they do), **add or subtract** the equations to eliminate that variable.

**Example.** $x + y = 7$ and $x - y = 1$.

The $y$-coefficients are $+1$ and $-1$. **Add** the equations:

$$(x + y) + (x - y) \;=\; 7 + 1$$

$$2x = 8 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 4$$

Substitute back: $4 + y = 7$, so $y = 3$. **Solution: $(4, 3)$**.

### 2. Scaling, then elimination

When coefficients aren't already opposite, **multiply** one (or both) equations to make them so.

**Example.** $2x + 3y = 13$ and $4x - y = 5$.

Multiply the second by $3$ to make the $y$-coefficients $+3$ and $-3$:

$$2x + 3y = 13$$
$$12x - 3y = 15$$

Add: $14x = 28$, so $x = 2$. Substitute: $4(2) - y = 5$, so $y = 3$. **Solution: $(2, 3)$**.

### 3. Substitution

When one equation has a variable already isolated (or easily isolated), **substitute** that expression into the other.

**Example.** $y = 3x - 1$ and $2x + y = 9$.

Substitute $y = 3x - 1$ into the second equation:

$$2x + (3x - 1) \;=\; 9$$

$$5x - 1 \;=\; 9$$

$$x = 2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = 3(2) - 1 = 5$$

**Solution: $(2, 5)$**.

Pick whichever method produces the cleanest arithmetic for that pair.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: substitute back to find the OTHER variable.** Once you've found $x$, you still need $y$ (or vice versa). A solution to a pair of simultaneous equations is a **pair** of values, not just one. Don't stop at $x = 4$; keep going until you have $(x, y)$.

</div>

## Three possible outcomes

A pair of two-variable linear equations has exactly one of three outcomes:

| Geometric picture | Solutions |
|---|---|
| Two distinct, non-parallel lines (cross at one point) | exactly one $(x, y)$ |
| Two parallel lines (same slope, different intercept) | no solution |
| The same line (one equation is a multiple of the other) | infinitely many |

The "one solution" case is what Cambridge tests almost exclusively. The other two are recognised on inspection.

## Worked example

Solve simultaneously: $3x + 2y = 13$ and $x - y = 1$.

**Step 1: Choose a method.** The second equation rearranges to $x = y + 1$, perfect for substitution.

**Step 2: Substitute $x = y + 1$ into the first equation.**

$$3(y + 1) + 2y \;=\; 13$$

**Step 3: Expand and solve.**

$$3y + 3 + 2y \;=\; 13$$

$$5y \;=\; 10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = 2$$

**Step 4: Find $x$.**

$$x \;=\; y + 1 \;=\; 3$$

**Step 5: Check both equations.** $3(3) + 2(2) = 13$ ✓ and $3 - 2 = 1$ ✓.

**Answer:** $\boxed{x = 3, \;y = 2}$. **[M1 for substitution; M1 for solving for one variable; A1 for the pair]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Word-problem flavour

**Example.** Two pencils and three erasers cost Rs. $50$. Three pencils and one eraser cost Rs. $40$. Find the price of each.

Let $p$ = price of a pencil, $e$ = price of an eraser.

$$2p + 3e = 50, \qquad 3p + e = 40$$

Solve by elimination. Multiply the second equation by $3$:

$$2p + 3e = 50$$
$$9p + 3e = 120$$

Subtract: $7p = 70$, so $p = 10$. From the second equation: $30 + e = 40$, so $e = 10$.

So a pencil and an eraser each cost Rs. $10$.

## Common mistakes

- Solving for one variable and stopping. The answer is a **pair** $(x, y)$.
- Subtracting wrong when "adding" is what eliminates. If both $y$-coefficients are $+1$, you must SUBTRACT to eliminate $y$, not add.
- Substituting back into only ONE equation. Always check by plugging into both.
- Forgetting to scale ALL terms when multiplying an equation through by a constant.

## Quick summary

- A pair of linear equations in two unknowns has **one solution** (lines cross), **none** (parallel), or **infinitely many** (same line).
- Three solving methods: **elimination**, **scale-then-eliminate**, **substitution**. Pick the cleanest.
- The solution is always a **pair** $(x, y)$; substitute back to find the second variable.
- Word problems: name the unknowns, write two equations, solve.
