## Why this matters

A pipe fills a tank in $x$ hours; a second pipe fills the same tank in $(x + 2)$ hours. Together they fill it in $\tfrac{x(x+2)}{2x + 2}$ hours. Set this equal to a target time, and you have a **fractional equation**, with the unknown sitting in a denominator. Cambridge tests these on Paper 2, often hidden inside word problems.

The trick: fractional equations look scary but they reduce to linear equations the moment you **clear the fractions**. Multiply both sides by whatever denominator(s) are giving you trouble, and you're back in p1 territory.

## The plan

For any equation with $x$ in a denominator:

1. **Identify the denominators** that contain $x$ (or any term).
2. **Multiply both sides** by each denominator (or by the LCM of all of them).
3. The fractions disappear; what's left is a linear (or sometimes quadratic) equation.
4. Solve as in p1.
5. **Check** the solution doesn't make any original denominator zero.

That last step matters: a value of $x$ that makes a denominator zero is **not allowed**, because dividing by zero is undefined.

## Walk: one fraction, one number

Solve $\dfrac{6}{x} = 2$.

**Step 1: Multiply both sides by $x$** (clearing the only denominator).

$$\dfrac{6}{x} \times x \;=\; 2 \times x \quad\Rightarrow\quad 6 = 2x$$

**Step 2: Solve the linear equation.**

$$x = 3$$

Check: $\dfrac{6}{3} = 2$ ✓.

## Walk: $x$ in numerator AND denominator

Solve $\dfrac{x}{x - 2} = 3$.

**Step 1: Multiply both sides by $(x - 2)$.**

$$x \;=\; 3(x - 2)$$

**Step 2: Expand.**

$$x \;=\; 3x - 6$$

**Step 3: Collect.**

$$-2x \;=\; -6 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 3$$

Check: $\dfrac{3}{3 - 2} = \dfrac{3}{1} = 3$ ✓.

## Walk: clearing two denominators

Solve $\dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{x}{3} = 5$.

The two denominators are $2$ and $3$. The **LCM is $6$**, so multiply both sides by $6$.

$$6 \times \dfrac{x}{2} + 6 \times \dfrac{x}{3} \;=\; 6 \times 5$$

$$3x + 2x \;=\; 30$$

$$5x = 30 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 6$$

Check: $\dfrac{6}{2} + \dfrac{6}{3} = 3 + 2 = 5$ ✓.

The big trick here is **multiplying every term** by the LCM, including the constant on the right.

## Cross-multiplying: a shortcut for fraction = fraction

When the equation looks like $\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$, the shortcut is to **cross-multiply**:

$$\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad ad = bc$$

This is just multiplying both sides by $bd$ in one step.

**Example.** Solve $\dfrac{2}{x + 2} = \dfrac{1}{x - 1}$.

Cross-multiply:

$$2(x - 1) \;=\; 1(x + 2)$$

$$2x - 2 \;=\; x + 2$$

$$x = 4$$

Check: $\dfrac{2}{4 + 2} = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}$ and $\dfrac{1}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{1}{3}$ ✓.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: multiply the WHOLE side, every term.** $\dfrac{x}{2} + 5 = 7$. Multiplying both sides by $2$ gives $x + 10 = 14$ (not $x + 5 = 14$). The constant term $5$ also gets multiplied by $2$.

</div>

## Walk: two algebraic denominators

Solve $\dfrac{2}{x + 2} + \dfrac{3}{2x - 1} = 1$.

**Step 1: Multiply both sides by both denominators.** That's $(x + 2)(2x - 1)$.

$$2(2x - 1) + 3(x + 2) \;=\; (x + 2)(2x - 1)$$

**Step 2: Expand each piece.**

$$4x - 2 + 3x + 6 \;=\; 2x^2 + 3x - 2$$

$$7x + 4 \;=\; 2x^2 + 3x - 2$$

**Step 3: Bring everything to one side.**

$$0 \;=\; 2x^2 - 4x - 6$$

$$0 \;=\; x^2 - 2x - 3$$

(After dividing both sides by $2$.) That's a quadratic, which we'll factorise in p4. Spoiler: $x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x - 3)(x + 1) = 0$, giving $x = 3$ or $x = -1$.

In Cambridge's hands, fractional equations sometimes "promote" themselves to quadratic equations. That's normal; you just keep going.

## Worked example

Solve $\dfrac{x + 1}{x - 2} = 2$.

**Step 1: Multiply both sides by $(x - 2)$.**

$$x + 1 \;=\; 2(x - 2)$$

**Step 2: Expand.**

$$x + 1 \;=\; 2x - 4$$

**Step 3: Solve.**

$$5 = x$$

**Step 4: Check the denominator isn't zero.** $x - 2 = 5 - 2 = 3 \ne 0$ ✓. Substitute back: $\dfrac{5 + 1}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{6}{3} = 2$ ✓.

**Answer:** $\boxed{x = 5}$. **[M1 for clearing the denominator; A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Dropping the constant term when multiplying through. $\dfrac{x}{3} + 4 = 7$: multiply by $3$ gives $x + 12 = 21$, NOT $x + 4 = 21$.
- Forgetting brackets when multiplying through a sum. $2(x + 1) \ne 2x + 1$.
- Cross-multiplying the wrong way. $\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$ becomes $ad = bc$, NOT $ab = cd$ or $\tfrac{a + d}{b + c}$.
- Accepting a solution that makes a denominator zero. $\dfrac{1}{x - 3} = \dfrac{1}{x - 3}$ has $x \ne 3$ as a constraint; check.

## Quick summary

- Multiply both sides by every $x$-containing denominator (or by the LCM of all denominators) to **clear the fractions**.
- Cross-multiply when the equation is fraction $=$ fraction.
- Always **check** the solution doesn't make any original denominator zero.
- Sometimes the cleared equation is quadratic; head to p4 for the next move.
