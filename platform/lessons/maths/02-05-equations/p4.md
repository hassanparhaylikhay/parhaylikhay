## Why this matters

Throw a cricket ball straight up. The height after $t$ seconds is roughly $h = 20t - 5t^2$. To find when it lands ($h = 0$), you solve $20t - 5t^2 = 0$. That's a **quadratic equation**, where the unknown sits to the second power. Cambridge tests these on Paper 1 (factorising) and Paper 2 (using the formula or completing the square).

A quadratic equation in standard form is

$$a x^2 + b x + c = 0$$

with $a \ne 0$. The graph of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ is a **parabola**, and the solutions of the equation are exactly the **$x$-coordinates where the parabola crosses the $x$-axis** (the **roots**).

When the discriminant is **positive**, the parabola crosses the $x$-axis at two distinct points (two roots). When **zero**, it just touches the axis at one point (one repeated root). When **negative**, it sits entirely above or below the axis (no real roots).

## Three solving methods

Cambridge expects all three. Pick whichever is cleanest for the equation:

1. **Factorising** (when the quadratic factorises with integer coefficients)
2. **Completing the square** (covered in 2.2 p5; also reveals the vertex)
3. **The quadratic formula** (always works, given on the formula sheet)

## Method 1: factorising

If the quadratic factorises as $(x + p)(x + q) = 0$, then the **zero-product rule** says one of the factors must be zero:

$$x + p = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad x + q = 0$$

So $x = -p$ or $x = -q$. Two solutions, immediately.

**Example.** Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$.

Factorise (from 2.2 p4): $(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$.

Set each factor to zero: $x = 2$ or $x = 3$. Two roots. ✓

**Example with DOTS.** Solve $x^2 - 9 = 0$.

Factorise: $(x - 3)(x + 3) = 0$. Roots: $x = \pm 3$.

## Method 2: completing the square

Useful when the quadratic doesn't factorise cleanly, or when Cambridge specifically asks for "exact" answers.

**Example.** Solve $x^2 + 6x + 4 = 0$.

**Step 1: Complete the square** (from 2.2 p5). Halve $b = 6$ to get $3$; square to get $9$.

$$x^2 + 6x + 4 \;=\; (x + 3)^2 - 9 + 4 \;=\; (x + 3)^2 - 5$$

**Step 2: Set the completed form equal to zero.**

$$(x + 3)^2 \;=\; 5$$

**Step 3: Take the square root of both sides.**

$$x + 3 \;=\; \pm \sqrt{5}$$

**Step 4: Solve.**

$$x \;=\; -3 \pm \sqrt{5}$$

Two roots: $-3 + \sqrt{5}$ and $-3 - \sqrt{5}$.

## Method 3: the quadratic formula

For any $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \ne 0$:

$$x \;=\; \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

This **always works**. The expression $b^2 - 4ac$ is called the **discriminant**, and its sign tells you how many real solutions there are:

| Discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ | Picture | Solutions |
|---|---|---|
| $\textcolor{#0fee89}{> 0}$ | parabola crosses $x$-axis at two distinct points | two real roots |
| $\textcolor{#fff067}{= 0}$ | parabola touches $x$-axis at one point | one repeated root |
| $\textcolor{#ff4670}{< 0}$ | parabola misses the $x$-axis entirely | no real roots |

**Example.** Solve $x^2 - 4x + 1 = 0$.

$a = 1, b = -4, c = 1$.

$$b^2 - 4ac \;=\; 16 - 4 \;=\; 12$$

$$x \;=\; \dfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{12}}{2}$$

Simplify the surd: $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$.

$$x \;=\; \dfrac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} \;=\; 2 \pm \sqrt{3}$$

The formula is on Cambridge's formula sheet. You don't need to memorise it, but you should be FAST with it.

**Trap: $x^2 = 9$ has two solutions.** $x = \pm 3$. Don't write only $x = 3$. The square-root step always introduces a $\pm$.

</div>

## Don't forget to set $= 0$ first

The quadratic-formula and factorising recipes only work when the equation is in the form $ax^2 + bx + c = \mathbf{0}$. If the question gives you something like $x^2 = 5x - 4$, **rearrange first**:

$$x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0$$

Now factorise or apply the formula.

## When to use which method

- **Factorise** first if you can spot the factor pairs (always faster).
- **Quadratic formula** if the coefficients are awkward or the roots involve surds.
- **Completing the square** if the question specifically asks for "exact" form or for the vertex of the parabola.

If you're not sure, the **quadratic formula always works**. Use it as a safety net.

## Worked example

Solve $2x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0$.

**Step 1: Try factorising.** AC method (2.2 p4): $a \cdot c = 2 \cdot (-3) = -6$. Need two numbers multiplying to $-6$ and adding to $+5$: that's $+6$ and $-1$.

Split the middle: $2x^2 + 6x - x - 3$.

Group: $2x(x + 3) - 1(x + 3) = (2x - 1)(x + 3)$.

**Step 2: Set each factor to zero.**

$$2x - 1 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = \dfrac{1}{2}$$

$$x + 3 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = -3$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{x = \dfrac{1}{2}}$ or $\boxed{x = -3}$. **[M1 for factorising; A1 for both roots]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at one root. Quadratics have **two** solutions (or one repeated, or none). $\pm$ is essential.
- Forgetting to set the equation to zero before applying the formula. Move every term to one side first.
- Sign errors with the formula. $b^2$ is always positive (squared), but the $-b$ term keeps the sign of $b$. So $-(-4) = +4$.
- Sloppy surd arithmetic. $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$, NOT $\sqrt{12}$ left as is. (See 1.18 for surd simplification.)

## Quick summary

- Standard form: $a x^2 + b x + c = 0$.
- The graph is a **parabola**; solutions are the **$x$-axis crossings**.
- Three methods: **factorise**, **complete the square**, **quadratic formula**.
- The formula always works: $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$.
- The discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ tells you how many real roots: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{>0}$ two, $\textcolor{#fff067}{=0}$ one, $\textcolor{#ff4670}{<0}$ none.
