## Why this matters

You met Venn diagrams in Unit 1.2. Here in 8.3 they show up as a tool for COMBINED-EVENT probabilities. A Venn diagram visualises the relationship between two (or sometimes three) events, and probabilities of "both", "either", "only one of them", and "neither" all read off it directly.

Exam questions of this type are worth $2$ to $4$ marks, often:

- "Use the Venn diagram to find $P(A \cap B)$."
- "Find $P(A \cup B)$."
- "Find $P(A')$, the probability that A does NOT happen."
- "A person is chosen at random. Find the probability they do at least one activity."

This page tightens up the set notation ($\cap$, $\cup$, $A'$) and shows how to read every region quickly.

## Reading the four regions

A standard two-circle Venn has four disjoint regions inside the rectangle (the sample space $\Omega$):

- **$A$ only** = $A \cap B'$ (in $A$, not in $B$)
- **Both** = $A \cap B$
- **$B$ only** = $A' \cap B$
- **Neither** = $A' \cap B'$ = $(A \cup B)'$

To find a probability, count items in the relevant region(s), divide by the total $n(\Omega)$.

**Example**: $20$ students total. $8$ play only cricket, $5$ play both, $4$ play only football, $3$ play neither.

- $P(A) = $ probability of cricket $ = \dfrac{8 + 5}{20} = \dfrac{13}{20}$. The whole $A$ circle is "$A$ only" $+$ "both".
- $P(B) = \dfrac{5 + 4}{20} = \dfrac{9}{20}$.
- $P(A \cap B) = \dfrac{5}{20} = \dfrac{1}{4}$.
- $P(A \cup B) = \dfrac{8 + 5 + 4}{20} = \dfrac{17}{20}$.
- $P(A') = 1 - P(A) = 1 - \dfrac{13}{20} = \dfrac{7}{20}$. Check: outside $A$ = "$B$ only" + "neither" = $4 + 3 = 7$. Same.
- $P(\text{neither}) = \dfrac{3}{20}$.

Each probability is just a count over $20$. The work is figuring out WHICH regions to add.

## Inclusion-exclusion

The key identity:

$$\boxed{P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)}$$

In words: to find the probability that A OR B happens, add $P(A)$ and $P(B)$, then subtract $P(A \cap B)$ once. Why subtract? Because the overlap was counted twice in the sum (once inside $P(A)$, once inside $P(B)$). The subtraction removes the double-counting.

This identity is useful when you do not have a Venn diagram drawn but you know $P(A)$, $P(B)$, and $P(A \cap B)$. It is also the standard way to FIND $P(A \cap B)$ if you know everything else.

## Worked example: read a Venn diagram

In a class of $20$ students, $8$ play only cricket, $5$ play both cricket and football, $4$ play only football, and $3$ play neither. Let $A = $ "plays cricket" and $B = $ "plays football". Find $P(A)$, $P(A \cap B)$, and $P(A \cup B)$.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p4-venn-combined" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:660px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: $P(A) = $ probability of cricket.** The whole $A$ circle holds the $8$ cricket-only students AND the $5$ who play both.

$$P(A) = \dfrac{8 + 5}{20} = \dfrac{13}{20}$$

**Step 2: $P(A \cap B) = $ probability of both.** Only the overlap region counts: $5$ students.

$$P(A \cap B) = \dfrac{5}{20} = \dfrac{1}{4}$$

**Step 3: $P(A \cup B) = $ probability of at least one.** All three inside regions: $8 + 5 + 4 = 17$.

$$P(A \cup B) = \dfrac{17}{20}$$

**Quick check via inclusion-exclusion.** $P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = \dfrac{13}{20} + \dfrac{9}{20} - \dfrac{5}{20} = \dfrac{17}{20}$ $\checkmark$. Matches.

**Answer:** $P(A) = \boxed{\dfrac{13}{20}}$, $P(A \cap B) = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{4}}$, $P(A \cup B) = \boxed{\dfrac{17}{20}}$. **[B1 for each part]**

Full marks $= 3$.

The "$3$ neither" was given but not needed for the three required probabilities. It would be needed for $P(\text{neither}) = \dfrac{3}{20}$, or to confirm the total: $8 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 20$ $\checkmark$.

## Filling in a Venn diagram from totals

Sometimes the question gives you $P(A)$, $P(B)$, and $P(A \cap B)$, and asks you to deduce the four regions. Use inclusion-exclusion in reverse.

**Example**: $30$ people. $P(A) = \dfrac{2}{3}$, $P(B) = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $P(A \cup B) = \dfrac{4}{5}$. Find $P(A \cap B)$, then count each region.

Step 1: $P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = \dfrac{2}{3} + \dfrac{1}{2} - \dfrac{4}{5}$. Common denominator $30$: $\dfrac{20}{30} + \dfrac{15}{30} - \dfrac{24}{30} = \dfrac{11}{30}$.

So in counts: $n(A) = 20$, $n(B) = 15$, $n(A \cap B) = 11$, $n(A \cup B) = 24$.

Step 2: Fill in regions:
- Both: $11$
- $A$ only: $20 - 11 = 9$
- $B$ only: $15 - 11 = 4$
- Neither: $30 - 24 = 6$

Check: $11 + 9 + 4 + 6 = 30$ $\checkmark$.

This subtraction sequence ("only" $=$ "total" $-$ "both") fills in the four regions reliably.

## A three-set Venn (briefly)

Some questions use THREE intersecting circles, with $8$ regions to fill in. The same logic applies but with more book-keeping. The key formula extends:

$$P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap C) - P(B \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C)$$

You will rarely need this exact formula at O-Level. Instead, fill in regions one at a time from the centre outward, using totals to work back.

For three-set questions on the paper, the standard approach is given counts for several regions (and overall totals), find the rest by subtracting from the relevant total. The page-1 example in your textbook is the template.

## Common mistakes

- **Including the overlap once vs twice.** $P(A)$ includes the overlap; $P(A \cap B)$ is just the overlap. Mixing them gives wrong answers.
- **Mis-reading "A only"**. "$A$ only" excludes the overlap; "in $A$" includes the overlap.
- **Forgetting the rectangle (sample space)**. The denominator is the GRAND total $n(\Omega)$, including students "outside" both circles.
- **Confusing union with intersection.** $\cap$ vs $\cup$: read them as AND vs OR.
- **Adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ for union without subtracting the overlap**. That double-counts. Inclusion-exclusion subtracts the overlap once.

## Quick summary

- **Four regions in a two-set Venn**: $A$ only, both ($A \cap B$), $B$ only, neither.
- **$\cap$ = AND** (both); **$\cup$ = OR** (at least one); **prime ($'$) = NOT**.
- **$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$** (inclusion-exclusion).
- **$P(A') = 1 - P(A)$** (the complement rule from 8.1).
- **Probabilities are counts over $n(\Omega)$**.
- For three-circle problems, work from inside out, using totals to backfill.
