## Why this matters

Up to now you have always asked the probability of ONE event. From here, the paper asks about TWO events at once. Roll two dice, add the scores: what is the probability the sum is $7$? Spin two spinners, multiply the readings: what is the probability the product is even?

When the two events each have a small number of equally likely outcomes, the simplest tool is a **sample space diagram**: a grid that lays out every combination. Once the grid is built, you just count cells.

This is one of the most reliable probability questions on the paper, worth $2$ to $4$ marks. The first mark is for drawing or imagining the grid; the second is for counting correctly; sometimes there are extra marks for follow-up parts.

## The setup

A sample space diagram is a table where:

- Each ROW corresponds to one outcome of one event.
- Each COLUMN corresponds to one outcome of the other event.
- Each CELL is the result of combining the two (sum, product, both values listed, etc.).

The grid contains EVERY possible combination, each one equally likely (assuming both events are fair). The total number of cells is the denominator for every probability.

## When to use a sample space diagram

The right tool when both of these are true:

1. There are TWO independent events.
2. Each event has a SMALL number of equally likely outcomes (typically $2$ to $6$ each).

If one event has many outcomes (say, drawing from a $52$-card deck) or three or more events are combined, a tree diagram (next page) is faster than building a giant grid.

The biggest grids you will see on the paper: two six-sided dice ($6 \times 6 = 36$), two four-sided spinners ($4 \times 4 = 16$), two five-sided spinners ($5 \times 5 = 25$).

## Worked example: two dice, sum = 7

Two fair six-sided dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is $7$.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-two-dice-sum-7" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Total outcomes.** Each die has $6$ outcomes; the dice are independent, so total $= 6 \times 6 = 36$.

**Step 2: List the favourable.** Pairs (die A, die B) with sum $7$:

$$(1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1)$$

That is $6$ pairs.

**Step 3: Probability.**

$$P(\text{sum} = 7) = \dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac{1}{6}$$

**Answer:** $P(\text{sum} = 7) = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{6}}$. **[M1 for a complete sample space OR listing $6$ correct pairs; A1 for $\tfrac{1}{6}$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

The sum $7$ is special: it is the MOST LIKELY sum to come up from two fair dice ($\tfrac{1}{6}$), because more pairs add to $7$ than to any other value. Sums of $2$ and $12$ are the LEAST likely ($\tfrac{1}{36}$ each).

## A second example: two spinners

Spinner A has sectors labelled $\{1, 1, 3, 5\}$. Spinner B has sectors $\{2, 4, 6, 8\}$. Both are spun; the two scores are added. Find the probability that the sum is greater than $10$.

<iframe src="/widgets/probability-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-two-spinners" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:540px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Total outcomes.** Spinner A has $4$ sectors (including the two $1$s, which are different sectors even though they show the same number). Spinner B has $4$ sectors. Total $= 4 \times 4 = 16$.

**Step 2: Build the sums.** Set out the sample space. Spinner A reads down the columns ($1, 1, 3, 5$); spinner B reads across the rows ($2, 4, 6, 8$). Sums:

$$\begin{array}{c|cccc} + & 1 & 1 & 3 & 5 \\ \hline 2 & 3 & 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 4 & 5 & 5 & 7 & 9 \\ 6 & 7 & 7 & 9 & 11 \\ 8 & 9 & 9 & 11 & 13 \end{array}$$

**Step 3: Count favourable.** Sums strictly greater than $10$: $11, 11, 13$. That is $3$ cells out of $16$.

$$P(\text{sum} > 10) = \dfrac{3}{16}$$

**Answer:** $P(\text{sum} > 10) = \boxed{\dfrac{3}{16}}$. **[M1 for a complete sample space; A1 for $\tfrac{3}{16}$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

The key detail in this question: spinner A's two $1$s are TREATED AS SEPARATE sectors. So the column "$1$" appears twice in the grid, and the row of sums starting from each one is the same. Without this, the denominator would be wrong.

## Common mistakes

- **Wrong denominator.** Two dice always give $36$ cells, not $11$ (the range of possible sums) and not $6 + 6 = 12$. The denominator is rows $\times$ columns.
- **Forgetting that order matters.** $(1, 6)$ and $(6, 1)$ are TWO different outcomes in the grid, even though they have the same sum. Both count.
- **Mixing "at least" with "more than".** "Sum greater than $10$" excludes $10$ itself; "sum at least $10$" includes $10$.
- **Missing cells.** Build the grid carefully. Markers commonly award the method mark for a complete grid even if the counting is wrong.
- **Skipping the grid.** For two-event questions with small numbers of outcomes, the grid is almost always the fastest route. Skipping it tends to lose the method mark.

## Quick summary

- **Sample space diagram** $=$ grid showing every combination of two events.
- **Total cells** $=$ (outcomes of event $A$) $\times$ (outcomes of event $B$).
- **Probability** $=$ (matching cells) $/$ (total cells), simplified.
- **Use when** both events have small numbers of equally likely outcomes.
- **Order matters**: $(1, 6)$ and $(6, 1)$ are different cells.
- **Method mark** is for a complete sample space; **accuracy mark** is for the count.

The next part handles the bigger problem: what if the events have many outcomes (like drawing from a bag with several colours), or if there are more than two events? Then tree diagrams take over.
