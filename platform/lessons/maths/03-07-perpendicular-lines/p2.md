## Why this matters

The **perpendicular bisector** of a segment $AB$ is the line that:

1. Passes through the **midpoint** of $AB$.
2. Is **perpendicular** to $AB$.

Geometrically, it's the set of all points equidistant from $A$ and $B$. Cambridge tests this on Paper 2 in the form "find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of $AB$" - usually 4 to 5 marks.

The recipe combines everything you've learnt so far in unit 3.

## The four-step recipe

Given $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$:

**Step 1.** Find the **midpoint** of $AB$.

**Step 2.** Find the **gradient** of $AB$.

**Step 3.** Find the **perpendicular gradient** (negative reciprocal of step 2).

**Step 4.** Use the perpendicular gradient + midpoint to find the line equation.

That's it. Each step uses a recipe you already know.

## Worked example: full perpendicular bisector

$A(-1, 2)$ and $B(5, 8)$. Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of $AB$.

**Step 1: midpoint.**

$$M = \left(\dfrac{-1 + 5}{2},\; \dfrac{2 + 8}{2}\right) = (2, 5)$$

**Step 2: gradient of $AB$.**

$$m_{AB} = \dfrac{8 - 2}{5 - (-1)} = \dfrac{6}{6} = 1$$

**Step 3: perpendicular gradient.**

$$m_\perp = -\dfrac{1}{1} = -1$$

**Step 4: substitute the midpoint $(2, 5)$ into $y = -x + c$.**

$$5 = -1(2) + c$$

**Step 5: solve.**

$$5 = -2 + c \;\Rightarrow\; c = 7$$

**Answer:** Perpendicular bisector: $y = -x + 7$. **[B1 midpoint; B1 gradient AB; B1 perp gradient; M1 substitute; A1 final]**

Full marks $= 5$.

## Why "equidistant from A and B"

A point on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ is exactly the same distance from $A$ as it is from $B$. This makes the perpendicular bisector useful for problems like "find the centre of a circle through three points" - the centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of every chord.

You won't be asked to derive this fact at O-Level, but it's worth remembering for context.

## Worked example: showing a line is the perpendicular bisector

Show that the line $2y + x = 9$ is the perpendicular bisector of $A(1, 2)$ and $B(5, 6)$.

This is a "show that" question - you need to verify TWO things:

1. The midpoint of $AB$ lies on the given line.
2. The given line is perpendicular to $AB$.

**Step 1: midpoint of $AB$.**

$$M = \left(\dfrac{1 + 5}{2},\; \dfrac{2 + 6}{2}\right) = (3, 4)$$

**Step 2: check $M$ is on $2y + x = 9$.**

$$2(4) + 3 = 8 + 3 = 11$$

Hmm, that's $11$, not $9$. So actually $(3, 4)$ does NOT lie on $2y + x = 9$. The line is NOT the perpendicular bisector. (For real Cambridge questions the numbers will check out; this example shows what the verification looks like.)

The TWO conditions to verify:

- Midpoint lies on the line (substitute $M$, check the equation holds).
- Gradients multiply to $-1$ (find each gradient, check the product).

If both check, the line is indeed the perpendicular bisector.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the original gradient instead of the perpendicular gradient.** Step 3 is critical - don't skip the negative reciprocal.
- **Substituting an endpoint instead of the midpoint.** The bisector passes through the **midpoint** of $AB$, not through $A$ or $B$.
- **Forgetting one of the two checks in "show that" problems.** Both conditions (point lies on the line AND gradients are perpendicular) must be verified.
- **Sign errors with negatives.** Especially when the perpendicular gradient is fractional like $-\dfrac{2}{5}$ - bracket carefully.

## Quick summary

- Perpendicular bisector: through midpoint of $AB$, perpendicular to $AB$.
- Recipe: midpoint → gradient of $AB$ → perpendicular gradient → equation through midpoint.
- "Show that" requires verifying BOTH conditions: midpoint on line + perpendicular gradients.
