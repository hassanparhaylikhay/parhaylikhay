## Why this matters

Two lines are **perpendicular** when they cross at a $90°$ angle. The gradient condition is short but unintuitive: $m_1 \times m_2 = -1$. Cambridge tests this constantly: "find the gradient perpendicular to..." or "find the perpendicular line through..." or "show that $L_1 \perp L_2$."

## The rule

For two non-vertical lines $y = m_1 x + c_1$ and $y = m_2 x + c_2$:

$$\textbf{perpendicular} \iff m_1 \times m_2 = -1$$

Equivalently:

$$m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{m_1}$$

In words: the perpendicular gradient is the **negative reciprocal**. Flip the fraction (reciprocal) AND change the sign.

| Original $m_1$ | Perpendicular $m_2$ |
|---|---|
| $2$ | $-\dfrac{1}{2}$ |
| $-3$ | $\dfrac{1}{3}$ |
| $\dfrac{2}{5}$ | $-\dfrac{5}{2}$ |
| $-\dfrac{3}{4}$ | $\dfrac{4}{3}$ |
| $1$ | $-1$ |

The vertical–horizontal pair is the special case: a horizontal line ($m = 0$) is perpendicular to a vertical line (undefined gradient). The product $0 \times \text{undefined}$ doesn't make sense numerically, but the geometry is clear.

## Worked example: perpendicular gradient

A line has gradient $\dfrac{2}{3}$. What's the gradient of any line perpendicular to it?

**Step 1: take the reciprocal.** Reciprocal of $\dfrac{2}{3}$ is $\dfrac{3}{2}$.

**Step 2: flip the sign.** Negative of $\dfrac{3}{2}$ is $-\dfrac{3}{2}$.

**Answer:** Perpendicular gradient is $-\dfrac{3}{2}$. **[B1]**

**Check.** $\dfrac{2}{3} \times \left(-\dfrac{3}{2}\right) = -1$. ✓

Full marks $= 1$.

## Worked example: perpendicular line through a point

Find the equation of the line perpendicular to $y = 2x + 1$ that passes through $(4, 1)$.

**Step 1: gradient of the original line.** $m_1 = 2$.

**Step 2: perpendicular gradient.** $m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{2}$.

**Step 3: substitute the point into $y = m_2 x + c$.**

$$1 = -\dfrac{1}{2}(4) + c$$

**Step 4: solve.**

$$1 = -2 + c \;\Rightarrow\; c = 3$$

**Answer:** $y = -\dfrac{1}{2}x + 3$. **[B1 perp gradient; M1 substitute; A1 final]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Why "negative reciprocal"

If $L_1$ rises by $a$ for every $b$ across (gradient $\dfrac{a}{b}$), the perpendicular line rotates the direction $90°$. Geometrically, that swaps the rise and the run AND flips the sign of one of them. End result: $-\dfrac{b}{a}$ - flip and negate.

A clean way to remember: the product MUST be $-1$ (a negative number times any positive can give $-1$, but two positives cannot - so one must be flipped).

## Common mistakes

- **Reciprocal without the sign flip.** $\dfrac{1}{2}$ is the reciprocal of $2$ but not the perpendicular gradient. Need $-\dfrac{1}{2}$.
- **Sign flip without reciprocal.** $-2$ is not perpendicular to $2$ - that's just a mirror, gradient still $|2|$. Reciprocal AND sign flip.
- **Forgetting to compute new $c$.** The perpendicular line's $c$ comes from the new point, not from the original line.
- **Saying $m_1 \times m_2 = 1$ for perpendicular.** It's $-1$. Lines with $m_1 \times m_2 = 1$ are NOT perpendicular.

## Quick summary

- Perpendicular gradients: $m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{m_1}$ (flip + negate).
- Equivalently: $m_1 \times m_2 = -1$.
- Recipe for perpendicular line through point: get perpendicular gradient, then substitute the point.
