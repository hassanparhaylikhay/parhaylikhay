## Why this matters

A **net** is what a 3D shape looks like when you unfold it flat onto paper. Cricket-ball makers, kite-shop walas in Lahore, and packaging engineers all start with nets: flat patterns that fold into the solid you want.

For Cambridge, nets serve two purposes:

1. **Identification.** "Which of these nets folds into a cube?" or "Sketch a net of this prism."
2. **Calculation.** Once you have a net, the **surface area** is just the total area of all the flat shapes: easy. And reading dimensions off a net is often easier than reading them off a 3D drawing, so questions sometimes give you a net to compute volumes too.

## What a net is

If you take a cardboard box, slit some of its edges and lay it flat, you get a net. Different sets of slits give different nets of the same solid (a cube has $11$ different valid nets, for instance), but they all have the same total area: that area is the **total surface area** of the solid.

Three rules that always hold:

1. The number of flat shapes in the net = the number of faces on the solid.
2. The total area of the net = the total surface area of the solid.
3. Every fold line in the net corresponds to one edge of the solid. So you can also count edges from a net.

If the cube has side length $s$:

- Each face: area $s^2$.
- Total surface area: $6 s^2$.
- Volume: $s^3$.

For a cube of side $4$ cm: surface area $= 6 \times 16 = 96$ cm². Volume $= 64$ cm³.

## Net of a cuboid

A cuboid (think of a juice box) has $6$ rectangular faces, in $3$ matching pairs. If the dimensions are length $\ell$, width $w$, height $h$:

- Two faces of $\ell \times w$.
- Two faces of $\ell \times h$.
- Two faces of $w \times h$.

The net usually shows the largest face as the base, with the other four faces unfolded around it like flaps, and one extra face attached as the lid.

**Total surface area:**

$$\text{TSA} = 2(\ell w + \ell h + w h)$$

**Volume:**

$$V = \ell \times w \times h$$

For a cuboid $3 \times 4 \times 5$ cm:

$$\text{TSA} = 2(3 \times 4 + 3 \times 5 + 4 \times 5) = 2(12 + 15 + 20) = 94 \text{ cm}^2$$
$$V = 3 \times 4 \times 5 = 60 \text{ cm}^3$$

## Net of a prism

A **prism** has two identical parallel ends (the **cross-section**) joined by rectangles. The number of rectangles equals the number of sides of the cross-section.

A **triangular prism** with triangular cross-section of sides $a, b, c$ and length $L$ has a net of:

- $2$ identical triangles (the two ends).
- $3$ rectangles of dimensions $a \times L$, $b \times L$, $c \times L$.

**Total surface area:** $2 \times (\text{area of triangle}) + (a + b + c) \times L$.

The expression $(a + b + c) \times L$ is the perimeter of the cross-section times the length: a useful general fact for any prism.

**Volume:** (area of cross-section) $\times L$.

For a triangular prism with cross-section $3, 4, 5$ cm (right-angled, since $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$) and length $10$ cm:

- Area of triangle $= \dfrac{1}{2} \times 3 \times 4 = 6$ cm².
- TSA $= 2 \times 6 + (3 + 4 + 5) \times 10 = 12 + 120 = 132$ cm².
- Volume $= 6 \times 10 = 60$ cm³.

## Net of a cylinder

A **cylinder** is a prism with a circular cross-section. Its "net" has:

- $2$ identical circles (the two ends), each of area $\pi r^2$.
- $1$ rectangle (the curved side, unrolled), with one side equal to the **circumference** $2\pi r$ and the other side equal to the **height** $h$.

## Net of a pyramid

A pyramid has a polygon base (often a triangle or square) with triangular faces meeting at a single apex.

A **square-based pyramid** with base side $s$ and slant height $\ell$ (the height of one of the triangular sides, measured from the base midpoint up to the apex along the face surface) has a net of:

- $1$ square of side $s$.
- $4$ identical isosceles triangles with base $s$ and height $\ell$.

**Total surface area:**

$$\text{TSA} = s^2 + 4 \times \dfrac{1}{2} \times s \times \ell = s^2 + 2 s \ell$$

A common Cambridge trap: the question gives you the **vertical height** $h$ of the pyramid (centre of base up to apex), not the slant height $\ell$. You must compute $\ell$ using Pythagoras in the right-angled triangle (apex, centre of base, midpoint of one base edge):

$$\ell = \sqrt{h^2 + \left(\dfrac{s}{2}\right)^2}$$

## Reading dimensions off a net

Sometimes the question gives you a flat net with dimensions labelled and asks you to compute the surface area or volume of the folded solid.

For a cuboid net, the rectangles you see give you the three pair of face dimensions directly. To find the volume, you need the three edge lengths $\ell, w, h$. Reading them off a net:

- The widths of the four rectangles in a row give you $\ell$, $w$, $\ell$, $w$ alternately.
- The heights of those rectangles all equal $h$.
- The two extra rectangles (top and bottom) have dimensions $\ell \times w$.

A clean test: pair up the matching rectangles. Each dimension should appear on at least two faces. If it appears only once, you've read the net wrong.

## Worked example

A net consists of $5$ flat shapes: a square of side $6$ cm and four congruent isosceles triangles, each with base $6$ cm and slant height $5$ cm.

(a) State the solid this net folds into.

(b) Calculate the total surface area of the solid.

(c) Given that the vertical height of the solid is $h$, find $h$.

**Step 1 (a).** A square base + $4$ triangular faces meeting at one apex $\to$ a square-based pyramid.

**Step 2 (b).** Area of the square base $= 6 \times 6 = 36$ cm².

**Step 3 (b).** Area of one triangle $= \dfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 5 = 15$ cm².

**Step 4 (b).** Total area of $4$ triangles $= 4 \times 15 = 60$ cm².

**Step 5 (b).** Total surface area $= 36 + 60 = 96$ cm².

**Step 6 (c).** The slant height $\ell = 5$ cm is the apex-to-base-midpoint distance, measured **along the face**. The vertical height $h$ runs from apex to centre of base.

**Step 7 (c).** In the right triangle formed by $h$, half a base side ($3$ cm), and the slant height $\ell$:

$$\ell^2 = h^2 + 3^2$$
$$5^2 = h^2 + 9$$
$$h^2 = 16$$
$$h = 4 \text{ cm}$$

**Answer (a):** Square-based pyramid. **[B1]**

**Answer (b):** $96$ cm². **[M1 for $36 + 4 \times \dfrac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 5$; A1 for $96$]**

**Answer (c):** $h = 4$ cm. **[M1 for Pythagoras with $\ell^2 = h^2 + 3^2$; A1 for $4$]**

Full marks $= 5$.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting faces wrong.** A square-based pyramid has $5$ faces (NOT $4$): the base counts.
- **Mixing slant height with vertical height.** Slant height runs along the face surface; vertical height runs through the inside. Use Pythagoras to switch between them.
- **Forgetting the base** when computing pyramid surface area. A pyramid sitting on the floor still has a base: it's just hidden.
- **For a cylinder**: forgetting that the curved surface unrolls into a rectangle of width $2\pi r$ (circumference), not $\pi r$ or $\pi d^2$.
- **Misreading a cuboid net**: two of the rectangles show length $\times$ height, two show width $\times$ height, two show length $\times$ width. The repeated dimension across pairs tells you which is which.
- **Adding instead of multiplying for volume.** Volume $=$ (cross-section area) $\times$ length. NOT (length + width + height).

## Quick summary

- A **net** is the unfolded flat pattern of a solid. Total area of net $=$ total surface area of solid.
- Cube of side $s$: TSA $= 6 s^2$, $V = s^3$.
- Cuboid: TSA $= 2(\ell w + \ell h + w h)$, $V = \ell w h$.
- Prism: TSA $= 2 \times (\text{cross-section area}) + (\text{cross-section perimeter}) \times $ length. $V = (\text{cross-section area}) \times $ length.
- Cylinder: TSA $= 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$, $V = \pi r^2 h$.
- Square pyramid: TSA $= s^2 + 2 s \ell$ where $\ell$ is the slant height. Use Pythagoras to switch between slant and vertical heights.
