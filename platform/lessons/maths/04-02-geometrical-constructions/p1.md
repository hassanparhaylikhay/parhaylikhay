## Why this matters

Cambridge marks construction questions on **what is on the page**, not on what you meant. The arcs you swing with your compasses are the proof you actually constructed the triangle; they earn the marks. Wipe them away to make the diagram tidy and you wipe away the mark with them.

This part covers two skills: (1) **measuring** lines and angles with a ruler and protractor to the accuracy Cambridge expects, and (2) **constructing** a triangle from three given side lengths using ruler and compasses, with arcs visible.

The 4024 syllabus is unusually clear on what is **not** required: you do **not** need to construct perpendicular bisectors or angle bisectors with compasses. So if you have memorised those from a textbook, set them aside : they are not on this exam.

## Tools you need

For this whole part, on the day:

- A sharp pencil (HB or 2H). A blunt pencil widens lines and you lose accuracy.
- A clear $30$-cm ruler with millimetre markings.
- A protractor that reads from $0°$ to $180°$ (most do).
- A pair of compasses that hold the pencil tightly. Wobbly compasses ruin arcs.
- An eraser (for genuine mistakes only : never for arcs you've drawn correctly).

Cambridge requires "ruler for all straight edges". A construction drawn freehand or with a wonky edge is marked down even if the lengths are right.

## Measuring a length

To measure the length of a segment $AB$:

1. Place the $0$ mark of the ruler exactly on $A$.
2. Read the position of $B$ on the same scale (cm).
3. Round to the nearest millimetre ($0.1$ cm).

Cambridge usually accepts $\pm 0.1$ cm of the true value. So a true length of $7.4$ cm is marked correct for any answer between $7.3$ and $7.5$ cm.

If you measure $7$ cm flat (no decimal), the mark scheme often does **not** accept it : it suggests you didn't actually use the millimetre marks. Always quote at least one decimal place.

## Drawing a line of given length

To draw a segment $5.2$ cm long:

1. Mark a starting point with a small cross or dot, label it $A$.
2. Place the $0$ on $A$.
3. Mark the $5.2$ cm position with a small cross, label it $B$.
4. Join $A$ to $B$ with a single sharp pencil line.

Don't draw the line first and try to make it the right length. You will be off by $1$ to $2$ mm. Always mark first, join after.

## Measuring an angle

To measure $\angle ABC$:

1. Place the centre of the protractor on the vertex $B$.
2. Line up the $0°$ line with one of the arms (say $BA$).
3. Read the size of the angle on the protractor where the other arm $BC$ crosses.

Two protractor scales run in opposite directions; pick the one that starts from $0°$ on your aligned arm. A common error is to read the wrong scale and get $180° - $ the right answer.

Cambridge accepts $\pm 1°$ for an angle measurement (sometimes $\pm 2°$ when the diagram is small). So an angle of $73°$ is correct for any reading from $72°$ to $74°$.

## Drawing an angle of given size

To draw an angle of $58°$ at point $B$ on a line $BA$:

1. Place the protractor centre on $B$.
2. Line up $0°$ with $BA$.
3. Mark a small cross at $58°$ on the protractor scale.
4. Remove the protractor and join $B$ to the cross with a ruler.

Always extend the new line a little beyond the cross : the question may want it long enough to hit another line, and the marker will not extrapolate for you.

## Constructing a triangle from three sides (SSS)

This is THE construction Cambridge asks for. You are given three side lengths, say $AB = 8$ cm, $AC = 6$ cm, $BC = 7$ cm. You must produce a triangle that is geometrically accurate.

The procedure:

1. **Draw the base.** Use the ruler to draw $AB = 8$ cm. Label the endpoints $A$ and $B$.
2. **Open the compasses to $6$ cm** (the length $AC$). Use the ruler to set this carefully.
3. **Place the compass point on $A$ and swing an arc** above (or below) the line. The arc is a curve, $5$ to $10$ cm long : enough that it will be crossed by the next arc.
4. **Open the compasses to $7$ cm** (the length $BC$).
5. **Place the compass point on $B$ and swing a second arc** that crosses the first. The crossing point is your third vertex $C$.
6. **Join $A$ to $C$ and $B$ to $C$ with the ruler.**
7. **DO NOT erase the arcs.** They are the evidence that you constructed (rather than guessed or measured) the triangle.

If only ONE arc is visible, Cambridge usually awards a partial mark (often B1 of B2). If NO arcs are visible, the construction mark is lost even if the triangle is geometrically correct.

## Construction tolerance

Cambridge accepts a constructed triangle if:

- All three sides are within $\pm 0.2$ cm of the given lengths, and
- The construction arcs are visible at the third vertex.

Two arcs intersecting cleanly at a small cross is the gold standard. Faint scratches or arcs in the wrong place lose marks.

## Constructing a rhombus (or any shape made of two SSS triangles)

A rhombus with side $5$ cm and one diagonal of $8$ cm is just two congruent triangles glued along the diagonal. Construct one triangle (SSS: $5, 5, 8$), then construct the second on the other side of the diagonal. Same arcs, same method, twice.

This is a common Cambridge twist : the question says "construct a rhombus" but really means "construct two SSS triangles back-to-back". Spot the trick and your method falls out.

## Worked example

Construct triangle $PQR$ where $PQ = 8.5$ cm, $PR = 6$ cm and $QR = 7$ cm. Then measure and write down the size of $\angle QPR$.

**Step 1.** Draw $PQ = 8.5$ cm using the ruler. Mark and label $P$ at one end, $Q$ at the other.

**Step 2.** Open the compasses to $6$ cm (the length $PR$). Place the point on $P$ and swing an arc above $PQ$.

**Step 3.** Open the compasses to $7$ cm (the length $QR$). Place the point on $Q$ and swing a second arc to cross the first.

**Step 4.** The two arcs cross at one point. Mark it $R$.

**Step 5.** Join $P$ to $R$ and $Q$ to $R$ with the ruler. The triangle $PQR$ is now complete with both arcs still visible.

**Step 6.** Place the protractor on $P$, align $0°$ with $PQ$, read the angle at which $PR$ crosses the scale. By the cosine rule, the true angle $\angle QPR \approx 54.5°$, so a careful protractor reading should land between $52°$ and $57°$ (Cambridge accepts a $\pm 2°$ tolerance).

**Answer (construction):** Triangle $PQR$ drawn with $PQ = 8.5$, $PR = 6$, $QR = 7$ cm, all to within $\pm 0.2$ cm; intersecting arcs visible at $R$. **[B2 for correct triangle with arcs; B1 if either arcs missing or one side wrong]**

**Answer (angle):** $\angle QPR \approx 54°$ (any value between $52°$ and $57°$). **[B1, follow-through from their triangle]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- **Erasing the arcs** because they look messy. The arcs are the mark : never erase them.
- **Setting the compass width by guessing.** Always use the ruler to set the compasses precisely : open them, line up the point on $0$, line up the pencil on the target length.
- **Drawing freehand sides** between the constructed points. Use the ruler for every straight line.
- **Reading the wrong protractor scale**, getting $180° - $ the answer. Always check: does the angle look acute? Then it should read below $90°$.
- **Measuring to the nearest cm** instead of the nearest mm. The accuracy mark requires $0.1$ cm precision.
- **Forgetting the bearing format** when the question asks "construct $T$ on a bearing of $060°$". Bearings are clockwise from North; the North line at the starting point is your reference, not the horizontal.
- **Trying to construct a perpendicular bisector or angle bisector with compasses.** These are NOT in 4024. Skip the textbook section.

## Quick summary

- Measurements: lines to the nearest mm ($\pm 0.1$ cm tolerance), angles to the nearest degree ($\pm 1°$ tolerance).
- Always use a sharp pencil and a ruler for every straight line.
- SSS construction: draw the base, swing two arcs of the other side lengths from the base endpoints, the intersection is the third vertex.
- **Leave arcs visible.** They earn the construction mark.
- Construction tolerance: $\pm 0.2$ cm on every side.
- Cambridge does NOT require perpendicular-bisector or angle-bisector constructions.
