## Why this matters

A **composite solid** is made of two or more standard solids stuck together. Cambridge loves these: half a 5-mark question, sometimes more. The two skills are:

1. **Add volumes.** Total volume = sum of parts. Easy.
2. **Carefully count surface areas.** When two solids share a face, that face is **inside** the new solid and doesn't count. The trick is identifying which faces cancel.

A second pattern in this part: **a cone formed by rolling up a sector**. The sector's arc becomes the cone's base circumference, and the sector's radius becomes the cone's slant. This shows up most years.

In this part:

- Composite solids: hemisphere + cone, cylinder + cone, cuboid + sphere.
- Cancelling internal faces.
- Cone formed from a sector.

## Adding volumes

For the hemisphere-plus-cone solid (radius $r$, cone height $h$):

$$V_\text{total} = \tfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3 + \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$$

The two volumes are independent. There is no overlap inside, so just sum them.

## Counting surface areas: cancel the join

Two faces that share a boundary cancel **if and only if** they are sealed against each other (both sides hidden inside the solid).

For a hemisphere with a cone on top, both radius $r$, the cone is glued onto the hemisphere's flat side (the dome faces down here, with the cone pointing up). Walk through every face:

- Curved hemisphere ($2\pi r^2$): exposed.
- Hemisphere flat side (the cut): hidden, glued to the cone's base.
- Cone base ($\pi r^2$): hidden, glued to the hemisphere's flat side.
- Cone curved ($\pi r l$): exposed.

$$\text{total SA} = 2\pi r^2 + \pi r l$$

The two $\pi r^2$ faces at the join are the same boundary, and both sides are inside the new solid. So they cancel.

**Visualise it.** If you took the cone off and looked at the hemisphere, you'd see a flat circle. If you flipped the cone upside-down and looked at it, you'd see another flat circle. When you glue them together, both circles are inside the solid: invisible.

## Worked example: hemisphere + cone (Cambridge Summer 2024 Paper 22 Q8b)

A solid is formed by placing a cone on top of a hemisphere. Both have radius $4.80$ cm. Total height = $10$ cm. Find the slant height of the cone.

**Step 1: cone height.** $h = 10 - 4.80 = 5.20$ cm.

**Step 2: slant via Pythagoras.**

$$l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2} = \sqrt{4.80^2 + 5.20^2} = \sqrt{23.04 + 27.04} = \sqrt{50.08}$$

$$l \approx 7.077\ldots \approx \boxed{7.08}\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $r^2 + h^2$; M1 for $\sqrt{\cdot}$; A1 for $7.08$]**

Full marks $= 3$. (The mark scheme also accepts the value substituted into $\pi r l$ to give a curved-cone-area answer of about $107$ cm² for the next sub-question, but the slant alone is a 3-mark item.)

## Worked example: total SA of the same solid

Continuing the composite from above (hemisphere + cone, both radius $r = 4.80$, slant $l = 7.08$):

$$\text{SA} = 2\pi r^2 + \pi r l$$

$$= 2\pi(4.80)^2 + \pi(4.80)(7.08) = 46.08\pi + 33.98\pi = 80.06\pi \approx \boxed{251}\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for $2\pi r^2$ (curved hemisphere); M1 for $\pi r l$ (curved cone); M1 for noticing the join cancels (no extra $\pi r^2$); A1 for the value]**

Full marks $= 4$.

**Cambridge Summer 2025 Paper 22, Q21(b)** is the closest Cambridge analogue: the same setup with different radii and slant produces a final SA of $712$ cm². The mark scheme there awards 5 marks total: 4 method marks for the two curved-surface formulas and the join reasoning, plus the accuracy mark for the final number.

## Cone formed from a sector

Take a flat sector. Hold its centre point fixed and roll the two straight edges together until they meet. You get a cone.

The geometry of this swap:

| Sector | becomes | Cone |
|---|---|---|
| sector radius $R$ | becomes | cone slant $l$ |
| sector arc length | becomes | cone base circumference $2\pi r$ |

So if you start with a sector of radius $R$ and angle $\theta$, the arc length is $\tfrac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi R$. After rolling, that becomes the circumference of the cone's base, $2\pi r$:

$$2\pi r = \tfrac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi R \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \tfrac{\theta}{360} \times R$$

Then the cone's height comes from Pythagoras using the slant ($l = R$, the original sector radius):

$$h = \sqrt{l^2 - r^2} = \sqrt{R^2 - r^2}$$

## Worked example: sector to cone (Cambridge Summer 2023 Paper 22 Q8b)

A sector of radius $8$ cm and angle $75°$ is bent into the curved surface of a cone. Find the volume of the cone.

**Step 1: arc length of the sector.**

$$\text{arc} = \tfrac{75}{360} \times 2\pi(8) = \tfrac{75}{360} \times 16\pi = \tfrac{1200}{360}\pi = \tfrac{10\pi}{3}\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{75}{360} \times 16\pi$]**

**Step 2: cone radius.** That arc becomes the base circumference $2\pi r$.

$$2\pi r = \tfrac{10\pi}{3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \tfrac{5}{3}\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $r = \tfrac{\text{arc}}{2\pi}$]**

**Step 3: cone height.** Slant $l = R = 8$ cm.

$$h = \sqrt{l^2 - r^2} = \sqrt{64 - \tfrac{25}{9}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{576 - 25}{9}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{551}{9}} = \tfrac{\sqrt{551}}{3} \approx 7.825\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $8^2 - r^2$; M1 for $\sqrt{\cdot}$]**

**Step 4: volume.**

$$V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi \times \tfrac{25}{9} \times \tfrac{\sqrt{551}}{3} = \tfrac{25\pi\sqrt{551}}{81} \approx \boxed{22.7}\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$; A1 for value in range $22.7$ to $22.9$]**

Full marks $= 6$.

The mark scheme allows a small range ($22.7$–$22.9$) because intermediate rounding produces slight variations. The marker's annotation here is **nfww** ("not from wrong working"), meaning the answer must come from a correct method.

## Composite with a cavity (frustum-like)

A solid can also have a part **removed**. For example, a large cone with a small cone cut from its tip ("frustum"-shaped solid).

For the **volume**, subtract:

$$V_\text{frustum} = V_\text{large} - V_\text{small} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r_1^2 h_1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r_2^2 h_2$$

For the **surface area**, count the exposed faces:

- Outer curved surface of the large cone: $\pi r_1 l_1$
- Inner curved surface of the small cone (the cavity): $\pi r_2 l_2$
- The annular ring (the flat top, which is the difference of two circles): $\pi r_1^2 - \pi r_2^2$
- The flat base of the original cone: $\pi r_1^2$ (only if the bottom is still closed)

This is exactly the **Cambridge Winter 2023 Paper 22, Q10(a)** structure, where a small cone is removed from the tip of a large one, leaving a frustum-shaped cap with three exposed surfaces (the two curved cones plus the annular top).

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to cancel the join.** Adding all six faces (cylinder top, cone base, etc.) double-counts the hidden surfaces.
- **Adding instead of subtracting for cavities.** A "remove" composite uses subtraction. A "join" uses addition.
- **Wrong slant for sector-to-cone.** The sector's RADIUS becomes the cone's SLANT, not the cone's perpendicular height. The arc becomes the base circumference, not the radius.
- **Using diameter instead of radius after sector-to-cone.** Once you have the cone's base circumference, divide by $2\pi$ to get $r$, not $\pi$.

## Quick summary

| Composite type | Volume | Surface area |
|---|---|---|
| Hemisphere + cone (same $r$) | $\tfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3 + \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | $2\pi r^2 + \pi r l$ |
| Cone + cylinder (same $r$) | $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h_1 + \pi r^2 h_2$ | $\pi r l + 2\pi r h_2 + \pi r^2$ |
| Cone with cone removed | $V_\text{large} - V_\text{small}$ | curved-large + curved-small + annulus |
| Sector rolled into cone | (gives slant $= R$, $r = \tfrac{\theta}{360} R$) | use $\pi r l$ etc |

**Strategy:** before computing anything, draw the composite, label every face, mark which are **exposed** and which are **hidden** at the join. Then sum only the exposed faces. That visualisation step is what separates the 5-mark answer from the 2-mark partial.
