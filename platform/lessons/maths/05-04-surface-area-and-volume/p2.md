## Why this matters

A **prism** is any 3D solid that has the same cross-section all the way along its length. Slice it anywhere perpendicular to the length and you get the same shape.

Examples of prisms:

- **cuboid** (cross-section is a rectangle)
- **triangular prism** (cross-section is a triangle)
- **trapezoidal prism** (cross-section is a trapezium)
- **cylinder** (cross-section is a circle, covered in p3)

The **volume formula given on every Cambridge paper**:

$$\boxed{\;V = \text{area of cross-section} \times \text{length}\;}$$

That single formula handles every prism in the syllabus. The work is in finding the cross-sectional area, which is from 5.2 (rectangle, triangle, trapezium) or from 5.3 (circle, sector).

In this part:

- The general prism formula.
- Triangular prisms (most common in Cambridge).
- Trapezoidal prisms (paper 2 questions).
- Surface area = sum of all faces (no shortcut).

## The general formula in action

For any prism, **find the cross-sectional area first**, then multiply by the length.

The cross-section can be a rectangle, triangle, trapezium, parallelogram, circle, sector, or compound shape. The 5.2 and 5.3 formulas all reappear here as the "first step" on a prism question.

| Cross-section | Area formula | Where it came from |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | $l \times w$ | 5.2 p1 |
| Triangle | $\tfrac{1}{2} \times b \times h$ | 5.2 p2 |
| Parallelogram | $b \times h$ | 5.2 p3 |
| Trapezium | $\tfrac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h$ | 5.2 p4 |
| Circle | $\pi r^2$ | 5.3 p1 |
| Sector | $\tfrac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$ | 5.3 p3 |

## Worked example: triangular prism

The cross-section of a prism is an isosceles triangle with base $10$ cm and equal sides of $13$ cm. The prism has length $3$ cm. Show that the volume is $180$ cm³.

**Step 1: find the height of the triangular cross-section using Pythagoras.**

Drop a perpendicular from the apex to the midpoint of the base. This creates a right triangle with hypotenuse $13$ and one leg $\frac{10}{2} = 5$.

$$h = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $\sqrt{13^2 - 5^2}$]**

**Step 2: find the cross-section area.**

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 12 = 60\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 10 \times \text{their } 12$]**

**Step 3: multiply by length.**

$$V = A \times \text{length} = 60 \times 3 = \boxed{180}\;\text{cm}^3 \;\checkmark$$

**[A1 for $180$]**

Full marks $= 4$. This is **w25_11 Q17(a)**.

A "show that" question always wants every step visible: the Pythagoras working, the area, and the multiplication. Skipping any of them risks losing the M marks.

## Worked example: trapezoidal prism

A trapezoidal prism container has cross-section $ABCD$ with $AB = 28$ cm, $DC = 24$ cm, $AD = 16$ cm (with right angles at $A$ and $D$). The prism's depth is $29$ cm. Water is poured in at $4000$ cm³/min for $2$ minutes. Is the container more than two-thirds full?

**Step 1: cross-section area.**

The cross-section is a trapezium with parallel sides $28$ and $24$, separated by perpendicular distance $16$.

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2}(28 + 24) \times 16 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 52 \times 16 = 416\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{2}(28 + 24) \times 16$]**

**Step 2: total volume.**

$$V = 416 \times 29 = 12{,}064\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for trapezium area × $29$]**

**Step 3: water poured in.**

$$\text{water} = 4000 \times 2 = 8000\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[B1 for $8000$]**

**Step 4: compare with two-thirds.**

$$\tfrac{2}{3} \times 12064 = 8042.67\ldots\;\text{cm}^3$$

$$8000 < 8042.67\ldots \;\Rightarrow\; \text{NOT more than two-thirds full}$$

**[M1 for comparison; A1 for "no" with valid working]**

Full marks $= 5$. This is **s23_21 Q11(b)**, a multi-mark question that rewards careful step-by-step work.

## Surface area of a prism

There is no single formula. Add the areas of all the faces.

For a triangular prism with two end-triangles and three rectangular sides:

$$\text{SA} = 2 \times (\text{triangle area}) + (\text{rectangle 1}) + (\text{rectangle 2}) + (\text{rectangle 3})$$

The three rectangles are formed by each side of the triangle running along the length of the prism. So if the triangle has sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and the prism has length $L$, the three rectangles are $aL$, $bL$, $cL$.

For the $13, 13, 10$ isosceles triangle of length $3$ cm above:

- Two triangles: $2 \times 60 = 120$ cm²
- Three rectangles: $13 \times 3 + 13 \times 3 + 10 \times 3 = 39 + 39 + 30 = 108$ cm²
- Total: $120 + 108 = 228$ cm²

The structure is the same for every prism: **2 end-faces + sides made of (each cross-section side $\times$ prism length)**.

## Reverse problems

If you are given the volume and either the cross-section or the length, divide.

If a prism has volume $300$ cm³ and length $15$ cm, the cross-section area is $\frac{300}{15} = 20$ cm². If the cross-section is a triangle with given base, you can then find the height. This chain of reasoning shows up regularly.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the perpendicular height.** Triangle area uses the height *perpendicular* to the base, not a slant side. Drop a perpendicular and use Pythagoras if needed.
- **Missing the two end-faces in surface area.** The cross-section appears twice (once at each end), then the sides wrap around.
- **Wrong unit on volume.** Cross-section is cm² (area), prism length is cm. Their product is cm³, not cm² or just cm.
- **Confusing "length" of the prism with "height".** The length is the dimension perpendicular to the cross-section. For a vertical can, that "length" is the vertical height. For a horizontal pencil, it is the horizontal length.

## Quick summary

| Quantity | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Volume of prism | $V = A_\text{cross} \times L$ | Given on the paper |
| Cross-section area | depends on shape | From 5.2 or 5.3 |
| Surface area | sum of all faces | Not given; build by listing |

A prism question usually splits into two: first find the cross-section area, then multiply. Treat them as two separate sub-problems. The M marks are typically on the cross-section step, the A mark on the multiplication.
