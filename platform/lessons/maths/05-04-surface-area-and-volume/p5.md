## Why this matters

A **pyramid** has a flat polygonal base and triangular sides meeting at a point (the **apex**). A **cone** is a pyramid with a circular base.

The volume formula is the same for both, given on the formula sheet:

$$V_\text{pyramid} = \tfrac{1}{3} \times \text{base area} \times h$$

$$V_\text{cone} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$$

Note the $\tfrac{1}{3}$. A pyramid (or cone) has **one third** the volume of the prism (or cylinder) with the same base and height. That factor is the whole story for these shapes.

For the cone, you also get curved SA $= \pi r l$, where $l$ is the **slant height** (apex to edge of the base). The slant height comes from Pythagoras: $l^2 = r^2 + h^2$. This is a near-guaranteed extra step on cone questions.

In this part:

- $V = \tfrac{1}{3} \times \text{base} \times h$ for any pyramid.
- $V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ for a cone.
- Curved SA $= \pi r l$ where $l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$.
- Total SA of a closed cone $= \pi r l + \pi r^2$.

The cone's curved surface unrolls into a **sector** of a larger circle (radius $l$, the slant height). The straight edges of the sector were the seam where the cone was cut. Plus a small base circle of radius $r$. The arc of the sector equals $2\pi r$ — the cone's base circumference.

For a pyramid, the net is the polygonal base plus one triangle per side:

Each triangular side has its apex meeting the base's central apex, and its slant height is the cone's "side slant" — the apex-to-midpoint-of-base-edge distance, found by Pythagoras separately.

## The slant height

For a cone, the **perpendicular height** $h$ is the vertical line from the apex straight down to the centre of the base. The **slant height** $l$ is the line from the apex along the curved surface to the edge of the base circle.

These three lengths form a right triangle:

$$l^2 = r^2 + h^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$$

This is just Pythagoras (from 6.1). The cone formulas use $h$ for volume and $l$ for surface area, so questions almost always require Pythagoras to convert between them.

A 3-4-5 cone (radius 3, height 4, slant 5) is a clean example. So is 6-8-10 (twice that), 5-12-13, and 9-12-15.

## Worked example: standard cone

A cone has radius $9$ cm and perpendicular height $12$ cm. Find the volume and the total surface area, in terms of π.

**Step 1: volume.**

$$V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi \times 81 \times 12 = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 972\pi = \boxed{324\pi}\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$; A1 for $324\pi$]**

**Step 2: slant height.**

$$l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2} = \sqrt{81 + 144} = \sqrt{225} = 15\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $\sqrt{9^2 + 12^2}$ or equivalent]**

**Step 3: total surface area.**

$$\text{curved} = \pi r l = \pi \times 9 \times 15 = 135\pi\;\text{cm}^2$$

$$\text{base} = \pi r^2 = 81\pi\;\text{cm}^2$$

$$\text{total} = 135\pi + 81\pi = \boxed{216\pi}\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for $\pi r l + \pi r^2$; A1 for $216\pi$]**

Full marks $= 6$.

The 9-12-15 triangle is a Pythagorean triple ($3 \times$ the 3-4-5). Cambridge picks these to keep the slant clean.

## Worked example: composite (cone on cylinder, Cambridge Winter 2024 Paper 21 Q6c)

<iframe src="/widgets/solid-step-explorer.html?preset=cone-on-cylinder" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:480px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>


A solid is formed by joining a cone to a cylinder. Both have radius $6.3$ cm. The cone's slant is $8.7$ cm, and the ratio cone-height : cylinder-height $= 2 : 3$. Find the total surface area.

**Step 1: cone height (Pythagoras).**

$$h_\text{cone} = \sqrt{8.7^2 - 6.3^2} = \sqrt{75.69 - 39.69} = \sqrt{36} = 6\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $\sqrt{8.7^2 - 6.3^2}$]**

**Step 2: cylinder height from the ratio.** Cone : cylinder $= 2 : 3$, and cone height is $6$ cm. So one "part" $= 3$, and cylinder height $= 3 \times 3 = 9$ cm.

**Step 3: count the surfaces.** The exposed faces are:

- the cone's curved surface ($\pi r l$)
- the cylinder's curved surface ($2\pi r h$)
- the cylinder's bottom circle ($\pi r^2$)

The top of the cylinder is glued to the bottom of the cone, so neither face is exposed. They cancel.

$$\text{SA} = \pi r l + 2 \pi r h + \pi r^2$$

$$= \pi(6.3)(8.7) + 2\pi(6.3)(9) + \pi(6.3)^2$$

$$= 54.81\pi + 113.4\pi + 39.69\pi = 207.9\pi$$

$$\approx \boxed{653}\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M2 for all three surface terms together]**

Full marks $= 5$. This is **Cambridge Winter 2024 Paper 21, Q6(c)**.

Three small lessons from this question:

1. **Internal faces cancel.** The cylinder top and cone base are glued together, so they don't count.
2. **Pythagoras goes the other way.** Given the slant and the radius, find the height: $h = \sqrt{l^2 - r^2}$.
3. **Use the ratio carefully.** A 2:3 ratio with a known cone height tells you both heights.

## Pyramid (general)

A pyramid has any polygonal base. The volume formula:

$$V = \tfrac{1}{3} \times A_\text{base} \times h$$

where $A_\text{base}$ is the base area and $h$ is the **perpendicular** height from the apex to the base.

For a square-based pyramid with base side $a$ and height $h$:

$$V = \tfrac{1}{3} a^2 h$$

For a triangular-based pyramid (a tetrahedron) with base triangle area $T$ and height $h$:

$$V = \tfrac{1}{3} T h$$

The surface area is the base + four (or three) triangular sides. Each triangular side has its own slant height from the apex to the midpoint of one base edge, found by Pythagoras. The slant heights of the side-triangles are not the same as the perpendicular height of the pyramid.

## Worked example: square-based pyramid

A pyramid has a square base of side $8$ cm and perpendicular height $6$ cm. Find:

(a) the volume.
(b) the slant height of one of the triangular faces.

**(a) volume.**

$$V = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 8^2 \times 6 = \tfrac{1}{3} \times 64 \times 6 = \boxed{128}\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 8^2 \times 6$; A1 for $128$]**

**(b) slant height.** The slant goes from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge. It forms a right triangle with the perpendicular height ($6$ cm) and half the base side ($4$ cm).

$$l_\text{face} = \sqrt{6^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{36 + 16} = \sqrt{52} \approx 7.21\;\text{cm}$$

**[M1 for $\sqrt{6^2 + 4^2}$; A1 for $7.21$]**

Full marks $= 4$.

A common mistake: using $\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2}$ instead of $\sqrt{6^2 + 4^2}$. The slant goes to the **midpoint** of the base side, so the horizontal leg is $\frac{8}{2} = 4$, not the full $8$.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the $\tfrac{1}{3}$.** Cone and pyramid volumes are $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of the corresponding prism / cylinder. Drop it and the volume is three times too big.
- **Using $h$ instead of $l$ for curved SA.** Curved SA $= \pi r l$, the slant. Not the perpendicular height $h$.
- **Skipping Pythagoras.** Cone problems almost always require either $l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$ or $h = \sqrt{l^2 - r^2}$. Plan for it.
- **Wrong leg for pyramid slant.** From apex to midpoint of base edge: horizontal leg $= \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base side}$, not the full base.

## Quick summary

| Quantity | Formula | Given on paper? |
|---|---|---|
| Pyramid volume | $V = \tfrac{1}{3} \times \text{base area} \times h$ | Yes |
| Cone volume | $V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | Yes |
| Cone slant | $l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$ | No (use Pythagoras) |
| Cone curved SA | $\pi r l$ | Yes |
| Cone base | $\pi r^2$ | No (add for closed cone) |
| Cone total SA | $\pi r l + \pi r^2$ | Curved part given |

The cone's set of three quantities ($r$, $h$, $l$) is the key. Two of them appear in the volume formula ($r$, $h$), two in the surface formula ($r$, $l$). The bridge between them is Pythagoras.
