## Why this matters

A **sphere** is the perfectly round 3D shape: every point on its surface is the same distance $r$ from the centre. Cambridge gives you both its volume and its surface area on the formula sheet:

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \qquad \text{SA} = 4\pi r^2$$

Notice the powers: volume is $r$ **cubed**, surface area is $r$ **squared**. Doubling the radius makes the volume $\times 8$ and the surface area $\times 4$.

A **hemisphere** is half a sphere. Sliced through the middle. Two formulas drop out, plus the new flat circular face.

In this part:

- $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ and SA $= 4\pi r^2$ for a full sphere.
- Hemisphere: half of each, plus the flat $\pi r^2$ base for total SA.
- Reverse problems: find $r$ from volume or surface area.

## Worked example: full sphere

A sphere has radius $6$ cm. Find its volume and surface area, in terms of π.

**Volume.**

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \times 6^3 = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \times 216 = \boxed{288\pi}\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$; A1 for $288\pi$]**

**Surface area.**

$$\text{SA} = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi \times 36 = \boxed{144\pi}\;\text{cm}^2$$

**[M1 for $4\pi r^2$; A1 for $144\pi$]**

Full marks $= 4$.

Both formulas are on the sheet, but the M mark is for **showing the substitution**. Just writing $288\pi$ with no working risks losing the M.

## Hemisphere: half of everything

A hemisphere is half a sphere. Cut along a diameter. Three things to know:

| Part | Formula | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | $V = \tfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3$ | Half of $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ |
| Curved SA | $2\pi r^2$ | Half of $4\pi r^2$ |
| Flat base | $\pi r^2$ | A circle, exposed by the cut |
| Total SA (solid hemisphere) | $2\pi r^2 + \pi r^2 = 3\pi r^2$ | Curved + flat |

A hemisphere on its own (sitting on a table) has total surface area $3\pi r^2$. **Don't forget the flat base.**

## Worked example: hemisphere "show that" question

A solid is formed by placing a cone on top of a hemisphere. Both have radius $r$ cm. The total height of the solid is $10$ cm. The curved surface area of the hemisphere is $145$ cm². Show that $r = 4.80$ correct to $2$ d.p.

**Step 1: write the curved SA equation.** For a hemisphere, curved SA $= 2\pi r^2$.

$$2\pi r^2 = 145$$

**[M1 for $2\pi r^2 = 145$ or $4\pi r^2 = 290$]**

**Step 2: rearrange.**

$$r^2 = \dfrac{145}{2\pi} = 23.077\ldots$$

**[M1 for $r^2 = \dfrac{145}{2\pi}$ or numerical value $23.07\ldots$]**

**Step 3: square root.**

$$r = \sqrt{23.077\ldots} = 4.8038\ldots \approx \boxed{4.80}\;\text{cm} \;\checkmark$$

**[B1 for $4.803$ to $4.804$]**

Full marks $= 3$. This is **s24_22 Q8(a)(i)**.

For a "show that" question with a value to a specific accuracy, the mark scheme awards the B1 for the unrounded number being in a tight range (here $4.803$–$4.804$) before rounding.

## Worked example: composite hemisphere + cone (volume)

Continuing from the question above, now find the volume of the combined solid (hemisphere + cone). Use $r = 4.80$.

**Step 1: cone height.** The total solid is $10$ cm tall. The hemisphere takes $r = 4.80$ cm. So the cone's height is $10 - 4.80 = 5.20$ cm.

**[B1 for $h_\text{cone} = 5.20$]**

**Step 2: cone volume.** ($V_\text{cone} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$, given on the sheet)

$$V_\text{cone} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi (4.80)^2 (5.20) = 125.4\ldots\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi (4.80)^2 (5.20)$]**

**Step 3: hemisphere volume.**

$$V_\text{hemi} = \tfrac{2}{3}\pi (4.80)^3 = 231.6\ldots\;\text{cm}^3$$

**[M1 for $\tfrac{2}{3}\pi (4.80)^3$]**

**Step 4: total.**

$$V = 125.4 + 231.6 = \boxed{357}\;\text{cm}^3 \;(\text{to 3 sf})$$

**[A1 for value in range $357$ to $358$]**

Full marks $= 4$. This is **s24_22 Q8(a)(ii)**.

The mark scheme accepts $357$ to $358$ because rounding at intermediate steps gives slightly different final values. The safe approach: keep full calculator precision, round only at the end.

## Reverse: find $r$ from the volume

If $V = 36\pi$ cm³, then:

$$\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 36\pi \;\Rightarrow\; r^3 = 36 \times \tfrac{3}{4} = 27 \;\Rightarrow\; r = 3\;\text{cm}$$

The π cancels because the answer is in terms of π. Then take the cube root.

If $V = 288$ cm³ (decimal), divide both sides by $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi$ first:

$$r^3 = \dfrac{288}{(4/3)\pi} = \dfrac{288 \times 3}{4\pi} = \dfrac{216}{\pi} \approx 68.75 \;\Rightarrow\; r \approx 4.10\;\text{cm}$$

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the flat base on a hemisphere.** A solid hemisphere sitting on a table has SA $= 3\pi r^2$, not $2\pi r^2$. The cut-side is a real face.
- **Wrong fraction.** Sphere volume has $\tfrac{4}{3}$, hemisphere $\tfrac{2}{3}$. Sphere SA has $4$, hemisphere curved $2$. The factors halve.
- **Mixing up powers.** SA uses $r^2$, volume uses $r^3$. If volume looks like a small number for a big radius, you probably forgot to cube.
- **Squaring a diameter.** If diameter $= d$, $r = d/2$. Then square or cube **the radius**, not the diameter.

## Quick summary

| Shape | Volume | Surface area | Given? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sphere | $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | $4\pi r^2$ | both yes |
| Hemisphere (curved only) | $\tfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3$ | $2\pi r^2$ | both via halving |
| Hemisphere (solid, with flat base) | $\tfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3$ | $3\pi r^2$ | flat base added |

**Memory cue:** on the formula sheet, sphere SA is the only $4\pi r^2$. Sphere volume has the only $\tfrac{4}{3}$. The $r^3$ vs $r^2$ split is what tells volume from surface area.
