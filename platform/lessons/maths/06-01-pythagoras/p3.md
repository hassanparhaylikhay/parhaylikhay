## Use Pythagoras when one leg is missing

Now the hypotenuse is given and you want one of the shorter sides. Rearrange the formula to isolate the unknown leg:

$$a^{2} = c^{2} - b^{2}$$

The big sign change: **subtract**, not add. This is where students lose marks. The hypotenuse is the BIG number, and big − small = small. The leg you want is shorter than the hypotenuse, so the subtraction direction is forced: hypotenuse² minus known leg², in that order.

If your subtraction comes out **negative**, you have probably swapped the hypotenuse and the leg. Check the diagram, find the side opposite the right angle, that is your $c$.

## Worked example: hypotenuse 13, leg 5

A right triangle has hypotenuse $13$ cm and one leg of $5$ cm. Find the other leg.

<iframe src="/widgets/pythagoras-step-explorer.html?preset=find-leg-13-5" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Step by step:

- Pythagoras: $a^{2} + 5^{2} = 13^{2}$.
- Rearrange: $a^{2} = 13^{2} - 5^{2} = 169 - 25 = 144$.
- Square root: $a = \sqrt{144} = 12$ cm.

This is the $(5, 12, 13)$ triple, one of the most common.

[M1 for $a^{2} + 5^{2} = 13^{2}$; A1 for $a = 12$ cm.]

Full marks $= 2$.

## Hypotenuse identification: the make-or-break step

Take a moment on every problem to identify the hypotenuse before substituting. The hypotenuse is **opposite the right angle**, never adjacent to it. Two quick visuals to lock it in:

If a question gives only side lengths, with no diagram, the hypotenuse is the longest of the three numbers.

## When the answer is irrational

For most Cambridge questions where Pythagoras finds a leg, the numbers are chosen so the answer is a Pythagorean triple. But for non-triple inputs, the answer is irrational. Two approaches:

- **Decimal**: report to $2$ or $3$ d.p. as the question asks.
- **Surd form**: leave it as $\sqrt{n}$ where the question allows. Often Cambridge surd-form questions simplify, e.g. $\sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}$. See [1.18 surds](../01-18-surds/p1) for simplification.

Example: $a = \sqrt{72}$. Factor: $72 = 36 \times 2$, so $\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36} \times \sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding when you should subtract.** Setting up $a^{2} = c^{2} + b^{2}$ instead of $a^{2} = c^{2} - b^{2}$. The negative result (or impossibly large answer) tells you something is wrong.
- **Misidentifying the hypotenuse.** If you put a leg in the $c$ slot, the equation falls apart. Always check the right-angle marker in the diagram.
- **Forgetting that the hypotenuse must be the longest.** If your "leg" comes out longer than the hypotenuse, you have swapped them.
- **Premature rounding.** Keep the full calculator value through to the last step.

## Quick summary

- Identify the hypotenuse first: it is opposite the right angle and always the longest side.
- If both legs are given, find the hypotenuse with $c = \sqrt{a^{2} + b^{2}}$.
- If the hypotenuse and one leg are given, find the other leg with $a = \sqrt{c^{2} - b^{2}}$. Subtract, do not add.
- A negative result under the square root means you have swapped the hypotenuse with a leg.
- To test if a triangle is right-angled, square the longest side and check it equals the sum of the squares of the other two.
