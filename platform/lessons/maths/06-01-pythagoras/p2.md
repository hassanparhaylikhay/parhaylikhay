## Use Pythagoras when both legs are known

The simplest case: you know both short sides and you want the long side. Set up Pythagoras, square, add, then square root.

$$c = \sqrt{a^{2} + b^{2}}$$

The four steps every time:

The most common mistake at this stage is **forgetting step 4**, the square root. Students sometimes write $c = 25$ when they meant $c^{2} = 25$. Always pause before the final answer and ask: am I reporting a length or an area?

## Worked example: legs 3 and 4

A right-angled triangle has legs of length $3$ cm and $4$ cm. Find the length of the hypotenuse.

Walking through the steps:

- **Step 2 (M1):** $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$, then substitute: $3^{2} + 4^{2} = c^{2}$.
- **Step 3:** $9 + 16 = 25$, so $c^{2} = 25$.
- **Step 4 (A1):** $c = \sqrt{25} = 5$ cm.

The mark scheme awards M1 for the correct Pythagoras setup and A1 for the final answer with units.

**[M1 for $3^{2} + 4^{2} = c^{2}$; A1 for $c = 5$ cm]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: legs 9 and 12

The same procedure with bigger numbers. A right triangle has legs $9$ m and $12$ m. Find the hypotenuse.

This is the $(9, 12, 15)$ triple. Notice it is the $(3, 4, 5)$ triple multiplied by $3$. Whenever you see a right triangle with sides that are a multiple of $(3, 4, 5)$ or another classic triple, the answer comes out exact.

**[M1 for $9^{2} + 12^{2} = c^{2}$; A1 for $c = 15$ m]**

Full marks $= 2$.

A right triangle has legs $5$ cm and $7$ cm. Find the hypotenuse, correct to 2 decimal places.

The Cambridge mark scheme convention: keep the full value on your calculator, only round the **final** answer. Premature rounding (rounding $\sqrt{74}$ to $8.6$ at the intermediate step then writing $c = 8.6$) can cost the accuracy mark when the question wants 2 d.p.

**[M1 for $5^{2} + 7^{2} = c^{2}$; A1 for $c = 8.60$ cm]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## A sanity check that costs nothing

Before reporting your answer, ask: **is the hypotenuse longer than each leg?** If you got $c = 4$ for legs $3$ and $4$, you have made an error (you cannot have a hypotenuse shorter than a leg). Use this check on every Pythagoras answer.

A second check: $c$ must be **less than $a + b$**. If you got $c = 8$ for legs $3$ and $4$, you have probably added the legs instead of squaring. The right answer ($5$) is less than $3 + 4 = 7$, as it should be.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding sides instead of squaring.** $3 + 4 = 7 \neq c$. The legs square first, THEN add.
- **Forgetting the square root.** Reporting $c = 25$ instead of $c = 5$ for the $(3, 4, 5)$ triangle.
- **Putting a leg in the $c$ slot.** Always identify the hypotenuse first (the side opposite the right angle).
- **Rounding too early.** Keep the calculator value full until the final answer.
- **Dropping the units** in the final answer. Cambridge does not always penalise missing units, but consistently giving them is good paper technique.

## Quick summary

- Write $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$ first.
- Substitute the two leg lengths.
- Square each, then add, to get $c^{2}$.
- Take the square root to get $c$.
- Sanity check: $c$ must be longer than each leg, but shorter than $a + b$.
- Keep the calculator value full through the working; only round the final answer.
