## When there is no obvious right triangle, build one

In Cambridge papers, the right triangle is often hidden. Your job is to spot it, draw it, and label it. Three classic patterns appear repeatedly:

1. **Rectangle diagonal.** The diagonal of a rectangle splits it into two right triangles.
2. **Isosceles split.** Drop the altitude of an isosceles triangle: it bisects the base, creating a right triangle.
3. **Pole / ladder / pylon.** The wall is vertical, the ground is horizontal, the meeting is a right angle.

You will also meet Pythagoras in coordinate geometry as the **distance formula** between two points, and in 3D problems on cuboids and pyramids.

## Pattern 1: rectangle diagonal

A rectangle is two right triangles glued along the diagonal. So if you know the two sides of the rectangle, you can find the diagonal.

<iframe src="/widgets/pythagoras-step-explorer.html?preset=rectangle-diagonal" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

For a rectangle of $12$ cm by $5$ cm, the diagonal is $\sqrt{144 + 25} = 13$ cm.

A useful pre-baked result: the diagonal of a **square of side $a$** is $a\sqrt{2}$. So a square of side $5$ has diagonal $5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07$.

## Pattern 2: isosceles split

An isosceles triangle has two equal sides and (by symmetry) the altitude from the apex to the base **bisects** the base. That perpendicular drops a right triangle into the picture: hypotenuse equals the slant side, one leg is half the base, the other leg is the altitude $h$.

<iframe src="/widgets/pythagoras-step-explorer.html?preset=isosceles-altitude" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

This same trick lets you find the area of an isosceles or equilateral triangle when you only know the side lengths. The altitude becomes the height in $A = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times h$.

## Worked example: ladder against a wall

A $5$ m ladder rests against a vertical wall. The foot of the ladder is $1.4$ m from the wall. How high up the wall does the ladder reach?

<iframe src="/widgets/pythagoras-step-explorer.html?preset=ladder" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

Identifying the right triangle: the **wall** is vertical, the **ground** is horizontal, and they meet at a right angle. The **ladder** is opposite the right angle, so the ladder is the hypotenuse.

$$h^{2} + 1.4^{2} = 5^{2}$$

$$h^{2} = 25 - 1.96 = 23.04$$

$$h = \sqrt{23.04} = 4.8\;\text{m}$$

**[M1 for $h^{2} + 1.4^{2} = 5^{2}$ or equivalent; A1 for $h = 4.8$ m]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Worked example: distance between coordinates

A point is at $A(2, 1)$ and another at $B(8, 9)$ on a coordinate grid. Find the length of segment $AB$.

<iframe src="/widgets/pythagoras-step-explorer.html?preset=coordinates-2-1-8-9" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:560px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

This is the **distance formula** in disguise. The general form, derived from Pythagoras:

$$AB = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^{2} + (y_2 - y_1)^{2}}$$

It is just Pythagoras applied to the coordinate grid.

**[M1 for $\sqrt{6^{2} + 8^{2}}$ or equivalent; A1 for $AB = 10$]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Spotting the right triangle: a checklist

When a Cambridge problem does not draw a right triangle for you, ask:

1. Is there a **rectangle** in the figure? Its diagonal makes a right triangle.
2. Is there an **isosceles** or **equilateral** triangle? Drop the perpendicular from the apex to the base.
3. Is there a **wall** or vertical post and a **horizontal ground**? The corner is a right angle.
4. Are there **coordinates**? The horizontal-vertical legs are always perpendicular.
5. Is there a **circle** with an inscribed angle from the diameter? That angle is $90°$ (Thales' theorem).
6. Is the question about a **3D solid**? Look for a cross-section that gives a right triangle.

## Common mistakes

- **Not bisecting the base** when working on an isosceles triangle. The altitude lands halfway along the base, not at one end.
- **Treating the diagonal as a side** of the rectangle. The diagonal IS the hypotenuse, not a leg.
- **Mixing up which side is the hypotenuse on a ladder problem.** The ladder is always opposite the right angle (the corner where wall meets ground), so the ladder is always the hypotenuse, never a leg.
- **Confusing coordinates with distances.** $A(2, 1)$ does not mean side length $2$ and side length $1$. The distances are the **differences** between coordinates: $8 - 2 = 6$ horizontal, $9 - 1 = 8$ vertical.

## Quick summary

- Most Cambridge Pythagoras questions hide the right triangle. Your job is to spot it.
- Rectangle diagonals split a rectangle into two right triangles, with the diagonal as the hypotenuse.
- An isosceles or equilateral triangle gives a right triangle when you drop the perpendicular from apex to base. The altitude bisects the base.
- A square of side $a$ has diagonal $a\sqrt{2}$.
- Ladders, poles, and pylons against horizontal ground always form a right angle at the base.
- The distance between two coordinate points is just Pythagoras on $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$.
- A semicircle's inscribed angle is $90°$, so the diameter becomes a hypotenuse for free.
