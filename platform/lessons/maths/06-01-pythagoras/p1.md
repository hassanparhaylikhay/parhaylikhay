## Why this matters

Pythagoras' theorem is the most-used result in O-Level mathematics. It shows up in mensuration (slant heights, diagonals), coordinate geometry (distance formula), trigonometry (sine, cosine, tangent are built on right triangles), 3D problems (space diagonals), and even circle theorems (the angle in a semicircle is $90°$, which lets you use Pythagoras inside a circle).

If you do not see a right angle in a question, **build one**: drop a perpendicular, split an isosceles triangle in half, draw the diagonal of a rectangle. Once a right triangle appears, Pythagoras can act.

In this part you will see **why** it is true, not just the formula.

Form 1 is the theorem. Forms 2 and 3 are the rearrangements you will actually use in questions. The key skill is recognising which side is the hypotenuse before you write anything down.

## How to spot the hypotenuse

The hypotenuse is **always opposite the right angle**, and it is always the longest side. Three fast checks:

1. Find the small square (the right-angle marker). The side **across** from it is the hypotenuse.
2. The two sides **forming** the right angle are the legs.
3. The hypotenuse is never one of the sides next to the $90°$ corner.

In a Cambridge question the right angle is sometimes hidden, marked only by a small square in the diagram. Read the figure carefully before you label sides.

## Common Pythagorean triples

A few right triangles have whole-number sides. Knowing them speeds up paper work:

- $(3, 4, 5)$, the most common
- $(5, 12, 13)$
- $(8, 15, 17)$
- $(7, 24, 25)$

And any multiple: $(6, 8, 10)$, $(9, 12, 15)$, $(15, 36, 39)$ are all $(3, 4, 5)$ scaled.

If you see a triangle with two sides matching one of these patterns, the third side is likely the third number in the triple. **Always verify** with the formula. The shortcut just tells you what answer to expect.

## Worked example: identifying the parts

A right triangle has sides labelled as follows: the side opposite the right angle is $13$ cm, and one of the other sides is $5$ cm. Without using a formula, decide which side is the hypotenuse and write down the equation Pythagoras gives.

The side **opposite** the right angle is always the hypotenuse. So the hypotenuse is $13$ cm, the known leg is $5$ cm, and the unknown leg call it $a$.

Pythagoras gives:

$$a^{2} + 5^{2} = 13^{2}$$

We will solve this kind of equation in [p3](p3). For now the point is: you must identify the hypotenuse first, **before** you can write the equation correctly. If you swap them, the answer is wrong.

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- **Using Pythagoras on a non-right triangle.** It only works when there is a $90°$ angle. For other triangles you need the sine or cosine rule (see [Unit 6.3](../06-03-non-right-triangles/p1)).
- **Putting the wrong side as $c$.** If you place a leg in the $c$ slot, the equation is wrong. The hypotenuse is **always** opposite the right angle.
- **Forgetting to square root.** $a^{2} + b^{2}$ gives you $c^{2}$, not $c$. The final step is the square root.
- **Mixing units.** All three sides must be in the same unit before squaring. If one side is in metres and another in cm, convert first.

## Quick summary

| What | Statement |
|---|---|
| When | Right-angled triangle only |
| Theorem | $a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}$ where $c$ is opposite the right angle |
| Find $c$ | $c = \sqrt{a^{2} + b^{2}}$ |
| Find a leg | $a = \sqrt{c^{2} - b^{2}}$ |
| Common triples | $(3,4,5)$, $(5,12,13)$, $(8,15,17)$, $(7,24,25)$ |
