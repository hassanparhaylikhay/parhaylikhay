## Why this matters

In every Cambridge Sets question that gives you numbers, the trap is the same: students count people in the overlap *twice*. The fix is a single rule: **always fill the intersection first.** Once that number is in the middle, every other region is a quick subtraction.

## The strategy

For a 2-set Venn problem, follow this order:

1. **Read carefully.** Write down what you know: $n(\xi)$, $n(A)$, $n(B)$, $n(A \cap B)$, anything labelled "both", "all three", "neither".
2. **Fill the centre first.** Put $n(A \cap B)$ (the "both" number) into the overlap.
3. **Fill the "only" regions.** $n(A \text{ only}) = n(A) - n(A \cap B)$. Same for $B$.
4. **Fill "neither".** $n(\text{neither}) = n(\xi) - (\text{everything inside the circles})$.

Three sets: same idea, but you fill the centre $A \cap B \cap C$ first, then the three two-way overlaps, then the "only" regions, then "neither".

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: the "both" group is already inside $n(A)$.** When the problem says "$25$ study Maths", that includes the students who study Maths *and* Physics. To get Maths-only, you must subtract the overlap.

If you treat $n(A)$ and $n(A \cap B)$ as separate groups, you'll double-count those students.

</div>

## Worked example

In a class of $30$ students:

- $18$ study French
- $12$ study German
- $5$ study both French and German

How many students study **neither** French nor German?

**Step 1: List what you know.**

$$n(\xi) = 30, \quad n(F) = 18, \quad n(G) = 12, \quad n(F \cap G) = 5$$

**Step 2: Fill the intersection.** $5$ students study both, so put $5$ in the overlap.

$$n(\text{neither}) = 30 - (13 + 5 + 7) = 30 - 25 = 5$$

## Three-set word problems

Same idea, just more regions. Always start from the deepest overlap and work outwards.

**Order to fill in:**

1. $n(A \cap B \cap C)$, the centre.
2. The three two-way overlaps (each minus the centre, so you don't double-count).
3. The three "only" regions (each set total minus the relevant overlaps).
4. "Neither/none": total minus everything inside the circles.

The arithmetic gets longer, but the logic is identical to the 2-set case.

## Common mistakes

- **Double-counting the overlap.** Writing $n(F \cap G)$ inside the overlap *and* leaving the rest of $F$ at $18$. The $18$ already includes those 5; subtract first.
- Forgetting the "neither" region. Always check whether the inside-circles total equals $n(\xi)$. If not, the gap is $n(\text{neither})$.
- On 3-set diagrams, filling the two-way overlaps without subtracting the centre. If $n(A \cap B \cap C) = 4$ and $n(A \cap B) = 10$, the *just A and B (not C)* region is $10 - 4 = 6$.
- Reading "all three" as a 2-way overlap. "All three" means the centre region only.

## Quick summary

- Fill from the inside out: deepest overlap first, then "only" regions, then "neither".
- Always subtract the overlap from each set's total to get the "only" region.
- Check at the end: every region's count should sum to $n(\xi)$.
