## Why this matters

A bacterial colony starts at $\textcolor{#fff067}{x}$ cells. After $5$ generations of doubling, it's $\textcolor{#fff067}{x} \cdot 2^5 = 32\textcolor{#fff067}{x}$. After another $3$ generations: $32\textcolor{#fff067}{x} \cdot 2^3 = 256\textcolor{#fff067}{x}$. The shortcut $2^5 \cdot 2^3 = 2^{5+3} = 2^8$ saved you from doing eight doublings. The same shortcut works with letters: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{x}^5 \cdot \textcolor{#0fee89}{x}^3 = \textcolor{#0fee89}{x}^8$.

Cambridge tests **all four index rules** on Paper 1 and Paper 2 together. We covered the rules with pure numbers in 1.7 (Indices I); now we apply them to algebraic expressions with letters and coefficients. The rules don't change. Only the bookkeeping does.

## A reminder of the four rules

For any base $a$ and any indices $m, n$:

| Rule | Statement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Multiply | $\textcolor{#fff067}{a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}}$ | $x^4 \cdot x^3 = x^7$ |
| Divide | $\textcolor{#fff067}{\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}}$ | $\dfrac{x^5}{x^2} = x^3$ |
| Power | $\textcolor{#fff067}{(a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n}}$ | $(x^3)^2 = x^6$ |
| Negative | $\textcolor{#fff067}{a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}}$ | $x^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ |

Plus the special cases: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a^0 = 1}$ for any non-zero $a$, and $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}}$ for fractional indices.

These four (with the special cases) cover everything Cambridge throws at you in this sub-topic.

## Coefficients ride alongside

When the term has a coefficient (a number in front of the letter), the **coefficient and the letter are handled separately**.

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{3} x^4 \times \textcolor{#fff067}{2} x^3 \;=\; \underbrace{\textcolor{#fff067}{3 \times 2}}_{\text{coefficients multiply}} \cdot \underbrace{\textcolor{#0fee89}{x^4 \times x^3}}_{\text{add indices}} \;=\; 6x^7$$

Same for division:

$$\dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{8} x^5}{\textcolor{#fff067}{2} x^2} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{8}}{\textcolor{#fff067}{2}} \cdot \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{x^5}}{\textcolor{#0fee89}{x^2}} \;=\; 4x^{5 - 2} \;=\; 4x^3$$

The coefficient follows ordinary number arithmetic. The index follows the index rule.

## Power of a product: every factor gets the power

The power-of-a-power rule extends: when a **whole bracket** is raised to a power, every factor inside gets that power.

$$(\textcolor{#fff067}{a} \textcolor{#0fee89}{b})^n \;=\; \textcolor{#fff067}{a}^n \textcolor{#0fee89}{b}^n$$

So $(2x^3)^2 = \textcolor{#fff067}{2}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{#0fee89}{x^3})^2 = 4x^6$, and $(3xy)^2 = 9x^2 y^2$.

The two indices on the same letter **multiply**: $(x^3)^2 = x^{3 \times 2} = x^6$. Don't add them.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: $(2x)^2 \ne 2x^2$.** $(2x)^2$ means $(2x)(2x) = 4x^2$. Without the brackets, $2x^2$ means $2 \cdot x \cdot x = 2x^2$. Different answers. Brackets pull every factor inside under the power.

</div>

## Negative indices: move to the other side of the bar

A negative index means **reciprocal**. The base shifts from top to bottom of a fraction (or vice versa), and the index flips sign.

$$x^{-n} \;=\; \dfrac{1}{x^n} \qquad \dfrac{1}{x^{-n}} \;=\; x^n$$

Note: **only the base** with the negative index moves. A coefficient sitting in front stays put.

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{5} x^{-2} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{5}}{x^2}$$

That $5$ is a coefficient, not part of the base. It does not move.

If the question gives you $5^{-2}$, that's a different beast: the whole $5$ has the negative index, so it becomes $\tfrac{1}{5^2} = \tfrac{1}{25}$.

## Fractional indices: roots in disguise

A fractional index means a root.

$$x^{1/n} \;=\; \sqrt[n]{x} \qquad x^{m/n} \;=\; \sqrt[n]{x^m}$$

So $x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$ and $x^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{x^2}$.

When you multiply two terms with fractional indices on the same base, **add the fractions** as you would with whole indices:

$$x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/2} \;=\; x^{1/2 + 1/2} \;=\; x^1 \;=\; x$$

$$x^{2/3} \cdot x^{1/3} \;=\; x^{2/3 + 1/3} \;=\; x$$

The same applies for division (subtract) and power-of-a-power (multiply).

## Putting it together

**Example.** Simplify $\textcolor{#fff067}{6} x^5 \times \textcolor{#fff067}{4} x^{-2}$.

- Coefficients: $\textcolor{#fff067}{6 \times 4 = 24}$.
- Letters: $x^5 \times x^{-2} = x^{5 + (-2)} = x^3$.

Result: $24x^3$.

**Example.** Simplify $\dfrac{15 x^7}{5 x^{-2}}$.

- Coefficients: $\tfrac{15}{5} = 3$.
- Letters: $x^7 \div x^{-2} = x^{7 - (-2)} = x^9$.

Result: $3x^9$.

## Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{(2x^3)^2 \cdot 3x^{-1}}{4x^4}$.

**Step 1: Apply the power to the bracket.** $(2x^3)^2 = 2^2 \cdot x^{3 \times 2} = 4x^6$.

**Step 2: Multiply the top.**

$$4x^6 \cdot 3x^{-1} \;=\; 12 x^{6 + (-1)} \;=\; 12 x^5$$

**Step 3: Divide top by bottom.**

$$\dfrac{12 x^5}{4 x^4} \;=\; \dfrac{12}{4} \cdot x^{5 - 4} \;=\; 3x$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{3x}$. **[M1 for the power of the bracket; M1 for combining indices; A1 for the simplified form]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Adding the indices when they should multiply. $(x^3)^2 = x^{6}$, NOT $x^5$.
- Multiplying the indices when they should add. $x^4 \cdot x^3 = x^7$, NOT $x^{12}$.
- Moving the coefficient under the bar with a negative index. $5x^{-2} = \tfrac{5}{x^2}$, NOT $\tfrac{1}{5x^2}$.
- Forgetting the brackets distribute the power. $(2x)^2 = 4x^2$, NOT $2x^2$.
- Treating a fractional index as a coefficient. $x^{1/2}$ means $\sqrt{x}$, NOT $\tfrac{1}{2}x$.

## Quick summary

- **Multiply** same-base: $\textcolor{#fff067}{a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}}$.
- **Divide** same-base: $\textcolor{#fff067}{a^m / a^n = a^{m-n}}$.
- **Power of a power**: $\textcolor{#fff067}{(a^m)^n = a^{mn}}$.
- **Negative index**: $\textcolor{#fff067}{a^{-n} = 1/a^n}$.
- **Fractional index**: $\textcolor{#0fee89}{a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}}$.
- Coefficients and letters are handled **separately** in each rule.
