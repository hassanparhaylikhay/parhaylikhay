## Why this matters

So far in Unit 7 you have either been given a column directly, or asked to find a column from two coordinate points. From here on, the question stops handing you components and instead names two vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, then asks you to find every other vector in a figure in terms of those two.

This is the topic that distinguishes a $1$-mark vector student from a $5$-mark vector student. Every $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ question on your exam, including the longest one on the paper (usually worth $4$ to $6$ marks), comes from the same simple foundation: **end minus start, head to tail**.

You learn three habits in this part:

1. Spot a position vector when you see one.
2. Reverse an arrow by flipping its sign.
3. Combine two arrows head-to-tail to get a third.

That is enough to start solving real exam questions in p2 and p3.

## Position vectors: arrows from the origin

A **position vector** pins down where a point is, measured from the origin $O$. For a point $A$, its position vector is $\overrightarrow{OA}$. Often this is shortened to a single bold letter $\mathbf{a}$. So:

$$\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{a}, \quad \overrightarrow{OB} = \mathbf{b}, \quad \overrightarrow{OC} = \mathbf{c}, \quad \ldots$$

The bold letter and the arrow notation say exactly the same thing. You will see both inside the same question on a paper. Treat them as interchangeable.

A position vector is always drawn from $O$. A vector between two NON-origin points (like $\overrightarrow{AB}$) is called a **displacement vector**. The next section shows how to find one from the other.

## End minus start

The displacement vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$ goes from $A$ to $B$. To find it in terms of the position vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, use the same rule you used in 7.2 with coordinates: **end minus start**.

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{OB} - \overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$$

This is the single most important identity in the whole unit. Memorise it. Read it the same way you would read column vectors: $B$ is the end, $A$ is the start, so subtract $A$ from $B$.

## Why it works: head-to-tail

If "end minus start" feels like a rule from nowhere, here is the picture behind it. To go from $A$ to $B$, you can take a detour via $O$:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{AO} + \overrightarrow{OB}$$

Walking from $A$ to $O$ undoes the arrow $\overrightarrow{OA}$, so $\overrightarrow{AO} = -\mathbf{a}$. Walking from $O$ to $B$ is $\mathbf{b}$. Add them:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = -\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$$

The same answer. The head-to-tail route is the proof; the "end minus start" is the shortcut. Marker schemes accept either as the method mark.

## The reverse arrow

Reading the arrow the other way around, from $B$ back to $A$, just flips every sign:

$$\overrightarrow{BA} = -\overrightarrow{AB} = -(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$$

You can also derive it the long way: $\overrightarrow{BA} = \overrightarrow{BO} + \overrightarrow{OA} = -\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}$. Same answer. Always check that the head and tail labels match the direction of the arrow you actually want.

## Worked example: find AB from position vectors

$O$ is the origin. $A$ and $B$ are two points, with $\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{a}$ and $\overrightarrow{OB} = \mathbf{b}$. Find $\overrightarrow{AB}$ in terms of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

<iframe src="/widgets/vector-geometry-step-explorer.html?preset=p1-find-AB" style="width:100%;max-width:696px;height:700px;border:0;border-radius:10px;display:block;margin:18px auto" loading="lazy"></iframe>

**Step 1: Pick a route from $A$ to $B$.** The cleanest route is via the origin:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{AO} + \overrightarrow{OB}$$

**Step 2: Reverse the first arrow.** $\overrightarrow{AO}$ is the reverse of $\overrightarrow{OA}$, so $\overrightarrow{AO} = -\mathbf{a}$.

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = -\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$$

**Step 3: Tidy up.** It is conventional to write the positive term first.

**Answer:** $\overrightarrow{AB} = \boxed{\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}}$. **[B1 for $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$ or $-\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$]**

Full marks $= 1$.

That single mark looks small, but it is the foundation for everything in p2 and p3. Almost every question on those pages starts by writing one displacement vector as $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$ (or similar), so this one identity locks in the first mark on a longer question.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing $\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$.** Wrong order. End MINUS start, not start minus end. That gives $\overrightarrow{BA}$ instead.
- **Forgetting the minus when reversing an arrow.** $\overrightarrow{AO} = -\mathbf{a}$, not $\mathbf{a}$. The arrow flips, every sign flips.
- **Treating $\mathbf{a}$ as a scalar.** $\mathbf{a}$ is a bold letter for a reason: it is a vector. You can multiply it by a number, but you cannot square it, take its reciprocal, or compare it to a number with $<$ or $>$.
- **Dropping the arrow on the answer.** "$\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$" is correct. "$AB = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$" is sloppy and can cost the mark on a strict marker.

## Quick summary

- **Position vector** of $A$: $\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{a}$ (or just $\mathbf{a}$ in bold).
- **Displacement vector** between any two points: $\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$. End minus start.
- **Reverse an arrow:** $\overrightarrow{BA} = -\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$.
- **Head-to-tail** lets you route a vector via any in-between point. Useful when the direct route is harder to see.
- **One mark** on the exam: writing $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$ for $\overrightarrow{AB}$ when given $\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{a}$ and $\overrightarrow{OB} = \mathbf{b}$. Lock it in early in any longer question.
