## Why this matters

Cambridge does NOT accept a surd in the denominator of a final answer. **Rationalising** means rewriting the fraction so the denominator is a whole number, by multiplying top and bottom by the right thing. There are two cases: a single surd in the denominator, and a binomial like $a + \sqrt{b}$ (which uses the conjugate idea from p2).

## Why we rationalise

A fraction like $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ is mathematically fine but Cambridge marks it as **not in simplest form**. The convention is that final answers should have a **rational** (integer or fraction) denominator. So we rewrite $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ as $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$, an equivalent form with a clean denominator.

The idea: **multiply the top and bottom by the same thing**. This doesn't change the fraction's value (you're multiplying by $1$), but it can clear the surd from the bottom.

## Case 1: single surd in the denominator

When the denominator is $\sqrt{n}$ (or $a\sqrt{n}$), multiply top and bottom by **$\sqrt{n}$**.

$$\dfrac{\textcolor{#fff067}{1}}{\textcolor{#00abfa}{\sqrt{n}}} \;\times\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{\sqrt{n}}}{\textcolor{#0fee89}{\sqrt{n}}} \;=\; \dfrac{\textcolor{#0fee89}{\sqrt{n}}}{\textcolor{#00abfa}{n}}$$

The surd in the denominator cancels because $\sqrt{n} \times \sqrt{n} = n$ (a whole number).

## Case 2: binomial denominator (the conjugate)

When the denominator is something like $a + \sqrt{b}$ or $\sqrt{a} - b$, multiplying top and bottom by the same thing won't kill the surd. Instead, multiply by the **conjugate**: same expression with the **opposite sign in the middle**.

| Denominator | Conjugate | Product (clean) |
|---|---|---|
| $1 + \sqrt{2}$ | $1 - \sqrt{2}$ | $1 - 2 = -1$ |
| $\sqrt{5} - 2$ | $\sqrt{5} + 2$ | $5 - 4 = 1$ |
| $-1 + \sqrt{3}$ | $1 + \sqrt{3}$ | $3 - 1 = 2$ |
| $3 + 2\sqrt{7}$ | $3 - 2\sqrt{7}$ | $9 - 28 = -19$ |

The denominator turns into the **difference of two squares** $a^2 - b$, which has no surd.

**Example.** $\dfrac{1}{-1 + \sqrt{3}}$.

Multiply by conjugate $\dfrac{1 + \sqrt{3}}{1 + \sqrt{3}}$:

$$\dfrac{1 \times (1 + \sqrt{3})}{(-1 + \sqrt{3})(1 + \sqrt{3})} \;=\; \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{3}}{(\sqrt{3})^2 - 1^2} \;=\; \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{3}}{3 - 1} \;=\; \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{3}}{2}$$

The denominator collapsed from $-1 + \sqrt{3}$ to $2$. Done.

## Worked example

Rationalise $\dfrac{2}{1 + \sqrt{2}}$.

**Step 1: Identify the conjugate.** Flip the sign in front of the surd: $1 - \sqrt{2}$.

**Step 2: Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate.**

$$\dfrac{2}{1 + \sqrt{2}} \;\times\; \dfrac{1 - \sqrt{2}}{1 - \sqrt{2}} \;=\; \dfrac{2(1 - \sqrt{2})}{(1 + \sqrt{2})(1 - \sqrt{2})}$$

**Step 3: Compute the bottom.**

$$(1 + \sqrt{2})(1 - \sqrt{2}) \;=\; 1 - 2 \;=\; -1$$

**Step 4: Compute the top.**

$$2(1 - \sqrt{2}) \;=\; 2 - 2\sqrt{2}$$

**Step 5: Combine.**

$$\dfrac{2 - 2\sqrt{2}}{-1} \;=\; -(2 - 2\sqrt{2}) \;=\; 2\sqrt{2} - 2$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{2\sqrt{2} - 2}$ (or equivalently $2(\sqrt{2} - 1)$). **[M1 for the conjugate; M1 for the bottom expansion; A1 for the final form]**

Full marks $= 3$.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: don't multiply by the same expression as the denominator.** $(1 + \sqrt{2})(1 + \sqrt{2}) = 1 + 2\sqrt{2} + 2 = 3 + 2\sqrt{2}$, which still has a surd. The conjugate has the OPPOSITE sign on the surd term, which is what makes the difference of squares work.

</div>

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying by the same denominator (not the conjugate). The conjugate has the OPPOSITE middle sign.
- Forgetting to multiply the top too. Multiplying ONLY the bottom by $\sqrt{n}$ changes the value of the fraction.
- Sign mistakes when expanding the conjugate product. $(a - \sqrt{b})(a + \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - b$ has the surd term cancel because of the difference of squares; check the signs.
- Forgetting to simplify the final answer. $\dfrac{6\sqrt{3}}{6}$ should simplify to $\sqrt{3}$.

## Quick summary

- Rationalising clears surds from the denominator. Multiply top and bottom by the right thing.
- Single surd $\sqrt{n}$ in denominator: multiply by $\sqrt{n}$.
- Binomial $a + \sqrt{b}$: multiply by conjugate $a - \sqrt{b}$.
- Binomial $\sqrt{a} - b$: multiply by conjugate $\sqrt{a} + b$.
- The conjugate uses the difference of squares: $(a + \sqrt{b})(a - \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - b$, no surd left.
