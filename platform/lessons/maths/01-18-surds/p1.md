## Why this matters

Type $\sqrt{20}$ into a calculator and you get $4.47213595\ldots$, a decimal that goes on forever with no pattern. Cambridge prefers the exact form $2\sqrt{5}$: shorter to write, more accurate, and easier to combine with other surds later. Once you see the trick, those messy roots become tidy.

A **surd** is a square root (or other root) that doesn't simplify to a whole number. Cambridge expects you to write any irrational answer in **simplest surd form**, e.g. $2\sqrt{5}$ rather than $\sqrt{20}$ or $4.472\ldots$. Surds appear all over Pythagoras (6.1), trigonometry (6.2-6.3), and quadratic equations (2.5). Learning to simplify them now saves marks later.

## What a surd is

A **surd** is an irrational root: $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{5}$, $\sqrt[3]{7}$. The value can't be written as a fraction; the surd notation **is** the simplest exact form.

| Number | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| $\sqrt{4}$ | NOT a surd | $\sqrt{4} = 2$, exact |
| $\sqrt{2}$ | Surd | irrational, $\approx 1.414$ |
| $\sqrt{20}$ | Surd | simplifies to $2\sqrt{5}$ |
| $\sqrt[3]{27}$ | NOT a surd | $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$ |
| $\sqrt[3]{2}$ | Surd | irrational |

The key rule: **if the number under the root is a perfect square, it's not a surd**. Otherwise it is.

## The product rule for roots

The single rule that makes simplifying possible:

$$\sqrt{\textcolor{#fff067}{a} \times \textcolor{#00abfa}{b}} \;=\; \sqrt{\textcolor{#fff067}{a}} \times \sqrt{\textcolor{#00abfa}{b}}$$

So $\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4 \times 5} = \sqrt{4} \times \sqrt{5} = 2\sqrt{5}$.

The idea is to **split the number into a perfect-square factor and a leftover**. The perfect square comes out as a whole number; the leftover stays under the root.

## How to find the perfect-square factor

For each number, check whether it's divisible by the common perfect squares: $4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100$.

| Number | Largest perfect-square factor | Simplified |
|---|---|---|
| $20$ | $4$ | $2\sqrt{5}$ |
| $50$ | $25$ | $5\sqrt{2}$ |
| $72$ | $36$ | $6\sqrt{2}$ |
| $200$ | $100$ | $10\sqrt{2}$ |
| $48$ | $16$ | $4\sqrt{3}$ |
| $98$ | $49$ | $7\sqrt{2}$ |
| $147$ | $49$ | $7\sqrt{3}$ |

A few quick checks: if the number ends in two zeros, divide by $100$. If it's even and divisible by $4$, pull out the $4$. If it's a multiple of $9$ or $25$ or $49$, those usually work.

## Step-by-step alternative

If you can't spot the largest perfect-square factor, just **split off any square factor** and repeat.

**Example.** $\sqrt{200}$:

- $200 = 4 \times 50$, so $\sqrt{200} = 2\sqrt{50}$.
- $50 = 25 \times 2$, so $\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}$.
- Combine: $2 \times 5\sqrt{2} = 10\sqrt{2}$.

Same answer. Slower but works when the largest factor isn't obvious.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: $\sqrt{a + b} \neq \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$.** The product rule splits across multiplication, NOT addition. So $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, NOT $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7$. Always check whether the operation under the root is multiplication (splittable) or addition (NOT splittable).

## When a surd is "in simplest form"

A surd $a\sqrt{b}$ is in **simplest form** when $b$ has **no square factor** other than $1$. So:

- $2\sqrt{5}$ is simplest (since $5$ has no square factor).
- $2\sqrt{20}$ is NOT simplest (since $20 = 4 \times 5$).
- $\sqrt{15}$ is simplest ($15 = 3 \times 5$, both prime).

Cambridge mark schemes usually require simplest form for the accuracy mark.

## Worked example

Simplify $\sqrt{180}$.

**Step 1: Find the largest perfect-square factor of $180$.**

- Try $36$: $\tfrac{180}{36} = 5$. Yes! $180 = 36 \times 5$.

**Step 2: Apply the product rule.**

$$\sqrt{180} \;=\; \sqrt{36 \times 5} \;=\; \sqrt{36} \times \sqrt{5} \;=\; 6\sqrt{5}$$

**Answer:** $\boxed{6\sqrt{5}}$. **[M1 for the factorisation; A1 for the simplified form]**

Full marks $= 2$.

## Common mistakes

- Splitting $\sqrt{a+b}$. The rule only works for multiplication.
- Pulling out a non-perfect-square factor. $\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$ requires $4$ (a perfect square), NOT $5$ or $10$.
- Stopping too early. $2\sqrt{20}$ is not simplest; you can still pull out a $\sqrt{4}$: $2\sqrt{20} = 2 \times 2\sqrt{5} = 4\sqrt{5}$.
- Treating $\sqrt{0}$ or $\sqrt{1}$ as surds. They're not; they equal $0$ and $1$ exactly.

## Quick summary

- A surd is a root that doesn't simplify to a whole number.
- Product rule: $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}$. Use this to pull out perfect-square factors.
- Simplest form: $a\sqrt{b}$ where $b$ has no square factor other than $1$.
- Always check: does $b$ have a perfect-square factor? If yes, you can simplify further.
