## Why this matters

Open up any cricket scoreboard and you'll see comparisons everywhere: Babar's average is greater than Rizwan's, this innings has at least 50 runs, that bowler conceded fewer than 30. Maths writes those same comparisons with six short symbols, and Cambridge expects you to read and write them without slipping.

Cambridge uses six symbols to compare numbers. They appear in inequality questions, set definitions, exam-style word problems, and graph regions. The symbols are simple, but the **direction** trips students up under pressure. This part nails the notation. Part 2 puts it to use.

The first two are about **identity**. $=$ says two things are the same value. $\textcolor{#ff4670}{\ne}$ says they are not.

The other four are about **size**. They split into two pairs:

- $\textcolor{#fff067}{\gt}$ and $\textcolor{#00abfa}{\lt}$ are **strict**: the values are different.
- $\textcolor{#ff822c}{\ge}$ and $\textcolor{#0fee89}{\le}$ are **inclusive** (the bar means "or equal to").

## How to read the direction

The classic memory hook: **the symbol opens toward the larger value**, like a hungry mouth eating the bigger number.

$$\textcolor{#fff067}{5 \gt 3} \qquad \textcolor{#00abfa}{3 \lt 5}$$

Both say the same thing: 5 is the bigger one. The mouth opens toward the 5.

A second check: read left to right.

- $5 \gt 3$ reads as "5 is greater than 3". ✓
- $3 \lt 5$ reads as "3 is less than 5". ✓

If the sentence makes sense, the symbol points the right way.

<div class="trap-callout">

**Trap: confusing $\lt$ and $\gt$.** They look like mirror images of each other and panic students. Two reliable checks:

- The mouth opens toward the **bigger** value.
- The pointy end touches the **smaller** value.

Use whichever rule sticks.

</div>

## Worked example

Fill in each blank with one of $=$, $\lt$, $\gt$, $\le$, $\ge$, $\ne$ to make the statement true. (More than one symbol may work; pick the *most precise* one.)

(a) $\;7 \;\square\; 7$

(b) $\;-3 \;\square\; -1$

(c) $\;\tfrac{1}{2} \;\square\; 0.5$

**(a)** $7 = 7$. Both sides are exactly the same value. While $7 \le 7$ and $7 \ge 7$ are also true, $=$ is the most precise.

**Answer:** $7 \;\boxed{=}\; 7$. **[B1]**

**(b)** On a number line, $-3$ sits to the left of $-1$. So $-3$ is the smaller value.

**Answer:** $-3 \;\boxed{\lt}\; -1$. **[B1]**

**(c)** $\tfrac{1}{2} = 0.5$. Same value, different notation.

**Answer:** $\tfrac{1}{2} \;\boxed{=}\; 0.5$. **[B1]**

Full marks $= 3$.

## Common mistakes

- Reading $-3 \gt -1$ as true because "3 is bigger than 1". On the number line, $-3$ is to the LEFT of $-1$. Negative numbers flip your intuition.
- Writing $5 \le 5$ as wrong. It's actually true: 5 is "less than OR equal to" 5, and the equal-to half is satisfied.
- Forgetting to slash the equals sign for "not equal to". $\ne$ has a clear slash; $=$ does not.
- Treating $\gt$ and $\ge$ as interchangeable. The bar matters: $\ge$ allows equality, $\gt$ does not.

## Quick summary

- Six symbols: $\textcolor{#7a7875}{=}$, $\textcolor{#ff4670}{\ne}$, $\textcolor{#fff067}{\gt}$, $\textcolor{#00abfa}{\lt}$, $\textcolor{#ff822c}{\ge}$, $\textcolor{#0fee89}{\le}$.
- The mouth always opens toward the larger value.
- The bar under $\ge$ / $\le$ adds "or equal to".
- On the number line, smaller = further left (matters for negatives).
